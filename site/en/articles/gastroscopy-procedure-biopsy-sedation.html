How gastroscopy is performed: a patient guide to sedation, biopsy, and recovery

What is gastroscopy and why is it recommended?

Gastroscopy, clinically termed upper gastrointestinal endoscopy or esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), is a precise and widely utilized diagnostic procedure designed to evaluate the internal mucosal lining of the upper digestive tract [1, 2, 3]. The examination employs a slender, flexible tube equipped at its tip with a miniature high-definition video camera and an advanced lighting system, which is guided gently through the mouth into the oesophagus, throughout the stomach cavity, and into the duodenum, the initial segment of the small intestine [1, 2, 3]. This provides the examining clinician with real-time, magnified optical visualization of the mucosal surface, offering diagnostic accuracy far superior to transabdominal ultrasound or routine barium radiographs when identifying subtle mucosal inflammation, superficial erosions, small peptic ulcers, or early structural changes [1, 3, 6, 7].

A gastroenterologist typically requests an upper endoscopy to resolve a specific clinical question that clinical history and non-invasive testing cannot conclusively answer [1, 3, 4]. Frequent clinical indications include evaluating chronic or recurrent dyspepsia unresponsive to standard medical therapy, persistent heartburn associated with refractory gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, unexplained epigastric discomfort, and protracted nausea or vomiting [1, 2, 4]. The procedure is also instrumental in investigating unexplained iron-deficiency anaemia to detect occult upper blood loss or small-intestinal malabsorption syndromes such as celiac disease, as well as evaluating difficulty or pain when swallowing (dysphagia or odynophagia) [1, 4, 5, 9]. These diagnostic objectives are entirely separate from lower bowel endoscopy, as upper endoscopy and colonoscopy inspect anatomically distinct territories [1, 3].

International clinical guidelines, including guidance from the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE CG184 and NG12), underscore that gastroscopy is not an indiscriminate screening tool for fleeting heartburn, but a targeted investigation indicated when organic pathology must be ruled out or when alarm features emerge, such as unintended weight loss, repeated vomiting, or signs of bleeding [4, 5, 13]. Beyond diagnostic inspection, the endoscope serves as an operative conduit, permitting the specialist to obtain painless pinpoint mucosal biopsies for microscopic evaluation, perform rapid microbiological tests, or provide therapeutic interventions such as haemostasis for bleeding lesions or balloon dilatation of strictures [1, 3, 7, 13].

Preparation before gastroscopy: fasting and medication management

Thorough pre-procedure preparation is vital to ensure both procedural safety and clear mucosal visibility during gastroscopy [1, 2, 3]. The cardinal physiological requirement is that the stomach and duodenum must be completely empty of solid food and opaque liquids when the examination begins [1, 2]. Retained gastric contents obscure the mucosal surface, preventing the examining clinician from identifying subtle lesions, erosions, or small ulcers, which often necessitates terminating the procedure prematurely and rescheduling for another date [1, 3]. More importantly from a patient safety standpoint, retained fluid or food in the stomach during endoscopic intubation or sedation substantially increases the risk of pulmonary aspiration, a hazard that is reliably eliminated by adhering strictly to standardized fasting protocols [1, 2, 3].

Standard clinical preparation mandates an absolute cessation of all solid food for six to eight hours prior to the scheduled endoscopy appointment [1, 2, 3, 12]. Clear fluids—such as plain water or weak black tea without milk—may typically be consumed in modest sips up to two to four hours before the test, depending on the specific endoscopy unit's institutional protocol [1, 2, 12]. Milk, dairy products, opaque juices, and carbonated beverages must be avoided entirely on the day of the procedure, and patients are advised to refrain from smoking and chewing gum, both of which stimulate gastric acid secretion and excess secretions within the stomach [1, 2, 3].

Regarding chronic pharmacotherapy, patients must inform the physician and nursing team of every prescription medication, over-the-counter drug, and supplement they take well in advance [1, 2, 3]. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications (such as warfarin, clopidogrel, aspirin, or direct oral anticoagulants) require individualized clinician guidance regarding temporary cessation or bridging, particularly when mucosal biopsies or therapeutic interventions are anticipated, and patients should never discontinue these independently [1, 3]. Essential cardiovascular and antihypertensive medications are typically taken at their usual morning time with a minimal sip of water to maintain hemodynamic stability, while diabetes therapies and insulin regimens are deliberately adjusted to accommodate the fasting state and avoid hypoglycemia [1, 2, 3]. Furthermore, if biopsies for Helicobacter pylori detection are planned, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) may need to be withheld for a specified interval as directed by the clinician to prevent false-negative diagnostic results [10, 11].

How gastroscopy is performed: a step-by-step procedural walkthrough

Many patients seeking clear procedure-day education ask how gastroscopy is performed step by step inside the endoscopy suite [1, 2, 3]. Once pre-procedural checks and baseline vital sign recordings (blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation) are complete, the patient is positioned comfortably on their left side on the examination bed, an optimal anatomical posture that aligns the digestive tract and facilitates natural drainage of oral secretions [1, 2, 3]. Any removable dentures, dental appliances, or eyeglasses are safely removed beforehand to safeguard them and ensure unhindered access to the oral cavity [1, 2, 12].

A small, contoured plastic mouthguard is placed gently between the patient's teeth and secured with a comfortable elastic strap behind the head, protecting the patient's teeth and preventing involuntary biting down on the delicate endoscope shaft [1, 2, 3]. The clinical team then administers local anaesthetic spray to the pharynx, or initiates intravenous conscious sedation via an indwelling cannula depending on the agreed management plan [1, 2, 3]. The endoscopist lubricates the tip of the slender, flexible endoscope and introduces it over the tongue toward the posterior pharynx, gently encouraging the patient to swallow, which opens the upper oesophageal sphincter and allows the instrument to glide smoothly into the oesophagus without trauma [1, 2, 3].

Throughout the entire procedure, normal respiration is completely unobstructed: the endoscope travels down the food pipe (oesophagus), leaving the larynx, trachea, and airway entirely free, allowing the patient to breathe steadily through the nose and mouth [1, 2, 3, 12]. To inspect the mucosal architecture thoroughly, the clinician gently introduces controlled pulses of air or carbon dioxide through a dedicated internal channel, distending the stomach and duodenal folds to expose the entire mucosal surface for comprehensive high-definition inspection [1, 2, 3]. The specialist systematically evaluates the oesophageal lining for reflux changes, mucosal breaks, or varices, inspects the gastric fundus, body, and antrum, and advances through the pyloric channel into the first and second portions of the duodenum [1, 3, 6, 7]. A standard diagnostic upper endoscopy typically takes between five and fifteen minutes of active scope time; upon completion, residual insufflated air is aspirated back through the scope to minimize post-procedure fullness before the instrument is withdrawn gently and swiftly [1, 2, 3].

Is gastroscopy painful? Sedation and local anaesthesia options

The most frequent concern raised by patients is whether gastroscopy is painful and whether general anaesthesia is mandatory [1, 2, 3]. The clinical answer is reassuring: gastroscopy does not cause sharp somatic pain because the internal visceral mucosa lacks the somatic cutting-pain receptors present on external skin [1, 2, 3]. What an unsedated patient experiences is not acute pain, but rather physical discomfort, mild pressure in the throat and epigastrium, and a temporary, involuntary gagging sensation (gag reflex) as the tip of the instrument negotiates the pharynx, followed by a sensation of abdominal fullness related to diagnostic air insufflation [1, 2, 3, 12].

Two principal approaches exist to ensure patient comfort, selected collaboratively by the clinician and patient based on health status and preferences [1, 2, 3]. The first option is a local anaesthetic throat spray, typically lidocaine, administered to the back of the tongue and pharyngeal walls [1, 2, 3]. Within sixty seconds, the spray numbs the throat lining, significantly blunting the gag reflex and facilitating smooth intubation [1, 2, 3]. Under local anaesthesia alone, the patient remains fully awake, alert, and capable of following simple instructions; the substantial advantage is rapid recovery, enabling the individual to leave the endoscopy facility promptly without requiring an escort or taking time off from routine activities [1, 2, 12].

The second modality is intravenous conscious sedation, in which short-acting sedative and analgesic agents are administered via a peripheral cannula to induce deep relaxation, drowsiness, and anxiolysis [1, 2, 3]. While sedated, patients remain capable of breathing independently and responding to gentle verbal commands, yet they experience minimal awareness and typically develop complete retrograde amnesia for the procedure [1, 2, 3]. In specific clinical situations, deep propofol-based sedation administered by an anaesthetist provides comprehensive unconsciousness during the brief test [1, 3]. Importantly, clinical practice varies by facility, clinical indication, and patient preference: not every gastroscopy requires intravenous sedation, nor is every patient an ideal candidate for sedative medications, and the optimal comfort protocol is tailored individually [1, 2, 3].

Gastric biopsy: why tissue samples are taken and what they mean

Patients frequently experience substantial anxiety upon learning that a tissue biopsy was taken during their gastroscopy, often leaping to the distressing conclusion that the endoscopist suspects malignancy or severe disease [1, 3, 12]. Clinical reality stands in sharp contrast to this misconception: taking mucosal biopsies is a standard, routine, and integral part of everyday diagnostic endoscopy, executed in the vast majority of cases to investigate entirely benign, treatable gastrointestinal conditions that cannot be characterized by gross optical inspection alone [1, 3, 6, 12]. Using miniature biopsy forceps passed through an internal instrumentation channel within the endoscope, the specialist gently samples pinpoint fragments of mucosal tissue—measuring merely one to two millimetres—without eliciting any pain or physical sensation [1, 2, 3].

There is an extensive array of benign diagnostic rationales for taking routine biopsies during upper endoscopy [1, 3, 6]. Foremost among these is testing for Helicobacter pylori infection, which can be evaluated immediately in the endoscopy suite via a rapid urease test or processed histopathologically to guide targeted antibiotic eradication regimens [1, 6, 11]. Biopsies are equally essential to characterize the presence, depth, and chronological activity of chronic gastritis and gastropathy, identifying mucosal atrophy or reactive chemical changes [6, 11]. Furthermore, obtaining systematic biopsies from the duodenal bulb and descending duodenum represents the definitive diagnostic standard for celiac disease, confirming characteristic villous blunting and intraepithelial lymphocytosis even when endoscopic gross appearances appear entirely unremarkable [9, 10].

Mucosal sampling is also routine in patients with chronic reflux disease to screen for and monitor Barrett's oesophagus, as well as to confirm the benign histology of gastric ulcers before initiating acid-suppressive healing therapy [4, 7, 8]. Patients should be reassured that sampling tissue is a disciplined diagnostic technique designed to establish objective histological certainty, eliminating guesswork in clinical decision-making [1, 3, 6]. Biopsy specimens are preserved and forwarded to an accredited histopathology laboratory, with definitive microscopic reports typically available within several days to guide subsequent clinical follow-up [1, 3, 12].

Recovery and aftercare: returning to eating and post-procedure rest

Following completion of the endoscopic examination, the patient is transferred to the designated recovery area for structured clinical observation, typically remaining under nursing monitoring for thirty to sixty minutes until vital signs stabilize and sedative effects diminish [1, 2, 3]. Individuals who received only local anaesthetic pharyngeal spray are alert and able to sit up immediately, whereas those administered intravenous sedation require a monitored recovery interval to achieve full mental clarity and motor stability before ambulating [1, 2, 12].

Resuming oral intake is a primary concern for patients following endoscopy, and the key physiological determinant is the complete resolution of the local pharyngeal anaesthetic [1, 2, 3]. Throat numbness generally persists for forty-five to sixty minutes after administration; throughout this interval, all eating and drinking are strictly withheld to eliminate the risk of choking or pulmonary aspiration due to temporary suppression of protective swallowing reflexes [1, 2, 12]. Once normal sensation returns and the patient feels their throat is clear, a cautious test sip of plain water is taken to confirm smooth swallowing function [1, 2, 3]. Subsequently, a light, easily digestible meal—such as warm broth, yogurt, or soft bread—may be enjoyed, while heavy, spicy, or greasy foods and carbonated drinks should be avoided for the remainder of the day to maintain gastric comfort [1, 2, 3].

It is entirely normal to experience modest abdominal bloating, cramping, or an urge to belch during the initial hours following the procedure, reflecting transient gas retention from diagnostic insufflation, which rapidly resolves through normal flatus and gentle walking [1, 2, 3]. Mild throat irritation or dryness, resembling the early stages of a common cold, is also frequent and characteristically dissipates within twenty-four to forty-eight hours [1, 2, 12]. Crucially, when intravenous sedation has been administered, strict safety directives must be observed: an adult companion must accompany the patient home, and the patient must refrain from operating motor vehicles, handling complex machinery, or executing binding legal or financial documents for a full twenty-four hours until cognitive faculties normalize completely [1, 2, 3, 12].

Post-procedure warning signs and follow-up with the medical team at La Rose

Gastroscopy is recognized internationally as one of the safest and most reliable diagnostic procedures in gastroenterology, with major adverse events occurring exceptionally rarely when performed by experienced endoscopists [1, 2, 3]. Nonetheless, patient safety requires clear education regarding clinical red flags that necessitate immediate contact with the endoscopy unit or prompt presentation to an emergency department [1, 2, 3, 12]. It is clinically essential to differentiate between benign, self-limiting sensations—such as mild throat soreness or passing gas—and acute complications requiring swift intervention [1, 2, 12].

Alarm features that demand urgent medical evaluation include severe, unrelenting, or worsening chest, neck, or abdominal pain that does not respond to routine measures, or progressive difficulty breathing or swallowing [1, 2, 3, 12]. Urgent assessment is equally mandatory for persistent fevers, rigors, or intractable vomiting that prevents adequate oral hydration [1, 2, 12]. Furthermore, signs suggestive of upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage—such as frank bloody emesis, coffee-ground vomiting, pitch-black tarry stools (melena), or marked presyncope and postural dizziness—warrant immediate emergency medical care [1, 2, 8, 12, 13]. Should any of these symptoms manifest following an endoscopy, the patient must seek immediate emergency medical care without delay [1, 2, 3].

At La Rose Clinics, the medical team at La Rose provides structured, comprehensive clinical evaluation for individuals experiencing chronic upper gastrointestinal symptoms or requiring endoscopic investigations. The care pathway opens with a thorough clinical consultation, physical examination, and selective non-invasive diagnostics—such as in-clinic abdominal ultrasound or targeted laboratory testing—to determine whether upper endoscopy is genuinely warranted. When gastroscopy is indicated, the medical team ensures strict procedural safety and tailored patient comfort, followed by transparent, empathetic explanation of endoscopic findings and histological biopsy results without confusing medical jargon or unrealistic promises. When you experience persistent upper digestive discomfort and require structured clinical clarity, you can book a consultation with the medical team to initiate your comprehensive evaluation.

Frequently asked questions

Gastroscopy is performed with the patient resting comfortably on their left side as the slender endoscope is guided through the mouth into the oesophagus and stomach, and it does not cause suffocation because it travels through the alimentary tract, leaving the airway and trachea entirely unobstructed [1, 2, 3]. The patient breathes steadily and naturally through the nose and mouth throughout the examination, while the mouthguard and local throat spray ensure comfortable passage without impeding ventilation [1, 2, 3].

Gastroscopy is not sharply painful because the inner gastrointestinal lining lacks somatic pain receptors, causing instead temporary discomfort, mild gagging sensations, and gaseous fullness [1, 2, 3]. General anaesthesia is not routinely required; the procedure is successfully performed either with local anaesthetic throat spray alone with the patient alert, or with intravenous conscious sedation inducing restful relaxation, with the choice guided by patient health and individual preference [1, 2, 3, 12].

No, taking a mucosal biopsy during gastroscopy is a standard, routine diagnostic procedure and does not indicate cancer or a grave condition [1, 3, 12]. Tissue samples are obtained in the overwhelming majority of cases for benign purposes, such as identifying Helicobacter pylori infection, grading chronic gastritis, or ruling out celiac disease in the duodenum, with pathology results returning within several days to guide targeted medical care [1, 3, 6, 9, 11, 12].

You can begin drinking and eating once the local throat anaesthetic spray has completely worn off, which generally takes approximately forty-five to sixty minutes after the procedure [1, 2, 3]. Once normal swallowing sensation returns, you should take a small test sip of water; once swallowed comfortably, you can proceed to light, soft foods like broth or yogurt while avoiding heavy, spicy, or greasy meals for the rest of the day [1, 2, 12].

The active procedural time for a diagnostic gastroscopy typically spans just five to fifteen minutes, which provides sufficient time to inspect the oesophagus, stomach, and duodenum thoroughly and obtain any needed biopsies [1, 2, 3]. However, the total duration of the clinic visit ranges between one and two hours to accommodate pre-procedure preparation, baseline checks, and post-procedure recovery monitoring [1, 2, 3].

Local throat spray numbs only the pharyngeal mucosa to reduce the gag reflex while keeping the patient fully awake, allowing for immediate independent discharge [1, 2, 3]. Conversely, intravenous conscious sedation induces deep relaxation and temporary amnesia, eliminating procedure-related anxiety, but mandates an adult escort to accompany the patient home and strict avoidance of driving for twenty-four hours [1, 2, 3, 12].
