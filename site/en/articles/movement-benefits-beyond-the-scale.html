Scientific update: movement has health benefits even when the scale barely changes

Published: 23 August 2026 7 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

  2. Fitness matters even at the same weight

Movement is not punishment for eating

Many people judge a walk or a workout by one question: did the scale go down? If it did not, the movement feels ‘pointless’. The World Health Organization summarises established benefits of physical activity in adults, including lower risk of early death, heart disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes and some cancers, along with better mental health, sleep and thinking [1]. The NHS says regular activity can lower the risk of early death by up to 30% [3].

None of these show up directly on the scale. Weight is affected by fluid, digestion and the time of measurement, while fitness, strength and the ability to walk or climb stairs improve on a completely different track [1, 4]. Movement is therefore not ‘payment’ for a meal or a punishment for eating more. It is a treatment in its own right that serves the heart, the muscles and mood, even when the number on the scale stays still. If you want to understand why the scale alone misleads, read the article on body composition not the scale.

Fitness matters even at the same weight

A systematic review and meta-analysis published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in 2025 compared people by weight and cardiorespiratory fitness together. People with overweight or obesity who were fit did not have a statistically different risk of death from fit people of normal weight. Unfit people, in every weight group, had roughly two to three times the risk [6].

The researchers concluded that fitness is a strong predictor of death from heart disease and from all causes, and that it lessens the risk linked to overweight and obesity [6]. The study is observational, so it shows an association rather than proven cause, but it supports the idea that a successful weight plan should track fitness alongside the number [1, 6].

For the liver, a meta-analysis of trials in people with metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease found that exercise improved liver enzymes, fibrosis markers and cholesterol compared with standard therapy, while the difference in weight between the groups was not statistically significant [11]. The liver can benefit before the scale says anything clear.

How much movement is enough?

Guidelines agree on clear numbers for adults. The NHS recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, spread over 4 to 5 days or every day, with strengthening activities for all the major muscle groups on at least 2 days a week, and less time spent sitting for long periods [2]. The World Health Organization says some activity is better than none and that all movement counts [1].

If step counts are easier for you, a meta-analysis of 15 studies found that more daily steps were linked with progressively lower risk of death, with the benefit levelling off at roughly 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day for people aged 60 and over, and 8,000 to 10,000 steps for younger adults [9]. The biggest difference appears in people who start from a low step count [9].

So the plan does not have to begin at the final number. NIDDK suggests, for example, starting with 5-minute walks several times a day, gradually building to 10 minutes 3 times a day, and then increasing the pace [4].

Keep a backup activity for days when the weather is bad or you have a minor injury, such as walking indoors or body-weight exercises, so the plan does not stop the moment something changes [4]. Cut down long sitting too: get up and move for a few minutes regularly if you work at a desk, because the NHS treats reducing sitting time as part of the guidelines themselves, not an extra [2].

Strength exercise protects muscle

Strength exercise is not only for athletes. A systematic review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that muscle-strengthening activities were associated with lower risk of death from all causes, heart disease, cancer and diabetes. The largest reduction in risk, about 10 to 20%, was seen at around 30 to 60 minutes a week, and combining strengthening with aerobic activity was linked with lower risk than either alone [7].

The NHS explains that strengthening does not require a gym: carrying heavy shopping bags, yoga, pilates, body-weight exercises such as push-ups, resistance bands and heavy gardening all count [2]. Each exercise should be done until you need a short rest before repeating it [2].

This matters especially during weight loss, because part of the weight lost may be muscle. The article on protecting muscle while losing weight explains how protein and resistance exercise work together [2, 7].

If strength exercise is new to you, start with light weights or just your body weight, and focus on correct form before adding repetitions or load. NIDDK advises increasing muscle-strengthening activity gradually, and a rest day between sessions for the same muscles gives time to recover [4]. Above all, find a type of exercise you enjoy and can keep up for weeks and months, not just one week [4, 5].

Glucose after meals and mood: gains that come quickly

Some benefits of movement appear the same day. A meta-analysis of trials comparing prolonged sitting with frequent short breaks of standing or light walking found that these breaks lowered blood glucose after meals, that light walking was better than standing alone, and that it also reduced insulin [8]. These trials lasted a single day, so they show an immediate effect rather than a long-term result [8]. If this matters to you, read the tip on walking after your hardest meal.

For mood, a systematic review in the BMJ in 2024 covering 218 studies found that exercise is an effective treatment for depression, with walking or jogging, yoga and strength training among the most helpful types, although confidence in the evidence was low [10]. This does not make movement a substitute for psychological or medical treatment when those are needed, but it is a real addition [10].

Sleep is also among the benefits the World Health Organization lists for adults [1]. If your weight is stuck despite effort, the article on sleep and a weight plateau explains the link.

Start safely with movement you can repeat

Walking, cycling, housework and movement throughout the day all count as activity; structured exercise matters but is not the only form [1, 5]. Start at a level that suits your fitness and joints, and build gradually so you can reach more intense activity without injury [4]. The NHS advises speaking to a doctor first if you have not exercised for some time, or if you have a long-term condition or health concerns [2].

Some signs mean you should stop at once. The NHS says to get emergency help immediately for chest pain or pressure that does not go away, pain that spreads to the arm, neck, jaw or back, or chest pain with sweating, nausea, light-headedness or breathlessness [12]. Chest pain that comes and goes also needs medical assessment [12].

Alongside weight, record other things: minutes or steps, energy, sleep, and how easily you manage stairs. These numbers show progress when the scale is silent [1, 9].

Leave with a movement plan that suits you

A good plan starts from where you are now: your current activity, your joints, any long-term conditions, the medicines you take and the time you really have each week [2, 4]. It then sets a small goal you can achieve, such as a short walk after meals or two strength sessions, and builds gradually [4, 7].

At a weight-management visit, body composition and muscle can be measured so progress is seen through more than one number, and the eating plan can be matched to the movement plan [4, 6]. If you need an individual assessment, you can book an appointment at the clinic. This is a practical next step, not a promised result, and the treating team will decide what suits your situation after assessment [2].
