Type 2 diabetes remission is not a permanent cure

Published: 20 August 2026 8 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Why remission is the more accurate term

An international consensus involving diabetes and endocrine organisations selected 'remission' for glucose results that remain below the diagnostic threshold for a sufficient period without glucose-lowering medicine. The word 'cure' was avoided because the tendency towards raised glucose may remain and improvement is not guaranteed to last for life. [1]

The terminology does not diminish the achievement. Better glucose after lifestyle change, weight treatment or bariatric surgery can be clinically meaningful, but it is not permission to cancel follow-up or erase the medical history. [1, 2]

The definition requires a specific length of time without glucose-lowering medicine, not just one good day or week. A clinician determines when these conditions have been met based on repeated testing, not on how you feel generally or on one result taken at an unsuitable time. [1]

Type 2 diabetes remission has a medical definition; it is not one good home reading. Expert consensus usually uses an HbA1c below the diabetes diagnostic threshold, sustained for at least 3 months after glucose-lowering medication has stopped. A clinician documents it because anaemia and some blood or kidney conditions can make HbA1c less reliable and may require another measure [1, 8].

How remission actually happens

The most studied route is substantial, sustained weight loss. The UK DiRECT trial, which used a low-calorie total diet replacement followed by gradual food reintroduction with weight-maintenance support, achieved remission in about 46% of participants at one year and 36% at two years. Those who maintained more than 10 kg of weight loss had a much higher remission rate. [3]

Bariatric surgery is another recognised route, particularly for people with diabetes and significant obesity. Recent reviews show surgery is associated with a markedly higher likelihood of remission compared with medication or lifestyle change alone, though the exact rate varies with the type of operation, length of follow-up and each person's situation. Choosing between these paths is an individual clinical decision, not a general prescription. The care team reviews your weight, diabetes duration, coexisting conditions and current medicines before recommending one path over another. [4]

Relapse after remission is common, not a rare exception

Long-term 5-year follow-up of the DiRECT trial showed that of those in remission at two years, only about 26% remained in remission at five years, even with continued dietary support. This is an important number: it shows that remission is a state requiring active maintenance, not a fixed endpoint. [3]

This is not meant to discourage people who achieve remission, but to explain why regular follow-up, weight and ongoing dietary habits matter even after an excellent result. A return of raised glucose is not a personal failure or proof that the effort was pointless; it is an expected part of the condition's nature that deserves a clear follow-up plan from the start. [1, 3]

The same study showed that those who continued regular dietary support after the first two years had a clearly higher rate of staying in remission than those whose support was withdrawn. In other words, sustained follow-up and support, not just the initial achievement, is what determines the long-term outcome. [3]

Who is more likely to achieve remission?

Evidence shows the chance of remission is higher when the duration of diabetes is shorter, weight loss is larger and more sustained, and the person does not need insulin before starting. This does not mean someone with longstanding diabetes cannot benefit from improved weight and glucose, but the likelihood of meeting the specific definition of 'remission' decreases with longer disease duration. [1, 3]

Other factors such as age, starting weight and adherence to the dietary or medical plan all play a role. A clinician can give a realistic assessment of the likelihood of remission in your specific situation, rather than relying on someone else's story seen in a video or post. [1, 3]

This is also why early detection of prediabetes or early-stage diabetes matters. The earlier changes in glucose are found, the better the chance of responding to lifestyle change before the condition progresses or needs more treatment. If you have a family history of diabetes or other risk factors, regular screening gives you a better chance of addressing it at an earlier stage. [1]

Monitoring continues

The consensus advises regular HbA1c measurement alongside continued eye, kidney, foot, blood-pressure and weight checks according to the clinical plan. Glucose can rise again, and effects of earlier hyperglycaemia may remain relevant even when current results are excellent. [1, 5]

Do not stop diabetes treatment because of a good reading or weight loss without clinical agreement. If remission is confirmed, ask when the next blood test and complication checks are due and which changes need earlier review. Success here is a state to maintain with follow-up, not a final discharge certificate. [1, 5]

Even with excellent readings, the effect of earlier years of diabetes does not vanish immediately. The remission consensus says eye, kidney, foot, blood-pressure and weight checks should continue, with HbA1c measured at least yearly to confirm remission. Regular follow-up does not mean remission failed; it is part of safe remission care [1, 9, 10].

Rapid weight-loss programmes need medical supervision, not a solo attempt

The trials that achieved high remission rates, such as DiRECT, used a medically supervised very-low-calorie diet replacement programme with structured support for reintroducing food and maintaining weight, not simply a strict diet someone starts alone without follow-up. This distinction matters because rapid unsupervised weight loss can cause fluid and electrolyte problems, particularly for someone taking diabetes or blood-pressure medicines. [3]

If you want to try a similar approach, discuss it with your doctor first rather than starting a programme found online without an assessment of your situation. A doctor can adjust medicine doses alongside weight loss and watch for signs that need earlier action. [1, 3]

If remission does not happen, that is not failure

Not everyone with type 2 diabetes will reach the specific definition of remission, and that is normal. Improvements in glucose, blood pressure, lipids and reduced medication doses are all real benefits even without meeting the precise remission definition. The more important goal is reducing long-term complication risk, rather than chasing a particular number or label. [1, 2]

Do not compare your journey to someone else's story seen online. Genetic factors, disease duration, coexisting conditions and the ability to sustain large weight loss all differ between people, and the right plan should be built on your situation, not a generic story. Discuss with your care team what a realistic goal looks like for you, and how to measure success in a useful way. [1, 2]

The pressure of following 'complete cure' stories on social media can leave someone feeling guilty or discouraged if their own result looks different. These stories often leave out important details such as the original disease duration, the level of medical supervision, or even whether the result lasted over the long term. Focus on your own actual health markers rather than comparing yourself to content whose source is not fully known. [1, 2]

Studies suggest remission is associated with better health outcomes, but this is not an individual guarantee. In Look AHEAD, participants who achieved remission had lower rates of kidney and cardiovascular outcomes than those who did not, with factors such as weight, fitness and blood pressure potentially contributing. The practical message is that remission can be valuable without replacing management of every risk factor [6].

When to review with your doctor

Review with your doctor regularly according to the agreed plan, even if you feel well. Seek earlier review for symptoms of raised glucose such as marked thirst, frequent urination or unexplained tiredness, or if you lose weight unintentionally or outside the planned pace. Discussing any change to medicines or diet with your doctor before applying it yourself matters more than trying to adjust things alone. [1, 5]

If you are considering bariatric surgery as an option, or have had diabetes for a long time and want to understand the likelihood of remission in your case, that deserves a direct conversation with an internal medicine specialist rather than relying on general information online. Ongoing follow-up, not a single moment, is what protects your health over the long term. [5]

A typical recommended schedule includes HbA1c measurement every 3 to 6 months depending on how stable your condition is, an annual eye exam, foot checks at each visit or as your doctor directs, and periodic kidney and lipid tests. Ask your doctor specifically what schedule fits your situation, since it can differ based on how long you have had diabetes and any existing complications. [5]

If you need to connect this information with your health history and current plan, you can book an assessment and review the decision with the clinician or dietitian.
