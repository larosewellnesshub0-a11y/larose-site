Endoscopy in children: when might a doctor consider it?

Published: 11 September 2026 11 min read Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

What does paediatric endoscopy mean, and how do the types differ?

The word “endoscopy” describes more than one procedure, so it is important to know exactly what the clinician means. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, or gastroscopy, uses a thin flexible tube with a camera to examine the oesophagus, stomach and first part of the small intestine. Colonoscopy enters through the anus to examine the colon and usually the end of the small intestine. Other specialised procedures exist, but they are not automatically what is meant when a child has tummy pain. Each procedure answers a different clinical question and requires different preparation [1, 10].

Endoscopy may be diagnostic: the clinician examines the digestive lining, records visible changes and takes tiny tissue samples when needed. It may also be therapeutic in selected situations, such as controlling bleeding or removing a foreign object. The camera sees the inner surface; it does not examine every abdominal organ. Ultrasound, imaging or laboratory tests can therefore answer questions that endoscopy cannot, and the reverse is also true. The choice is not a competition between tests. It is about selecting the tool that can answer the question with an appropriate burden for the child [1, 10].

A child’s age, size and health affect the equipment, team and monitoring required. Guidance emphasises performance by a team experienced with children and equipped for their size, with clear documentation of the indication, extent of examination, biopsies, initial findings and any adverse event. Calling it a “simple scope” does not remove the need for a complete paediatric plan before and after the procedure [1, 2].

Recurring tummy pain does not automatically mean endoscopy

Tummy pain is common in children, and many have a functional pattern without visible inflammation or an ulcer. The clinician therefore begins with the story: where the pain occurs, how long it lasts, its relationship to meals and bowel movements, vomiting or diarrhoea, effects on sleep and school, and medicines already used. Examination and the child’s height and weight trajectory also matter. When the picture is reassuring and there are no alarm features, endoscopy is usually not the first step simply because pain has recurred [3].

A sound decision asks two questions: what suspected condition could endoscopy confirm or exclude, and would the result change treatment or follow-up? Without a clear question, a procedure may add anaesthesia and anxiety without a likely benefit. When there is persuasive evidence of organic disease, delaying solely because of fear is not helpful either. Our guide to recurring tummy aches in children explains pain patterns, growth and bowel habits in more detail without turning every ache into one diagnosis [2, 3].

Features such as weight loss or faltering growth, painful or difficult swallowing, persistent vomiting, gastrointestinal bleeding, unexplained anaemia, chronic diarrhoea, persistent fever or an important family history may increase the need for specialist assessment. They are not a self-service “endoscopy checklist”; the clinician interprets them with age, examination and test results. Their absence is not an absolute rule when the story is evolving. The decision should be individual and balanced rather than identical for every child with similar symptoms [1, 3].

When might the clinician consider endoscopy?

Upper endoscopy may be discussed when symptoms or results point to a problem in the oesophagus, stomach or duodenum and seeing the lining or obtaining tissue is important. Examples include difficult swallowing, food impaction, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, persistent unexplained vomiting, anaemia or faltering growth with concern for organic disease, or suspected oesophageal inflammation. In other situations, clinicians use endoscopy for surveillance or treatment, not as an indiscriminate search for a cause [1, 4, 7].

Colonoscopy has different indications, including unexplained lower gastrointestinal bleeding, chronic diarrhoea with inflammatory evidence, or suspected inflammatory bowel disease. When concern for inflammatory bowel disease in a child is genuine, specialist assessment may include upper endoscopy, ileocolonoscopy with biopsies and small-bowel imaging; the paediatric gastroenterology team decides which steps are required and in what order [11]. Constipation alone is usually assessed through history and examination, and our guide to childhood constipation: when does a child need assessment? covers that pathway without drifting into endoscopy [3].

There is an important distinction between planned endoscopy and emergency foreign-object removal. Swallowing a button battery, more than one high-powered magnet, or an object that becomes stuck with drooling, difficulty swallowing or breathing needs immediate assessment. Do not wait for a clinic appointment or try to make the child vomit. Object type and location, time since ingestion and the child’s symptoms determine urgency. A button battery lodged in the oesophagus can cause severe injury rapidly, and guidance treats its removal as an emergency [1, 12].

What may happen before the decision, and when can tests be enough?

Before endoscopy, the clinician may request blood tests, stool tests or imaging according to the question; every child does not need the same panel. Laboratory tests may look for evidence of anaemia, inflammation or coeliac disease, while ultrasound assesses selected organs outside the stomach and bowel lumen. One abnormal result does not automatically mean endoscopy, and a normal result does not close every possibility. Meaning comes from combining the story, examination and results [3, 5, 11].

For coeliac disease, current guidance allows diagnosis without an intestinal biopsy in some children when specific blood results meet strict criteria, are confirmed in a second sample and are managed by a specialist. Other children need endoscopy with biopsies from defined sites in the duodenum. Do not stop gluten before assessment is complete; changing the diet too early can alter blood and tissue findings and make a secure diagnosis harder [5].

H. pylori is another example of asking the right question. Guidelines do not support a “test and treat” approach for every child with tummy pain. The primary aim is to identify the cause of symptoms, not merely to look for the organism. When there is a genuine indication for invasive assessment, biopsies can support diagnosis and antibiotic-susceptibility-led treatment. Read H. pylori in children: when might testing be considered? for more about testing, because this page stays focused on the endoscopy decision [6].

Reflux does not mean every child needs endoscopy either. The procedure may help when alarm features are present, symptoms persist despite an appropriate plan, or another cause of oesophageal inflammation must be excluded. It does not measure every reflux episode and a normal appearance does not rule out all reflux disease. The clinician chooses among a directed treatment trial, reflux monitoring and endoscopy according to age, pattern and the question that needs answering [4].

Why might the clinician take biopsies when everything looks normal?

The camera shows the appearance of the lining, but some diseases are more apparent under a microscope than to the naked eye. A biopsy is a very small tissue sample collected with an instrument passed through the scope; the child does not feel the tissue being sampled during the procedure. The number and location of biopsies depend on the question. A stomach sample cannot substitute for an oesophageal or duodenal sample. Planning biopsy sites helps the pathologist interpret the findings correctly [2, 5, 7, 10].

In eosinophilic oesophagitis, for example, symptoms vary by age: an infant may refuse feeds, while an older child may struggle to swallow or have food become stuck. Diagnosis depends on symptoms and oesophageal biopsies, and appearance alone may be insufficient. In coeliac disease, the number and location of duodenal samples matter when the child does not qualify for a no-biopsy pathway. Suspected inflammatory bowel disease requires sampling from several segments rather than one random biopsy [5, 7, 11].

There are two layers of results after the procedure. The initial report describes what the clinician saw, such as inflammation, an ulcer or narrowing, and may be discussed with the family that day. Tissue requires preparation and microscopic examination by a pathologist, so those results arrive later according to the laboratory and case. A normal visual appearance does not guarantee normal histology, and the reverse can occur; final decisions should not be based on one photograph or a brief comment immediately after recovery [2, 10].

Before consent, ask which areas will be examined, whether biopsies are expected, who will explain the final report and what happens if results are normal. These questions prevent endoscopy from being treated as “the end of the road.” It is one part of a diagnostic pathway, and its real value comes from connecting findings with symptoms, tests and growth to produce a clear plan [2].

Preparation and anaesthesia: a plan tailored to the child

If endoscopy is agreed, the centre gives fasting instructions based on the child’s age, procedure time and anaesthesia plan. Follow the written instructions exactly. Do not use a fasting interval heard from another family, and do not prolong fasting independently because the child may become uncomfortable or dehydrated. If the child accidentally eats or drinks, tell the team clearly; they decide whether postponement is needed to protect the airway [8, 9].

Before the appointment, tell the team about allergies, asthma, severe snoring or pauses in breathing during sleep, heart or lung disease, a previous anaesthetic problem, a bleeding disorder, loose teeth, and every medicine or supplement. If a new cold, cough, fever or vomiting develops, contact the centre instead of hiding the symptom or cancelling without advice. Children with additional risk need appropriate assessment and arrangements, sometimes in a setting with higher-level support [8, 9].

Most paediatric procedures require deep sedation or general anaesthesia according to age, procedure, centre policy and the child’s condition. The responsible team should assess the child, monitor breathing, oxygen and circulation, and be able to manage the airway. The plan is not one fixed dose for every child. Its purpose is to complete the procedure safely without harmful movement and ensure recovery to an appropriate level of consciousness before discharge [8, 9].

Prepare the child with simple, honest, age-appropriate language: “The doctor will use a small camera to look at your tummy while you are asleep,” without frightening detail or promising there will be no sensation afterwards. Bring reports, a medicine list, growth records and anything the centre requests. Arrange who will take the child home and supervise the first day, because anaesthesia may temporarily affect activity and concentration [8].

After endoscopy: what to expect and when not to wait

After upper endoscopy, the child is monitored in recovery until breathing, consciousness and movement are suitable for discharge. A mild sore throat, bloating or temporary nausea can occur. Gas or mild cramps may follow colonoscopy. The team explains when to restart drinks and food, when school and activity can resume, and how medicines should be handled. Follow the centre’s discharge sheet because it reflects what was actually done [8, 10].

Endoscopy is generally safe when appropriately selected and prepared, but it is not risk-free. Possible problems include an anaesthetic reaction or breathing event, bleeding, infection or a perforation in the digestive wall. Risk varies with the child and procedure; removing an object or dilating a narrowing is different from diagnostic examination with small biopsies. Before consent, the team should explain the expected benefit, alternatives and case-specific risks [1, 8, 9, 10].

Seek urgent care after endoscopy if the child has difficulty breathing; severe or worsening chest or abdominal pain; repeated or bloody vomiting; black stool or a concerning amount of visible blood; fever with deterioration; a very swollen abdomen; fainting or unusual lethargy; or cannot drink. A mild sore throat is different from worsening swallowing difficulty. If uncertain, use the emergency contact on the discharge instructions or obtain urgent assessment [10].

Before any planned endoscopy, green or bloody vomit, dehydration signs, severe constant pain or inability to keep fluids down also require urgent assessment rather than waiting for the appointment. Our guide to child vomiting and dehydration: important warning signs explains the details that help describe the situation, but it does not replace emergency care when a child is deteriorating [3].

Make the decision shared and the next step clear

Before agreeing, ask the clinician to state the medical question in one sentence: “What are we trying to find or exclude with this endoscopy?” Ask about alternatives, what may happen if you wait, and whether the result will change treatment. Clarify the procedure type and location, anaesthesia plan, likely biopsies and who will follow up pathology. If the answers are unclear, you are entitled to a simpler explanation before deciding [1, 2, 8].

Bring a short summary of symptom onset and frequency, links with food and bowel movements, height and weight, medicines and supplements, earlier tests, allergies and previous anaesthetic experiences. Do not independently start stomach medicine, antibiotics, a gluten-free diet or broad food restriction to “prepare” for endoscopy. Such changes can obscure evidence or impair nutrition. The treating clinician decides what should continue or stop and when [4, 5, 6].

For recurring symptoms without an emergency, you can request a paediatric appointment at the clinic so assessment begins with history, examination and growth. The clinician can then decide whether paediatric gastroenterology input, another test or endoscopy in an equipped centre is appropriate. This clinic mention is only an organisational next step; an article cannot decide the procedure for an individual child, and the final choice remains with the treating clinician and family [1, 3].
