Vitamin D in children: when to test and review intake

Published: 11 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

Vitamin D matters, but general symptoms are not a diagnosis

Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium and phosphorus and supports bone mineralisation during growth. Severe, prolonged deficiency, especially alongside low calcium intake, can cause osteomalacia or rickets while a child's bones are still developing. That does not mean every episode of tiredness, leg pain, sweating, poor appetite or mildly delayed movement is caused by vitamin D. These are broad features with many possible explanations. Some children have a low result without obvious symptoms, while others have pain or fatigue for a different reason [1, 4].

A clinician therefore does not turn one symptom into a diagnosis. Assessment considers age, growth, food, breast or formula feeding, supplements, sun exposure, skin pigmentation and coverage, medical conditions and medicines. Examination may include bones, muscles, walking, teeth and development as appropriate for age. When rickets is genuinely suspected, diagnosis is not made from a vitamin D value alone; history, examination, mineral and bone-related blood tests, and sometimes radiographs work together [4]. The result is a tool used to answer a clinical question, not an isolated verdict or a reason to copy a treatment dose from the internet.

Who needs a closer review of risk factors?

Risk rises when several factors occur together. Breastfed or partly breastfed infants need an age-appropriate prevention plan from early life under local guidance because human milk alone usually does not provide the full requirement. A premature or low-birth-weight infant may need a different plan. Later, low intake of fortified foods, fish or eggs, a narrowly planned vegetarian pattern, food allergy excluding whole groups, or a very restricted food list gives the clinician a reason to review intake rather than assuming that the phrase “healthy diet” answers the question [2, 7, 8].

Other factors affect production, absorption or handling of vitamin D: spending very little time outdoors, covering most skin, darker skin pigmentation, coeliac or other intestinal disease, liver or kidney disease, malabsorption, and prolonged use of some antiseizure medicines or corticosteroids. Obesity can be associated with lower results, but it is not by itself a reason to test every otherwise healthy child [1, 9]. The trajectory also matters. Slowing height, poor weight gain, motor delay or a changed gait needs broader assessment, not a single nutrient test. Our guide child growth is a trajectory explains why serial measurements matter more than comparison with a friend [4, 9].

When is a vitamin D test useful, and when is it unnecessary?

The usual marker used to assess vitamin D status is serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, or 25(OH)D. Specialist guidance does not recommend routine screening for every healthy child growing normally, including testing based on excess weight alone, because a result may create confusion without changing care. Testing is more appropriate when there are signs of rickets or osteomalacia, repeated low-trauma fractures, a disease or medicine known to affect bone or absorption, or prolonged high-dose use that needs monitoring [5, 9, 10].

If a clinician suspects a bone or calcium disorder, 25(OH)D may be accompanied by calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, parathyroid hormone and kidney tests, with radiographs selected according to findings. The 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D test is not the routine measure of stores and is reserved for particular questions. Definitions of “deficiency” and “sufficiency” are also not identical across every organisation and laboratory, so the value is read with its unit and clinical context [1, 3, 4]. Avoid an untargeted panel or frequent retesting merely to chase a number. Ask what question the test will answer and which decision would change because of it.

Food, sunlight and prevention all need context

Natural food sources of vitamin D are limited. Oily fish and egg yolk contribute, while some milks, alternatives and cereals are fortified depending on the country and product. Fortification cannot be inferred from package design; read the label for serving size and amount because not every yoghurt or plant drink contains the same addition. Calcium matters too, because nutritional rickets can result from vitamin D deficiency, low calcium intake, or both. A varied diet supports bone health, but food alone may not meet the preventive plan advised for certain ages or risk factors [1, 4, 8].

Skin produces vitamin D in response to ultraviolet radiation, but production varies widely with time of day, season, pollution, skin pigmentation, clothing and the area exposed. No single sun duration is guaranteed to be both adequate and safe for every child, and an infant or child should never be exposed to sunburn in pursuit of a vitamin; protection from burning remains important [4, 7]. Prevention in infants and young children therefore commonly relies on a clear supplement programme based on country and feeding pattern rather than guessing from sunshine. If you want to review the whole diet and growth pattern instead of one nutrient, our page child nutrition and growth visit explains what to prepare for the appointment [2, 6, 7].

A preventive supplement is not a treatment dose

There is an important distinction between routine prevention and treatment of confirmed deficiency or rickets. Many international sources use 400 IU daily during the first year and a dietary requirement of 600 IU after age one, but how that amount is achieved and whether a supplement is routinely advised varies by country, milk feeding, diet, age and risk [1, 2, 4, 8]. Those figures are not a treatment prescription. A child with confirmed deficiency or chronic disease may need a different clinician-prescribed regimen, with calcium and other laboratory monitoring when appropriate.

Check the product name, concentration and unit before every dose. Vitamin D liquids are not interchangeable: one may state the amount per drop and another per full millilitre. Use the supplied dropper or oral syringe, and if the product changes, recheck the instructions with a pharmacist or clinician. Review infant formula, multivitamins, cod-liver oil and calcium products too, because the same vitamin can appear in more than one container [7, 10]. Keep every product closed and out of the child's reach, and record what was actually given. Do not double the next dose after a forgotten one without advice, and ask before repeating a full dose if the child spits or vomits it.

If the result is low, the plan is bigger than raising a number

When a result is low, the clinician first checks its unit, laboratory reference and why the test was ordered. The review then asks whether there are bone signs or symptoms, what calcium and vitamin D the child receives, whether a preventive supplement was taken consistently, whether a medicine or condition affects absorption, and whether related tests are abnormal. A low result can be part of the explanation, but it does not automatically account for fatigue or every pain. If deficiency persists despite an apparently correct plan, questions about absorption, liver or kidney disease, medicines and the diagnosis matter more than increasing the dose indefinitely [1, 4].

A treatment plan specifies product, dose, duration, review date and whether calcium, 25(OH)D or other markers need reassessment. Large daily doses or a single high dose are not home experiments, and an infant's plan differs from an adolescent's. Do not stop early merely because the child seems better, and do not prolong treatment because a social-media post calls the number “suboptimal.” Recent guidance emphasises that optimal thresholds for preventing diseases beyond bone remain uncertain and that the benefit-risk balance of indiscriminate testing and extra supplementation is unclear [3]. Agree on a defined goal—treating rickets, correcting confirmed deficiency or preventing deficiency because of risk—and a clear point for ending or changing the plan.

Warning signs need prompt action, and excess dosing can harm

Arrange a prompt paediatric assessment for new bowing after a child begins standing, persistent bone pain, clear muscle weakness, delayed or lost motor skills, changed walking, swollen wrists or ankles, faltering growth, or fractures after minor trauma. A wide fontanelle or delayed teeth can have several causes but should be mentioned as part of the picture. These findings do not prove rickets and are not a reason to give a high dose before examination; rickets has nutritional and non-nutritional causes that need distinction [4].

Seek urgent care for a seizure, breathing difficulty, loss of consciousness, sudden profound weakness or inability to stand as usual. If a child takes a large amount accidentally or drinks the bottle, contact emergency services or a poison centre immediately and take the container; do not wait for symptoms or induce vomiting. Excess vitamin D can raise calcium and cause vomiting, poor appetite, constipation, excessive thirst and urination, dehydration, weakness or confusion, and severe toxicity can injure the kidneys or heart [1, 10]. These symptoms during high-dose use or combined products deserve rapid assessment even when every product was given with good intentions.

What should you prepare, and what is the next step?

Before the visit, photograph the front and back of every supplement container and record concentration, dose, number of days used and missed doses. Note feeding type and approximate formula volume for an infant, usual fortified foods, fish, eggs and dairy foods, and any food exclusions. Bring dated earlier results with their units, imaging, a medicine list and the child's growth record. Record when pain or weakness began, whether it wakes the child, and whether walking, stairs or play have changed. This is more informative than saying that the child “takes drops” or “gets plenty of sun” [1, 8].

At the clinic, assessment begins with history, growth and examination. The clinician then decides whether the child needs reassurance and a consistent prevention plan, adjustment of food or supplement, targeted testing, treatment of confirmed deficiency, or referral to paediatric endocrinology or another specialty. No outcome is guaranteed, and the final decision is individual and aligned with applicable local guidance. If the child is stable and concern continues or a result needs interpretation, you can request a paediatric consultation and bring the list and containers. This information is educational and does not replace examination; use urgent care rather than waiting for an appointment after a seizure, breathing difficulty or overdose [4, 10].
