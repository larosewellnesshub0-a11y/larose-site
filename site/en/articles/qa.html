Knowledge Centre

Ask the doctor

Questions in patients' own words, answered directly by La Rose doctors without remote diagnosis or ready-made promises.

Ask the doctor

Is there one test that proves insulin resistance?

No single number is a final verdict for everyone. Fasting glucose, HbA1c and an oral glucose tolerance test, when indicated, examine different parts of glucose regulation. Fasting insulin and calculations based on it may help in selected settings, but results vary with laboratory method and fasting conditions and should not be read away from glucose and medical history.

Symptoms such as early hunger, sleepiness after food or dark neck skin are not diagnostic either. The same experiences occur with poor sleep, anaemia or an unbalanced meal. Assessment reviews family history, waist, medicines, blood pressure, lipids, liver health and PCOS when relevant, then chooses the test that answers the real question.

Do not order a large panel yourself or remove carbohydrate because of one result. Bring previous results and record symptoms and timing. Your clinician can establish whether prediabetes, diabetes or broader metabolic risk needs a plan instead of attaching every problem to the insulin-resistance label.

Ask the doctor

Can I have fatty liver even if my weight is in the normal range?

Yes. Excess weight is an important risk factor, but it is not required for fatty liver. Abdominal fat distribution, insulin resistance, glucose and triglycerides, genes, some medicines and alcohol where relevant can contribute. A BMI in the normal range does not reveal body composition or metabolic risk on its own.

Ultrasound may identify fatty change but cannot by itself show inflammation or grade fibrosis. The clinician therefore reviews liver tests, blood count, glucose, lipids, blood pressure and medicine history, using further calculations or tests when fibrosis risk needs estimating. Normal liver enzymes do not cancel that assessment.

Do not begin a 'detox', a liver supplement or weight loss you do not need. Your plan may focus on food quality, movement, body composition and management of glucose and lipids, adapted to the cause and risk. Review the result with a clinician even when appearance and weight do not suggest a problem.

Ask the doctor

After H. pylori treatment, do I need a test to confirm it is gone?

Yes. Confirming eradication matters because symptom improvement does not prove the infection has cleared, while continuing bloating or heartburn does not prove it remains. A urea breath test or stool antigen test may be used for confirmation. A blood antibody test can remain positive after treatment and does not answer the same question.

Timing matters. Antibiotics and strong acid-suppressing medicine can produce a false-negative result if testing is too early or medicines have not been withheld appropriately. Do not stop prescribed treatment yourself; your clinician selects the test and gives the interval after treatment and before testing, taking account of your health if stopping medicine is difficult.

If the result remains positive, do not automatically repeat the same antibiotics. A further plan depends on previous treatment, local resistance, adherence and side effects. If the test is negative but symptoms remain, look for another explanation rather than continuing to chase H. pylori.

Ask the doctor

I have bowel symptoms. Do I need a colonoscopy to be sure it is IBS?

No. Not everyone with pain, bloating or altered stool needs a colonoscopy. IBS is diagnosed from a defined pattern of pain, its relationship with bowel movements and changes in stool form or frequency, alongside examination and assessment for warning features. There is no particular colonoscopy appearance that proves IBS.

Colonoscopy becomes important with blood in stool, anaemia, unintentional weight loss, persistent night symptoms, significant family history, new onset at an age that raises concern, or tests suggesting inflammation or another disease. It also forms part of age- and risk-appropriate bowel cancer screening even when symptoms differ.

Begin with a consultation and a brief record of symptoms, stool, medicines and food. Your clinician may select blood, stool or coeliac tests before deciding. An unnecessary colonoscopy does not guarantee reassurance, while delaying one in the presence of a warning feature is inappropriate; your story determines the decision.

Ask the doctor

I am tired all the time. Should I take vitamin D without a test?

Not as an automatic first step. Fatigue is a broad symptom that can come from poor sleep, stress, anaemia, thyroid or glucose problems, infection, medicines and many other causes. Vitamin D deficiency may be present, but tiredness alone cannot diagnose it, and someone else's response to a supplement does not prove the same cause.

The clinician asks about duration, sleep, mood, food, periods when relevant, medicines, and features such as weight loss, fever, breathlessness or palpitations. Examination guides whether a vitamin D test is useful and whether a blood count, thyroid, glucose or different tests are more relevant. High-dose vitamin D is not harmless simply because it is a vitamin; excess can raise calcium and cause harm.

Severe breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, sudden weakness or rapid deterioration needs urgent assessment. For quieter persistent fatigue, arrange an organised review rather than starting several supplements. If deficiency is confirmed, dose and follow-up can be matched to the result and your health.

Ask the doctor

My ferritin is low but my blood count is normal. Do I have anaemia?

You may have iron deficiency without clear anaemia. Iron stores can fall before haemoglobin does, so a normal blood count does not cancel a low ferritin result. Ferritin still needs context because inflammation can raise it and conceal deficiency, while the laboratory method, history and other blood indices add meaning.

The more important question is why stores are low. Insufficient intake, heavy periods, pregnancy, frequent blood donation, digestive blood loss or poor absorption are possibilities reviewed according to the person. In men, after menopause, or with black stool, weight loss or digestive pain, searching for blood loss matters and a supplement alone is not enough.

Do not begin a high iron dose yourself. The clinician links the result with symptoms, identifies a likely cause, selects suitable treatment and plans repeat testing. Improvement in tiredness alone cannot confirm the diagnosis because fatigue has other causes and iron stores need follow-up.

Ask the doctor

My child refuses meat. Does that mean iron deficiency is certain?

No. Refusing meat alone does not prove iron deficiency. A child may obtain iron from poultry, fish, pulses, fortified cereals and other foods within a varied day, although absorption differs by source. Conversely, a child who sometimes eats meat may still be at risk depending on age, growth speed and the rest of the food pattern.

Look at the whole picture. Are large amounts of milk or juice replacing meals? Is the food list extremely narrow? Is there pallor, tiredness, poor concentration or craving for non-food substances? Symptoms guide questions but do not diagnose deficiency. The growth curve, dietary history and examination show whether a blood count and ferritin are useful.

Do not pressure the child to eat a large piece and do not start an unplanned iron supplement. Offer an age-safe texture and a familiar iron source beside an accepted food, repeating it calmly. If variety is very limited or growth or activity has changed, arrange assessment for a practical plan and a decision about testing or treatment.

Ask the doctor

I have taken acid medicine for a long time. Should I stop or continue?

It depends on why the medicine was prescribed, the dose, your response and risk factors. Some cases of reflux disease or its complications need longer treatment, while others may step down or stop after a defined period with review. Long use alone does not prove harm, and symptom improvement alone does not prove the medicine is no longer needed.

Do not stop suddenly yourself because symptoms may rebound temporarily, and do not continue automatically for years without review. The clinician confirms the diagnosis, asks about swallowing difficulty, bleeding and weight loss, reviews other medicines, then selects the lowest effective plan and whether endoscopy or tests are useful in your circumstances.

Bring the medicine name, dose and duration rather than relying on box colour or the phrase 'stomach protector'. Vomiting blood, black stool, severe chest pain or worsening difficulty swallowing needs prompt assessment instead of adjusting treatment at home.

Ask the doctor

My ultrasound found a gallstone, but I only have bloating. Is the stone the cause?

Not necessarily. Gallstones are common and may be found incidentally without causing symptoms. Typical biliary pain is strong and steady in the upper abdomen, often on the right or in the centre, and may spread to the back or shoulder with nausea. Bloating alone has many possible food, stomach, bowel and medicine-related causes.

The clinician reviews timing, any pain, stool, heartburn and medicines, examines the abdomen, and reads the ultrasound with liver tests. If the gallbladder and ducts look normal and the stone is silent, its presence alone often does not require an operation. Clear pain attacks, inflammation or obstruction change that decision.

Do not begin a severe diet or turn every rich meal into a gallbladder test. Keep a brief record of symptoms and meals, and seek urgent assessment for persistent pain with fever, jaundice or repeated vomiting. The aim is to establish that the stone explains the complaint before building treatment around it.

Ask the doctor

I have a small kidney stone. Can I wait for it to pass at home?

Sometimes, but the word 'small' is not enough. The chance of passage depends on size and position in the urinary tract, obstruction or infection, kidney function, ability to drink and control of pain. Observation needs a clear treatment plan, follow-up point and warning signs that mean returning immediately.

Fever or chills, very little or no urine, vomiting that prevents fluids, uncontrolled pain, severe general illness, one kidney or pregnancy all require prompt assessment. Infection with obstruction should not wait for a stone to pass. Ultrasound may show kidney swelling, but some stones need more detailed imaging to establish position.

Do not suddenly force excessive water during pain in an attempt to push the stone, and do not use someone else's medicine. Follow fluid and treatment advice suited to you, and keep the stone for analysis if it passes. After the episode, prevention depends on stone type, history and tests, not simply removing calcium.

Ask the doctor

My HbA1c is in the prediabetes range. Do I have to take medicine?

Not necessarily. Prediabetes means glucose is above the normal range but not in the diabetes range, and the first step is to review the result in context. Anaemia, altered red-cell turnover and some health conditions can affect HbA1c, so the clinician may confirm the picture with fasting glucose or another test when needed.

Changes to food, movement, sleep and weight management when excess weight is present form the foundation and can lower the chance of progression. Medicine may suit selected people according to overall risk, age, weight, previous gestational diabetes, other tests and related illness. One answer does not fit everyone; always refusing medicine and automatically starting it are both oversimplifications.

Bring all glucose results, medicines and family history, then agree on measurable changes and a review date. The aim is to improve risk and protect health without promising that the result can never rise. Increased thirst and urination, unintentional weight loss or marked fatigue deserve faster assessment.

Ask the doctor

I sleep too little and my weight is stuck. Is sleep the reason?

Too little sleep may be part of the reason, but it is rarely the only explanation. Short or broken sleep can increase hunger and preference for quick food, reduce energy for movement and make a plan harder to follow. Staying awake longer also creates more time for snacks. Weight plateaus are also influenced by portions, drinks, medicines, constipation, fluid, activity and other health factors.

Instead of punishing yourself with a harsher diet, record sleep time and quality alongside hunger and food for several days. Keep a consistent sleep window where possible, reduce late caffeine and screens, and make the evening meal suitable rather than going to bed extremely hungry or uncomfortably full. Review sleep as part of the whole plan.

Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, choking during sleep, morning headaches or marked daytime sleepiness need assessment for possible sleep apnoea. Treating a sleep disorder matters for health even if the scale does not move immediately.

Ask the doctor

I eat under stress even when I am not hungry. Is that weak willpower?

No. Eating under stress is an understandable behaviour that may briefly soothe an uncomfortable feeling, especially when the day also contains restriction, delayed meals or too little sleep. Calling it weak willpower adds blame without changing the cycle: pressure, quick eating, temporary relief, then guilt and more pressure.

Begin by noticing the pattern without judgement: what happened before eating, your hunger level, where you were and how you felt afterwards. Keep satisfying meals regular so extreme hunger does not combine with stress, and prepare another brief response such as a quiet walk, a call or slower breathing before deciding. The first goal is not to abolish emotional eating but to widen your options.

Episodes of lost control, rapidly eating a large amount, hiding food, self-induced vomiting, laxative use or severe distress deserve specialist psychological support alongside nutrition care. A useful plan does not punish an episode with a harsher diet; it reviews triggers and builds safety and regularity.

Ask the doctor

Is more protein always better?

No. Protein matters for muscle, satiety and body functions, but need is not one number for everyone, and extra protein does not automatically become muscle. Requirement changes with body size, age, activity, training, goals, pregnancy or breastfeeding and illness. Kidney or liver disease and other conditions may need closer calculation and monitoring.

It is often more useful to distribute a suitable source across meals than to concentrate most of it at the end of the day. Meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy and pulses can all contribute according to preference and health. Protein powder is a convenience for some people, not a condition for progress, and cannot replace food variety, resistance training and adequate sleep.

Do not copy a number from an athlete or influencer, and do not begin an unknown supplement. At assessment, the nutrition clinician reviews your day, body composition, training, illness and tests, then sets a practical range and turns it into meals you can sustain.

Ask the doctor

Does everyone need eight glasses of water a day?

No single number of glasses suits every body and every day. Need changes with body size, climate, sweating, activity, pregnancy, breastfeeding, fever and diarrhoea, as well as certain illnesses and medicines. Water also comes from different foods and drinks, not plain water alone.

Thirst and pale urine are often practical cues for a healthy person, but they are not precise rules in every setting. Persistently dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth or reduced urine may suggest too little fluid, while drinking enormous amounts quickly can lower blood sodium and become dangerous. Aim to spread drinks across the day and increase them with heat and effort, not turn bottles into a contest.

If you have heart failure, kidney or liver disease, swelling, or a prescribed fluid limit, do not apply general internet advice. Clearly increased thirst and urination despite drinking also needs review because diabetes, medicine or another problem may be responsible rather than an ordinary need for more water.

Ask the doctor

If I start weight-management medicine, will I depend on it for life?

Needing medicine for a long time is not the same as addiction. Obesity is a chronic condition for many people, and some weight-management medicines work only while treatment continues; appetite or weight may rise again after stopping. Duration depends on the benefit to you, side effects, your health and the other parts of the plan, not one fixed course for everyone.

Do not stop treatment or change the dose by yourself simply because you have reached a goal. Review why you want to stop, whether reducing or changing treatment is appropriate, and the food, movement and follow-up plan afterwards. Some regain can happen, and it does not prove failure or weak willpower; it means the maintenance plan needs review.

If treatment is not providing clear benefit or side effects are troublesome, the safe response is to reassess the plan, not automatically continue forever or stop abruptly. Ask from the start about the treatment goal, signs that it is helping and the date for review.

Ask the doctor

My hair started shedding during medical weight loss. Is the medicine the cause?

There may be a connection, but the medicine should not automatically be treated as the only cause. Rapid loss, too little protein or energy, iron or other nutrient deficiency, thyroid problems, psychological stress or a recent illness can all increase shedding. Timing, the pattern of loss and other symptoms help separate them.

Do not stop the medicine or begin supplements at random. Record when shedding began and how quickly weight has changed, then review your food and every medicine and supplement with your clinician. Examination can establish whether the pace of the plan or nutrition needs changing, whether targeted tests are appropriate, or whether the scalp needs a dermatology assessment.

Clear bald patches, marked scalp itching or pain, inflammation, eyebrow loss, palpitations or severe fatigue deserve earlier review. Treating the cause matters more than adding a hair product without a diagnosis.

Ask the doctor

Can I use weight-management medicine during pregnancy or breastfeeding?

Weight-loss medicines are not used during pregnancy or while planning a pregnancy. During breastfeeding, most are not recommended because safety differs by the substance and the evidence available. The decision should not come from another person's experience or the medicine name alone.

If pregnancy occurs during treatment, contact the prescriber and the obstetric clinician promptly, with the active ingredient, dose and date of the last dose. Do not take another dose before receiving clear instructions, but do not assume that exposure alone proves harm. Individual assessment determines the next step.

During pregnancy and breastfeeding, the nutrition goal is not a harsh diet or rapid loss. Care focuses on adequate nourishment, monitoring pregnancy or feeding, and managing diabetes, blood pressure or deficiencies with the responsible team. The timing of any weight-management medicine can be reconsidered after this stage.

Ask the doctor

I have heard mesotherapy can cause problems. What are the risks?

Mesotherapy is not risk-free, and the word itself does not tell you which substance will be injected or whether its use is appropriate for that area. Temporary pain, redness, swelling or bruising may occur. Less common but more important risks include allergy, infection, lumps, scarring or a change in skin contour.

Before a session, ask for the substance and its source, why it is being used, the injector's experience, infection-control measures and the alternatives. Medical history, allergies, blood-thinning medicines, problems with wound healing, pregnancy or breastfeeding can change the decision. Results are not guaranteed; evidence and outcomes vary with the substance, area and individual.

Increasing pain, spreading redness, fever, discharge, marked colour change or unusual swelling after an injection needs prompt medical contact. Never inject a product yourself, and do not proceed without knowing exactly what is being injected and why.

Dr. Shimaa Fouad
Ask the doctor

Is a mesotherapy result guaranteed?

No cosmetic result can be guaranteed before assessment. Response varies with the problem, area, thickness of local fat or degree of skin laxity, the substance used and how change is measured. Local mesotherapy is not an obesity treatment and cannot replace weight loss when the concern is general.

Before starting, agree on a specific, realistic aim, consistent photographs or measurements, and a clear review point for deciding whether any change justifies continuing. Photographs should use similar lighting and position where possible, because a mirror impression can vary from one day to another.

If there is no objective improvement by the agreed review point, reassess the diagnosis and plan instead of repeating sessions indefinitely. Ask about alternatives, adverse effects and whether choosing no procedure is also reasonable.

Dr. Shimaa Fouad
Ask the doctor

I have an event coming up. What is the fastest safe way to lose weight?

There is no safe, guaranteed number for everyone before an event. What is realistic depends on your starting point, time, health, medicines, and whether the aim is actual fat loss or simply less bloating and a better fit. Crash diets, diuretics and laxatives may shift fluid or make you unwell without producing a durable result.

Begin with regular meals containing protein, vegetables and a suitable carbohydrate source, reduce sugary drinks and very salty food, sleep adequately and move as your health allows. Prescription treatment is not a quick fix to start without assessment; selection, dosing and follow-up are part of its safety.

Diabetes, high blood pressure, pregnancy or breastfeeding, an eating-disorder history, or kidney or liver disease all require medical review before a major change. Set a realistic aim for the event and a plan you can continue afterwards, rather than a cycle of restriction and regain.

Dr. Shimaa Fouad
Ask the doctor

Are slimming pills sold without a prescription safe?

A product being described as natural or available without a prescription does not prove that it is safe. Some slimming products from uncertain sources may contain undeclared medicines, stimulants, laxatives or inconsistent doses, which can affect the heart, blood pressure or liver and interact with other treatment.

Do not combine products or rely on packaging and testimonials. Before use, review the active ingredients, licensing and source with a doctor or pharmacist who knows your health and medicines. If the ingredients are unclear, that alone is a reason not to start.

Severe palpitations, chest pain, fainting, breathlessness, confusion, persistent vomiting or jaundice after a slimming product requires urgent assessment. Take the packet or a photograph of its ingredients and do not try to treat the reaction with another product.

Ask the doctor

Is intermittent fasting suitable for me, or could it make me unwell?

Intermittent fasting is a way of arranging meal times, not magic and not a requirement for weight loss. It may simplify one person's day and increase hunger or late eating for another. Outcome also depends on food quality, amount and whether the pattern is sustainable without severe restriction.

Do not start it by yourself if you have diabetes and use insulin or medicines that can lower glucose, a history of an eating disorder, undernutrition or low weight, or during pregnancy or breastfeeding. Children, teenagers, older adults and people with chronic illness need individual assessment before fasting for weight loss.

If you develop severe dizziness, fainting, confusion, shaking or sweating with possible low glucose, stop the fast and follow the emergency plan for your condition. The useful plan is the one that structures food, preserves energy and fits your life, whether or not it includes a fasting window.

Ask the doctor

I eat a proper meal and feel hungry again an hour later. Why?

Hunger returning quickly has more than one possible cause. The meal may be too small or mostly rapidly digested carbohydrate with little protein or fibre, or you may eat so fast that fullness is hard to notice. Too little sleep, stress, a new exercise routine and some medicines can also change appetite.

For several days, record the meal, time, hunger before and after, sleep and medicines. Try combining a protein source with vegetables or fruit, a suitable carbohydrate and a moderate amount of fat, and eat at a calmer pace. The aim is not to suppress hunger by force, but to see whether the meal is satisfying and whether the signal is physical hunger, habit or stress.

A sudden appetite increase with marked thirst and urination, unintentional weight loss, palpitations, shaking or pronounced fatigue needs medical assessment. Tests should follow the history and examination rather than hunger alone.

Ask the doctor

I have episodes when I eat quickly and cannot stop. Is that normal?

An eating episode accompanied by a sense of lost control deserves attention and does not mean that you lack willpower. A diagnosis cannot be made from one episode or the amount alone. Assessment asks about recurrence, speed, eating without hunger, secrecy, distress afterwards, and whether there is self-induced vomiting, laxative use or punitive fasting.

Do not punish yourself after an episode by skipping food or exercising harshly, because restriction may feed the cycle. Return to regular meals and record what happened beforehand and how you felt without rigid calculations. Assessment by a mental health professional experienced in eating disorders, alongside nutrition support, can establish the right help.

Fainting, vomiting blood, chest pain, severe dehydration, thoughts of self-harm, or repeated vomiting or laxative use needs urgent help. You can seek support even if your weight has not changed; eating disorders do not have one body shape.

Dr. Shimaa Fouad
Ask the doctor

How can I tell physical hunger from eating because I am upset?

No single test separates the two every time. Physical hunger often builds gradually and accepts different foods, while emotional eating may appear suddenly after an event and ask for one particular food. These are clues, not rules; you may be stressed and physically hungry at the same time.

Before eating, pause briefly and ask when you last ate, whether there are physical sensations such as an empty stomach or weakness, what emotion is present, and whether any suitable meal would satisfy you or only one food feels acceptable. If you are hungry, eat an appropriate meal or snack without guilt. If emotion is stronger, add another response such as a call, a quiet walk or slow breathing without turning it into a test of willpower.

A brief record of the situation, hunger, food and feeling afterwards can reveal the pattern over several days. If eating has become the only way to soothe yourself, or lost control and distress recur, specialist support is more useful than a stricter diet.

Ask the doctor

When does emotional eating need specialist help?

Seek help when eating is repeatedly tied to stress, you feel out of control, hide food, or live through guilt and restriction followed by another episode. Effects on sleep, work or relationships, or avoiding social occasions because of food or body concerns, are enough reason to speak with a professional even if weight is stable.

A nutrition clinician can help regularise meals and prevent extreme hunger, but a mental health professional is an essential part of care when depression, marked anxiety, trauma, binge episodes or compensatory behaviour is present. The aim is not to remove every comforting food; it is to build more ways to find safety and relief.

You do not have to wait until the problem becomes severe. Self-induced vomiting, laxative use, fainting, thoughts of self-harm, or inability to eat and drink needs urgent help.

Ask the doctor

I snore and my breathing stops during sleep. Is my weight the cause?

Excess weight can increase the chance of obstructive sleep apnoea, but it is not the only cause. Loud snoring with witnessed pauses, choking or gasping during sleep, morning headache or marked daytime sleepiness deserves assessment because snoring alone cannot diagnose the condition.

Do not wait for weight loss before investigating sleep. The clinician reviews symptoms, blood pressure, airway and medicines, and may arrange a sleep study. If the diagnosis is confirmed, treatment may include a breathing device or other options according to type and severity, alongside weight management when relevant. Weight loss may help but does not replace prescribed treatment.

Severe choking, blue discolouration, chest pain or difficulty breathing while awake needs urgent care. Bring the description from the person who noticed the pauses, or a brief recording if it was obtained safely; it may help the clinician understand what is happening.

Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad
Ask the doctor

I think my medicine is making me gain weight. Should I stop it?

Do not stop the medicine by yourself. Some medicines can increase appetite, alter fluid balance or reduce activity, but weight gain may have another cause that began at the same time. Abruptly stopping some psychiatric, neurological or steroid treatments and other medicines can be harmful.

Record the medicine, dose and start date, weight or measurements before and after, and any swelling or change in appetite and sleep. The prescriber can balance benefit against the effect and may change dose, timing or the medicine when appropriate. Sometimes continuing treatment with nutrition, movement and monitoring is safer than switching.

Rapid gain with swelling of the legs or abdomen, breathlessness, chest pain or reduced urine needs prompt assessment because it may be fluid rather than fat. Bring a list of every medicine and supplement, including those bought without a prescription.

Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad
Ask the doctor

My weight rose suddenly without a change in my food. Why?

A sudden increase should not automatically be assumed to be body fat. It may reflect a fluid shift, a new medicine, constipation, changed sleep or activity, or a hormonal or medical cause. One reading may also change with weighing time, clothing or a salty meal, so the trend and accompanying symptoms matter more than an isolated number.

Repeat weight under similar conditions and record the time course, swelling, breathlessness, menstrual change, constipation, thirst or altered urination, together with medicines. Examination determines whether targeted tests or simple monitoring are appropriate; there is no single test panel for every gain.

Rapid gain with clear swelling, breathlessness especially at rest or when lying down, chest pain, severe dizziness or reduced urine needs urgent assessment. Do not begin a diuretic or crash diet before establishing the reason.

Answered by

Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad
Ask the doctor

How can I tell whether I have visceral fat around my organs?

The scale and BMI do not directly measure visceral fat. Waist size and fat distribution help estimate risk, but they do not precisely diagnose the amount around each organ. Body-composition devices also provide an estimate influenced by method and hydration, so the number must be read in context.

More useful than chasing one figure is assessing related risk: blood pressure, glucose, blood lipids, family history, movement and sleep. Ultrasound may identify fatty change in the liver or particular abdominal problems, but it is not a complete measure of visceral fat. More detailed imaging is not routinely needed simply because the abdomen is prominent; it is selected when it would change care.

Measure the waist consistently at spaced intervals and review it alongside health and test results. The aim is to reduce risk and improve health, not promise one device number or target fat around a single organ.

Answered by

Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad
Ask the doctor

My daughter fears gaining weight, refuses food and is losing weight. What should I do?

Intense fear of weight gain with food restriction or falling weight can be a sign of an eating disorder and deserves early assessment, even if she believes everything is under control. Do not argue about her appearance, praise the loss or threaten her over food. Say that you are concerned about her health and energy and want to help without blame.

Arrange assessment with a paediatric or adolescent clinician and with mental health and nutrition professionals experienced in eating disorders. Review includes the growth trajectory, pulse, blood pressure, symptoms, eating and psychological behaviour, with tests and treatment chosen by the team. Weight and appearance alone do not establish severity.

Fainting, chest pain, marked palpitations, confusion, dehydration, repeated vomiting, refusing fluids or thoughts of self-harm needs urgent help. Calm family support without punitive surveillance is an important part of safety.

Ask the doctor

How can I tell whether being underweight is inherited or caused by a health problem?

Having always been slim and having relatives with a similar build may support a constitutional pattern, but it does not by itself prove that everything is healthy. What matters is whether weight has followed a stable path, energy is good and intake, growth or strength are appropriate, or whether there is new loss or persistent difficulty gaining.

Unintentional loss, persistent diarrhoea or abdominal pain, vomiting, difficulty swallowing, palpitations and tremor, marked thirst and urination, fever or night sweats, severe fatigue or a clear appetite change deserves medical assessment. The clinician uses the history and examination to select targeted tests; not every slim person needs the same panel.

If assessment is reassuring, a gain plan focuses on adequate energy and protein, meals that fit your appetite, and resistance exercise when appropriate. The aim is gradual health and muscle gain, not simply increasing the scale with random food.

I have started a vegetarian diet. How do I avoid protein and iron deficiency?

A vegetarian diet can be balanced, but planning depends on whether it includes eggs or dairy or is fully plant-based. Spread protein sources such as beans, lentils, chickpeas, soy foods and nuts across the day, with eggs and dairy if they form part of your pattern. Variety over the day matters more than relying on one food.

Absorption of plant iron is influenced by the meal. A vitamin C source with pulses or vegetables can help, while tea or coffee with the meal can reduce absorption. Vitamin B12 needs particular attention in a fully plant-based diet, and a fortified source or supplement should be chosen according to the pattern and assessment.

Do not begin high-dose iron or multiple supplements at random. Persistent fatigue, pallor, breathlessness on effort, tingling or weakness deserves review and appropriate tests. Pregnancy, breastfeeding, growth and chronic illness need more individual planning.

Ask the doctor

I have gestational diabetes. What can I eat without harming the pregnancy?

Gestational diabetes does not mean removing all carbohydrate or severely restricting food. The plan spreads carbohydrate through the day and adjusts its amount and type alongside protein and vegetables according to glucose readings, stage of pregnancy and growth. Juice and sugary drinks can raise glucose quickly, but one universal banned-food list does not fit everyone.

Record glucose at the times set by the obstetric and diabetes team, together with food and symptoms. Gentle movement after a meal may help when the obstetric clinician agrees, and some people need medicine alongside nutrition. Do not change medicine or skip a meal to compensate for a reading without instructions, and do not use prolonged fasting for weight loss during pregnancy.

Vomiting that prevents food or fluid, symptoms of low glucose, repeated readings outside the agreed target, reduced fetal movement or any concerning pregnancy symptom needs prompt contact with the obstetric team. The goal is monitored glucose and adequate nourishment for parent and baby, not weight loss.

Ask the doctor

My measurements are shrinking but the scale is unchanged. Is my plan not working?

Not necessarily. Weight combines fat, muscle, fluid and the contents of the digestive tract, so measurements can improve while the scale stays still for a time. Periods, a salty meal, constipation, travel or starting a new exercise routine can shift fluid temporarily and conceal the direction of body fat. Conversely, a rapid fall is not always a success if some of it is muscle or fluid.

Compare measurements under similar conditions: the same scale, a similar time and comparable clothing. Measure the waist at the same place and point in the breath, rather than once after food and once first thing. Do not punish yourself by suddenly cutting food after one reading or adding punishing exercise. Look at the trend over time, satisfaction after meals, energy, movement and body composition where available.

If neither weight nor measurements change over a reasonable period despite following the plan, review portions, snacks, drinks, sleep, medicines and new symptoms instead of blaming ‘metabolism’. At La Rose, InBody is included in the nutrition consultation and repeated during weekly follow-up, helping distinguish fat, muscle and fluid changes before adjusting the plan. A smaller measurement is useful information; consultation places it alongside the other signals instead of letting the scale erase it.

Ask the doctor

My weight-management injection is causing nausea and constipation. Should I continue or stop?

Do not increase the dose, and do not stop or repeat it on your own. Contact the prescriber before the next dose is due. Nausea and constipation can occur with some weight-management medicines, but their severity, duration and your ability to drink and eat determine what should happen. Severe or persistent abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, marked swelling with no stool or wind, significant dizziness or reduced urine need prompt assessment.

If symptoms are mild, eat a smaller amount slowly and stop at the first comfortable sense of fullness rather than finishing the plate. Very rich meals may worsen nausea for some people. Spread fluids through the day and increase fibre gradually, not suddenly, with gentle movement if your condition allows. Do not add a laxative or anti-sickness medicine at random; the right choice depends on your history and other medicines.

Record the injection time, when symptoms began, episodes of vomiting or bowel movements, and whether you can drink, then share this with your clinician. At La Rose, weekly follow-up reviews symptoms, weight and InBody, and dosing responds to you rather than somebody else's schedule. The safe decision may be to hold a dose, delay an increase, change the plan or stop treatment after assessment.

Ask the doctor

I finished H. pylori treatment but I am still bloated. Should I repeat the antibiotics?

Do not repeat the antibiotics by yourself. Bloating after treatment does not prove that H. pylori remains. The symptom may relate to the bowel, constipation, a temporary change after antibiotics, reflux or another cause. Equally, feeling better does not prove eradication. Confirmation uses an appropriate test at a time and with preparation set by your clinician, because antibiotics and some acid-suppressing medicines can affect the result.

Write down the medicines, doses and duration, whether doses were missed or vomiting occurred, and bring the earlier test. Do not stop acid medicine before testing without clear instructions. For now, smaller meals, slower eating and attention to constipation and repeatable food triggers are more useful than banning a long list at once. Persistent vomiting, unintended weight loss, difficulty swallowing, anaemia, vomiting blood, black stools or severe pain should not wait for a routine test.

Dr Mohab can review whether the first diagnosis used a suitable test, which treatment was taken and whether a test of cure or another investigation is appropriate. Endoscopy is not required for every episode of bloating; it is selected when medically indicated. The goal is to identify the reason for your symptoms and reserve antibiotics for a genuine need, rather than repeat an old course on guesswork.

Ask the doctor

My scan showed fatty liver but my enzymes are normal. Does that mean there is no problem?

Normal liver enzymes are reassuring about one part of the picture, but they do not cancel a fatty-liver finding or establish whether fibrosis is absent. Ultrasound can identify an appearance consistent with fat accumulation, but it cannot accurately grade inflammation or fibrosis in every case. We therefore combine the report with history, examination and other blood results, considering glucose, blood lipids, blood pressure and fat distribution around the middle.

Do not buy a liver ‘detox’ or stop prescribed medicine because you assume it caused the finding. Bring the ultrasound report and images if available, earlier results, and a list of medicines and supplements. Your clinician can decide whether you need monitoring alone, a calculation to estimate fibrosis risk, or another test. Yellow eyes, dark urine, abdominal or leg swelling, vomiting blood, black stools, severe pain or confusion require urgent assessment.

At La Rose, Dr Mohab performs the internal-medicine assessment and abdominal ultrasound himself during the same visit when the case requires it, connecting your symptoms with the image and blood results. If nutrition and weight are part of the cause, care can be coordinated with the nutrition clinic. The aim is neither to frighten you with a phrase in a report nor ignore it, but to understand risk and set out clear steps for your case.

Ask the doctor

Thyroid dose and a weight plateau

No. Do not increase thyroid hormone because the scale is unchanged. The dose is chosen from blood results, symptoms and your clinical situation. Taking more than you need may cause palpitations, tremor, anxiety and other health effects. Thyroid treatment restores hormone to an appropriate level; it is not a weight-loss medicine. Once results are stable, other influences on weight may still remain.

Before deciding that progress has stopped, review how the medicine is taken. Is the timing consistent? Is iron, calcium or a stomach medicine taken near it? Has the brand or dose changed? Do not rearrange or separate medicines by yourself; your clinician or pharmacist should tailor timing to your prescription. Sleep, movement, appetite, periods, constipation and other medicines also matter, as does the trend in weight and body composition rather than one day's reading.

Severe palpitations, chest pain, breathlessness, marked dizziness or new swelling need prompt assessment according to severity. At consultation, Dr Mohab can review thyroid results, dose and the wider history. If hormone levels are stable, nutrition support can then focus on a plan fitted to your needs while following muscle and fat. A still scale is a question to investigate, not a reason to adjust a sensitive medicine yourself.

Ask the doctor

I am breastfeeding, hungry all the time and want to lose weight. What can I do safely?

Hunger during breastfeeding is understandable, especially with broken sleep and meals postponed until everyone else is settled. A harsh diet or removal of all carbohydrate is not the answer; it is more likely to increase fatigue and make consistency difficult. First, check whether meals are regular or whether coffee and quick snacks have quietly replaced most of the day's food.

Create a fixed station near the place where you usually feed, with a water bottle and a satisfying snack: yoghurt, fruit with nuts if suitable, or a small egg or cheese sandwich. Include protein, vegetables or fruit and a carbohydrate source at meals, and try not to wait for extreme hunger. Ask for help preparing two useful components for the week instead of expecting yourself to cook something different every day.

The pace and shape of weight loss depend on time since birth, your health, feeding pattern and medicines. Dizziness, marked fatigue, palpitations, continued bleeding, disabling low mood or anxiety, or concern about the baby's feeding and growth need appropriate clinical review. Dr Alyaa can assess your nutrition, pregnancy and feeding history and daily routine, then agree a realistic goal without deprivation. The priority is nourishment and enough energy for your day, not paying for weight loss with your wellbeing.

Ask the doctor

My child eats very few foods. Should I pressure him or leave him alone?

Neither constant pressure nor leaving the whole day open for snacks is helpful. Pressure and bribery can turn the table into a battle, while grazing all day reduces the chance that a child reaches a meal with natural hunger. Aim for predictable meal and snack times, with water between them, and sit together without a screen whenever possible.

At each meal, offer one food your child accepts alongside a very small amount of something new, without requiring a clean plate. Touching, smelling or licking are stages of familiarity, not failure. Offer the food again on different days in simple forms, and let your child decide how much to eat from what is served. You decide what is served, and when and where. Avoid cooking an immediate replacement after the first refusal, but keep a familiar option on the table.

Growth pattern matters more than comparison with another child. Weight loss, stalled growth, distress during meals, repeated coughing or choking, difficulty swallowing, persistent vomiting, or a rapidly shrinking food list need medical assessment. Dr Alyaa can review growth, diet and the home routine, then build age-appropriate nutrition steps. If a sign needs a paediatric or swallowing assessment, she will direct you to the appropriate clinician.

I am underweight and never seem to gain. Should I take an appetite stimulant?

Ask the doctor

I have PCOS and keep dieting. Why does my weight go down and return?

PCOS can make appetite, glucose regulation and weight management harder for some women, but it does not make weight loss impossible, and regain does not mean you have failed. Many diets produce a quick fall because they are extremely restrictive or remove whole food groups. They are difficult to live with through work, social meals, periods and poor sleep. When pressure rises, the old eating pattern returns and weight follows.

We need to separate the role of PCOS from other factors. Is the diagnosis confirmed? Are periods irregular? Could thyroid disease or a medicine contribute? Do meals keep you satisfied? Was the change fat, or mainly fluid and muscle? Following body composition and waist over time is more informative than comparing two isolated scale readings.

Begin with a change you can repeat: suitable protein at meals, vegetables or fruit, a carbohydrate portion fitted to you, and regular movement with resistance exercise at your own level. Sleep belongs in the plan too. Do not start a PCOS medicine or supplement because it helped a friend. At consultation we review symptoms, tests, InBody and your priorities, referring to gynaecology or internal medicine where needed. The most useful plan is not the fastest one; it is the one you can continue and adjust as life changes.

Ask the doctor

My fasting glucose is normal. Could I still have insulin resistance?

Yes, fasting glucose can remain within the normal range while the body is working harder to keep it there, but insulin resistance cannot be diagnosed from one symptom or from body shape. Hunger soon after eating, strong sweet cravings or sleepiness after meals may guide better questions, yet the same experiences can come from poor sleep, anaemia, stress or an unbalanced meal. Even a fasting insulin result, when requested, should not be read away from glucose, your history and the laboratory method.

A sound assessment starts with family history, changes in weight and waist, periods, sleep, movement and medicines. Your clinician chooses the glucose tests that answer your particular question and may assess thyroid, PCOS or liver factors where the history suggests them. Avoid ordering an internet checklist of tests or removing all carbohydrate because you assume resistance is present.

Until your appointment, build meals around protein, vegetables or fruit and a suitable carbohydrate portion, and note the timing of hunger and sleep for two or three days. At La Rose, we read your tests and history alongside InBody, then decide whether insulin resistance is genuinely part of the picture and what fits your routine. The aim is to understand the cause, not attach one diagnosis to every tired day or weight plateau.
