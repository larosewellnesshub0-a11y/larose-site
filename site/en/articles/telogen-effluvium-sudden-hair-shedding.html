Telogen effluvium: understanding sudden hair shedding

Published: 11 September 2026 8 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

What is telogen effluvium?

Hair does not all grow at once. Each follicle cycles through growth, transition, and rest, after which the resting hair sheds and new growth begins. Telogen effluvium occurs when more follicles than usual move into the resting phase after a change or stress affecting the body. The result is diffuse shedding across the scalp rather than one defined empty area, often noticed as extra hair in the shower, brush, or on the pillow [1, 2, 3].

The word sudden describes when the shedding becomes noticeable, but the follicle change began earlier. There is usually a delay between the trigger and visible shedding because a hair spends time resting before it falls. The latest shampoo or meal is therefore not automatically the cause. Reviewing the preceding months is more useful than focusing only on the first day of shedding [1, 2, 3].

Telogen effluvium is usually nonscarring, so follicle openings remain and the scalp generally looks healthy. It is still a possible pattern, not a diagnosis made from a photograph or a home hair count. It can coexist with hereditary thinning, and other disorders can mimic it. For comparison in men, Hair loss in men: when should you get checked? explains rapid shedding versus gradual pattern loss [1, 3, 12].

Look for a trigger in the months before shedding

Recognized triggers include a feverish illness, severe infection, major operation, physical trauma, childbirth, or intense psychological strain. Rapid weight change, a restrictive diet or low protein intake, and thyroid disorders can also enter the history. Some medicines or a change in hormonal medication may be associated with shedding, but the timing needs review with a clinician [1, 2, 3, 4].

There may not be one dramatic event. Sometimes the body has faced several smaller stresses, or the trigger has resolved and been forgotten by the time shedding begins. Make a simple timeline of illness and fever, surgery, major stress, weight or appetite change, eating pattern, and medicines or supplements started or stopped. This is more useful than trying to count every hair [2, 5].

Psychological stress alone does not prove the diagnosis. It can be associated with telogen effluvium, but it can also accompany hair pulling, alopecia areata, or another pattern. Do not abruptly stop a prescribed medicine because you suspect it; some medicines are essential, and unsupervised withdrawal can be harmful. Ask the prescriber to review timing and alternatives safely [3, 4, 7].

The expected pattern and what does not fit

In telogen effluvium, thinning is usually diffuse and the scalp itself looks healthy, without a marked rash, heavy scale, or sores. A ponytail may feel smaller or the part may look wider, but there should not be one suddenly smooth patch or shiny scarred skin. The hairline usually does not recede in the defined pattern of hereditary loss, although heavy shedding can reveal underlying pattern thinning that was previously subtle [1, 3, 12].

Round patches on the scalp or beard, eyebrow change, or nail pitting may point toward alopecia areata. Spreading scale with broken hairs, redness, swelling, or discharge raises concern for infection or inflammation. Severe itch, burning, pain, or scalp scarring are not minor details; they justify faster dermatology examination [6, 7, 11].

It is also important to distinguish shedding from the root from shaft breakage. A shed hair has completed its cycle, while breakage produces varied lengths and damaged ends and often relates to heat, chemical processing, or traction. Gentle care reduces breakage, but it is not a diagnostic test for telogen effluvium. The clinician combines scalp appearance, hair testing, and the timeline before naming the condition [5, 10].

Examination and targeted testing

The clinician asks about onset, speed, and distribution and reviews preceding health events, eating and weight, menstruation or childbirth where relevant, chronic illness, and every medicine and supplement. Examination covers the scalp, follicle openings, and shaft thickness. A gentle pull test may assess active shedding, while dermoscopic magnification can help distinguish relatively uniform hair from pattern thinning or inflammation [1, 2, 5].

Tests are not automatic for every case. If the story and examination are clear and a trigger is known, a broad list may not be needed. If there is fatigue, pallor, weight change, thyroid symptoms, restricted intake, heavy menstruation, chronic disease, or persistent unexplained shedding, the clinician may select a blood count, iron measures, thyroid tests, or other investigations based on the clinical question. Hair examination or scalp biopsy can occasionally help when the diagnosis remains uncertain [2, 5, 8].

Hair-loss blood tests: what should come first? explains targeted selection instead of random panels. If you take a hair supplement containing biotin, read Biotin before blood tests and tell the clinician and laboratory because biotin can distort some results. Do not use a universal stopping schedule on your own; follow instructions from the service performing the test [5, 8].

Recovery takes time and is not visible all at once

If the diagnosis is truly acute telogen effluvium and the trigger has ended, shedding usually settles with time and growth returns gradually. This does not happen in a week. Follicle cycling is slow, and new hairs need time to lengthen enough to contribute visible density. Short new growth can appear while some shedding is still occurring, so both may overlap for a period [1, 2, 3].

The course varies with persistence of the trigger, an untreated deficiency or illness, and coexisting hereditary thinning. Ongoing or repeated stress on the body can prolong shedding. Loss that continues for many months, absence of any improvement, or a changing pattern should prompt reassessment rather than repeating the same plan indefinitely [1, 2].

Periodic photographs in the same lighting are more useful than checking the mirror every day. Daily hair counting is imprecise and can increase anxiety; the number seen on wash day depends on wash frequency and hair length. A clinician follows the trend, scalp condition, part width, and returning density. No oil or mask can guarantee a faster follicle cycle, and promises of instant regrowth are unrealistic [1, 3, 9].

Support the body and hair without overdoing it

The foundation is addressing a confirmed trigger or cause: recovery from illness, correction of a demonstrated deficiency, management of thyroid disease, or review of a medicine with its prescriber. Eat adequate, varied meals with protein and energy appropriate for your situation. If you are losing weight within a medical plan, shedding does not mean stopping the plan yourself; discuss the rate of loss, appetite, and nutrient coverage with the treating team. Hair loss during medical weight loss explores this point in detail [1, 2, 9].

Do not start iron, zinc, or hair vitamins in random high doses. A deficiency should be corrected when present, but excess amounts of some nutrients can cause harm or worsen shedding. Biotin is not a general treatment for every case and can interfere with important laboratory tests. A clinician or nutrition professional can decide whether a true need exists and how response should be followed [5, 6, 10].

Use gentle shampoo and conditioner, comb carefully, and reduce high heat, chemical processing, and traction. Washing does not pull healthy hair out of its follicle; it releases hairs that have completed their cycle, so less frequent washing may make a larger amount appear at once. Cutting the hair may simplify care but does not change the follicle cycle. Psychological support matters if shedding is affecting confidence or sleep [3, 10].

When to get checked and when care should be faster

Book an assessment if shedding is heavy, persistent, distressing, or has no clear trigger. Seek an earlier appointment for bald patches, eyebrow or beard loss, marked pain or burning, heavy scale, redness, discharge, or shiny scarred skin. These do not fit uncomplicated telogen effluvium, and some causes need early treatment to protect the scalp and follicles [5, 6, 7, 11].

Medical review is also important for severe fatigue, pallor, dizziness, breathlessness, palpitations, unintended weight loss, thyroid symptoms, or a new systemic illness. If fever accompanies rapidly spreading scalp redness, swelling, or discharge, seek urgent care because infection is possible. Do not assume every additional sign belongs to telogen effluvium merely because shedding followed stress [1, 7, 8].

At La Rose Clinics, you can request an initial medical assessment to review health history, eating pattern, weight, medicines, and the need for dermatology, internal medicine, or nutrition input. Bring a supplement list, older photographs, and an event timeline. This page is educational and cannot replace scalp examination; Dr Shimaa's topic review should be complemented by a dermatologist's reading of an individual case when skin signs are present [5, 9].
