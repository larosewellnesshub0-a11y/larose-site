I have an event coming up. What is the fastest safe way to lose weight?

Published: 7 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

The short answer: there is no fast, safe number for everyone

If the event is only days or two weeks away, there is no guaranteed way to lose a large amount of body fat safely. A commonly cited gradual range for adults is roughly half to one kilogram per week, and people who lose at a steady pace are more likely to maintain it. This is a general range, not a prescription or promise: starting point, age, medicines, activity, sleep and health conditions all change what is realistic [1, 2].

A more practical pre-event goal is smaller: begin habits you can continue, reduce things that worsen your own bloating or fluid retention, and protect your energy rather than arriving exhausted or dehydrated. Even a modest loss, or stable weight with clothes feeling more comfortable, may be a reasonable outcome. Do not compare yourself with another person's photograph or story, and do not turn the event date into a test of your worth. A safe programme should fit your health, include balanced food and feasible movement, and have a maintenance plan; it is not merely a race with the scale [1, 2].

Rapid loss for a genuine medical reason, such as preparation for a procedure, is a different situation that requires clinical assessment and follow-up. NICE restricts very-low-energy diets to a specific, clinically assessed need within a specialist service, not a dress, photograph or social deadline [3].

A fast scale change often reflects fluid, not fat alone

Your weight at any moment combines fat, muscle, fluid and the contents of your digestive tract. During the first days of changing food intake, especially after a sudden carbohydrate reduction, glycogen stores and their associated water may fall and create a rapid scale change. A scientific review of obesity treatments notes that much of the early difference seen with some lower-carbohydrate diets reflects glycogen and water, after which the rate slows. The number is therefore not all body fat, and part of it can return when eating patterns normalise [9].

High sodium intake can also increase temporary fluid retention and bloating, so reasonably reducing very salty and highly processed foods may help you feel and look more comfortable. That does not mean eliminating salt or restricting fluids; the aim is a balanced pattern, not drying out the body. The American Heart Association links excess sodium with water retention, bloating and temporary weight gain [11].

Follow the trend rather than one day's reading. Weigh under broadly similar conditions if monitoring does not make you anxious, and notice clothing fit, energy and strength too. Body composition is not the scale explains how to distinguish trends in fat, muscle and fluid without giving one number more meaning than it deserves [9, 11].

A simple plan for the time before the event

Start by organising the day rather than deleting food. Keep meals reasonably regular for your schedule and include a protein source such as eggs, yoghurt, pulses, fish or poultry, alongside vegetables or fruit and a suitable carbohydrate source. Replacing sugary drinks with water or an unsweetened drink and reducing highly processed meals are clearer steps than a long ban list. Health guidance supports a varied pattern containing vegetables, fruit, whole grains and protein sources while limiting added sugar, saturated fat and sodium [4].

Do not introduce unfamiliar foods or supplements just before the event. If a particular food reliably causes bloating, reduce its portion temporarily without removing an entire food group. Eat unhurriedly, leave a sensible interval between a large meal and bedtime, and plan for busy days so extreme hunger does not lead to chaotic eating. Record a few days of food, drinks, sleep and activity; simple tracking can reveal your pattern and help you choose two or three realistic changes instead of ten decisions you cannot sustain [1, 2].

Drink according to thirst and your usual needs, adjusting appropriately for heat or activity. Dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth or passing less urine are not signs of success. If you have heart or kidney failure, or a clinician has set fluid or sodium limits, follow that advice because general guidance cannot replace your medical plan [14].

Shortcuts that look fast but do not safely reduce body fat

Avoid laxatives, diuretics, self-induced vomiting, prolonged sauna use, heat suits or fluid restriction to change the scale. These alter fluid or bowel contents, not the amount of stored body fat. Laxatives can cause dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, blockage, weakness and abnormal heart rhythm; using them without a medical indication is not a weight-loss method [12]. Programmes promising a dramatic loss in a very short period are also a warning sign, not proof of effectiveness [2].

Severely restricting food or living on juice or one food can reduce energy, protein and micronutrient intake, worsen headache, fatigue and constipation, and leave hunger rebounding after the event. NICE says low- and very-low-energy diets must be nutritionally complete, time limited and delivered with specialist support and follow-up; medicines and possible eating disorders should be reviewed before starting [3].

Rapid loss also carries risks beyond the event day. The US NIDDK explains that losing weight quickly or going for long periods without eating may raise the chance of gallstones and impair normal gallbladder emptying; repeated loss and regain is also linked with gallstone problems [5]. If a plan cannot reasonably continue after the event, it is unlikely to be what your body needs before it.

Movement and sleep can improve the outcome without punishment

Walking or another moderate activity you can do safely supports health and energy, but do not suddenly try to exercise away food with punishing sessions. Long-term adult guidance aims for 150 minutes of moderate activity each week plus muscle strengthening on two or more days, but the starting point should progress according to fitness, joints, heart health and chronic conditions [4, 7]. If you have been inactive, a 10- or 15-minute walk may be a better beginning than a severe class that causes injury before the event.

During an energy deficit, muscle needs reasons to remain: adequate food, suitable protein and repeatable resistance work. A systematic review found that interventions containing exercise showed the most consistent benefit for preserving fat-free mass during weight loss, with adequate protein also important [10]. How to protect muscle while losing weight covers the detail without turning this page into a personal exercise prescription or protein calculation [10].

Sleep is not time lost from the plan. Adults aged 18 to 60 generally need at least seven hours, and sufficient sleep supports mood, attention and metabolic health. Keep sleep and wake times as regular as possible, and avoid a large meal, late caffeine and screens close to bedtime. A better night's sleep before the event is more useful than staying up for a punishment workout [1, 8].

Who should have the plan clinically reviewed before starting?

Check with your clinician before a major change if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, heart, kidney or liver disease, gallstones, or medicines that may be affected by food or fluid intake. The same applies during pregnancy or breastfeeding, at younger or older ages, with low body weight, before surgery, or with an eating-disorder history. Changing meal timing or carbohydrate intake can, for example, require adjustment of diabetes treatment to avoid low glucose; that should not be done by trial and error [2, 3, 5].

Weight-management injections or tablets are not cosmetic fixes to begin for an event. Choosing medication is a decision between you and a clinician after assessing weight, risk and related conditions, and medicine forms part of an eating, activity and follow-up programme. Never take another person's medicine, increase a dose, or stop current treatment yourself to move the scale faster [6].

If weight is rising suddenly without explanation, or you have swelling, breathlessness, palpitations, severe weakness, marked menstrual changes or thyroid-type symptoms, the priority is to understand the cause rather than assume starvation is the answer. A medicine list, history of earlier attempts, and two ordinary days of eating and sleep help the clinician make an appropriate decision. A coming event can prompt you to begin health care, but it is not a reason to bypass assessment [2, 6].

Warning signs that mean stop the plan and seek help

Stop the plan and seek urgent assessment for fainting or confusion, chest pain, breathlessness, a fast or irregular heartbeat, severe weakness, persistent vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, or markedly reduced urination. Ongoing dizziness with dark urine and a dry mouth may indicate dehydration that needs replacement and assessment, not successful loss. Severe dehydration can present with confusion or a rapid pulse and needs urgent help [14].

Take it seriously if you become afraid of every meal, eat very little, make yourself vomit, use laxatives, exercise excessively, or find that the scale controls your day and mood. These behaviours may be part of an eating disorder, a mental health condition that deserves specialist support and can affect a person at any weight or appearance [13].

If there are thoughts of self-harm or immediate danger, contact local emergency services now and ask a trusted person to stay with you. If the signs are not an emergency but keep recurring, speak with a doctor or mental health professional soon. The event will pass; your safety and relationship with food and your body matter more than any clothing size or photograph [3, 13].

The event day and the next step

On the event day, eat ordinary meals you know are comfortable for you, drink normally, and do not experiment with prolonged fasting, supplements or a new workout. If you will stand or move for hours, carry water and a suitable meal or snack instead of entering the day without energy. Choose comfortable clothing that fits now; comfort and confidence do not have to wait for a scale number. After the event, return to your routine from the next meal without punishment or compensation [1, 4].

If you want to turn temporary motivation into health care, a first weight-management visit can organise your health history, medicines, tests, eating, activity and sleep, then set a suitable goal without rapid promises. Follow-up helps you choose specific steps and review them instead of repeatedly trying conflicting plans alone [1, 2].

At the clinic, assessment starts with understanding your health and goal, and the final decision is made with the clinician or nutrition professional according to your needs, not the event date alone. You can request a weight-management appointment if you need an individual assessment and a plan that continues after the big day. Until then, do not change prescribed medicine or begin a severe diet on your own [2, 3, 6].
