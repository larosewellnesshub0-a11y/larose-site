My ultrasound found a gallstone, but I only have bloating. Is the stone the cause?

Published: 7 September 2026 7 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

Short answer: the stone and the bloating may be unrelated

Often, no. Gallstones are common, and most people who have them never develop symptoms; many stones are found by chance on an ultrasound done for another reason. [1] When gallstones do cause symptoms, the typical complaint is pain, not bloating on its own. [1, 2] Bloating, fullness and belching are common digestive complaints with many possible explanations, so finding a stone does not prove that the stone is responsible. [11]

UK guidance from NICE advises that people with stones in a normal gallbladder and normal bile ducts, and no symptoms, can be reassured that they do not need treatment unless symptoms develop. [10] So the useful question is not “is there a stone?” but “does my pattern of symptoms match what gallstones actually cause?” The rest of this answer walks through that comparison, so that you arrive at the appointment with the details a surgeon or gastroenterologist needs to decide whether the stone matters in your case.

This page is general information. It cannot examine you or read your scan, and it does not replace a consultation. [1, 10]

What gallstone pain usually feels like

The symptom most closely linked to gallstones is biliary pain, sometimes called biliary colic. It happens when a stone blocks the outlet of the gallbladder or a bile duct for a while. [1] It is usually a steady, severe pain in the upper right part of the abdomen or in the centre just below the breastbone. It may spread to the back or to the right shoulder blade, and it can come with nausea or vomiting. [1, 2] The pain typically builds, lasts from a short while up to several hours and then settles. It often comes as separate attacks with pain-free periods in between, and many people notice it after a large or fatty meal, sometimes at night. [1, 2]

Compare this with bloating: a feeling of tightness, fullness or a swollen abdomen, often with gas, that may come and go through the day and ease after passing wind or opening the bowels. [11] Bloating can be uncomfortable, but a vague, mild fullness that lasts all day without distinct attacks of pain is not the usual gallstone picture. [1, 11]

Write down what you actually feel. Is there real pain, or mainly pressure and swelling? Where exactly is it? How long does an episode last: minutes, hours or all day? Does it wake you from sleep? Is there vomiting? These answers help the clinician decide far more than the ultrasound image alone. [2]

Why bloating on its own needs a wider look

Bloating and gas are among the most common digestive complaints. Swallowed air, foods that ferment in the gut, constipation and conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome are frequent causes, and some people are simply more sensitive to normal amounts of gas. [11] Reflux and indigestion can also cause upper-abdominal fullness and belching. Because these problems are common, and gallstones are common too, the two often turn up together by coincidence. [1, 11]

This is why a clinician will ask about the whole pattern: how bloating relates to meals and particular foods, your bowel habit and any change in it, heartburn, the medicines and supplements you take, and whether anything has changed recently. [11] Some changes deserve prompt attention whatever the ultrasound shows, such as unexplained weight loss, persistent vomiting, blood in the stool or black stools, or yellowing of the eyes. [1, 11] Mention these at the start of the visit.

If the bloating turns out to have another cause, treating that cause is more likely to help than removing a gallbladder that was not producing the symptom. [10, 11]

What the assessment may include

Assessment starts with the history and examination, then goes back to the ultrasound report itself. The report may describe the number and size of the stones, the thickness of the gallbladder wall and the width of the common bile duct; these details help show whether the stones are sitting quietly or have caused inflammation or a blockage. [1, 10] Blood tests, including liver function tests, are often part of this step. [10]

NICE recommends liver function tests and ultrasound for people with suspected gallstone disease. If the ultrasound does not show a stone in the bile duct but the duct is widened or the liver tests are abnormal, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), a specialised MRI scan of the bile ducts, may be considered. [10] If the story points elsewhere, for example to reflux, indigestion or a bowel problem, the next test may be entirely different, or no further test may be needed. [1, 11]

Ask what question each test is meant to answer. Normal blood tests, a stone on ultrasound and no typical pain usually point toward observation rather than an operation, but the decision belongs to the clinician who has examined you. [10]

If the stone is silent: observe and know the signs

When stones are found in a normal gallbladder and normal bile ducts and cause no symptoms, NICE advises reassurance rather than treatment. [10] NIDDK also notes that silent gallstones usually do not need treatment. [1] Observation does not mean ignoring the stone: it means knowing which symptoms would change the plan and returning if they appear.

The plan changes if you start to have attacks of typical biliary pain, or signs of a complication such as inflammation of the gallbladder, a stone in the bile duct or pancreatitis. [1, 10] Keep a copy of the ultrasound report with your records, so that any future doctor can see when the stone was first found.

In the meantime, do not start medicines, herbal “stone dissolvers” or strict diets based on online advice. Medicines that dissolve stones exist, but they suit only some types of stone, take a long time and the stones can return, so they are a decision for your doctor rather than a self-treatment. [1] If you take regular medicines or supplements, review them with your doctor or pharmacist rather than stopping them on your own. [1, 2]

When surgery is discussed: what it can and cannot fix

For people whose gallstones cause symptoms, the usual treatment is removal of the gallbladder, most often by keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery. [3, 4, 10] You can live normally without a gallbladder, because bile still flows from the liver into the intestine. [4, 8]

The key point for this question is matching the operation to the symptom. The operation removes the source of the stones; it cannot be expected to cure bloating that comes from something else. The NHS notes that some people continue to have symptoms such as pain, indigestion or diarrhoea after gallbladder removal. [7] This is one reason a surgeon wants to be confident that your symptoms really come from the gallbladder before recommending surgery.

If surgery is recommended, the team should explain why, what they expect it to improve, the risks, the alternatives and how preparation and recovery will work. [5, 6, 9] Ask directly: “Which of my symptoms do you expect this to fix, and which may stay?” A clear answer helps you make an informed decision.

Warning signs: when not to wait

Seek urgent medical care, without waiting for a routine appointment, if you develop abdominal pain that is severe, lasts several hours or keeps getting worse; fever or shivering with abdominal pain; yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes; dark, tea-coloured urine or pale, clay-coloured stools; or repeated vomiting, or being unable to keep fluids down. [1, 2]

These can be signs of a gallstone complication such as inflammation of the gallbladder, a blocked bile duct, infection or pancreatitis, which need prompt assessment. [1, 2] If you are unsure whether a change is urgent, seek medical advice rather than trying to decide from an article.

Preparing for the appointment

Bring the ultrasound report itself (and the images if you have them), any recent blood results and a list of your medicines and supplements. [1, 10] A simple diary kept for one or two weeks helps a great deal: note when bloating or pain happens, how long it lasts, what you ate beforehand, your bowel habit and anything that relieved it. [11] Tell the doctor about pregnancy, diabetes, blood thinners and previous abdominal operations, because they can change which tests and treatments are suitable. [5]

For related reading, see our guide to gallstone symptoms and surgery, when bloating needs a doctor, and what an abdominal ultrasound can and cannot show. If you would like an individual assessment, you can book an appointment. [1, 2]

Frequently asked questions

Not necessarily. Surgery is aimed at symptoms that come from gallstones, mainly biliary pain and complications. If the bloating has another cause it may continue after the operation, and the NHS notes that some people have ongoing digestive symptoms after gallbladder removal. [7, 10] Ask your surgeon which symptoms the operation is expected to help.

For stones in a normal gallbladder and bile ducts that cause no symptoms, NICE advises that treatment is not needed unless symptoms develop. [10] What matters is knowing the warning signs, such as severe or prolonged pain, fever, yellow eyes or skin and dark urine, and seeking care if they appear. [1]

Gallstone pain is usually a steady, severe pain in the upper right or upper middle abdomen that comes in attacks lasting minutes to hours, sometimes spreading to the back or right shoulder, often with nausea. [1, 2] Bloating is more a feeling of fullness, pressure or swelling that often varies with meals and gas. [11]

It depends on your symptoms and results. If the bile duct looks widened or liver tests are abnormal, NICE suggests an MRCP scan may be considered to look for a stone in the duct. [10] If the pattern points to another digestive cause, different tests, or none, may be more useful. [11]

Bring a short timeline of when the symptoms started and what makes them better or worse, a list of your medicines and supplements (especially blood thinners), and any previous operation reports, imaging or recent test results. This helps the clinician focus the history and examination and decide whether imaging is actually needed. [1, 10]

Go urgently if you have severe or prolonged abdominal pain, fever or shivering, yellowing of the skin or eyes, dark urine or pale stools, or vomiting that will not stop. [1, 2]

Most people go home on the day of the operation, and someone should stay with you for the first 24 hours. You may be able to return to work after 1 to 2 weeks depending on your job, and you should not drive until you can do an emergency stop without discomfort. [6]

Eat a normal, healthy diet; you may find small meals easier for the first few days. [6]
