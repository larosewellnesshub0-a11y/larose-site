Does everyone need eight glasses of water a day? The medical facts

Published: 7 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

Dr Shimaa Fouad answers: where did the 8-glass rule come from?

There is no single amount of water that suits every person every day. The rule of eight glasses a day is a simple guide, not a fixed medical requirement, and it was not based on a trial showing that everyone needs that exact amount of plain water. Reviews of the evidence find no reason for a healthy adult in a mild climate with light activity to force eight glasses of plain water regardless of thirst, food, and other drinks [1, 2, 3].

The rule is often linked to older nutrition guidance that estimated total water needs from calorie intake. An important part was easily missed: total water includes water from food and other drinks, not only glasses of plain water [1, 2, 6]. Hormones, the brain's thirst system, and the kidneys work together to adjust water and salts as needs change, although this system can be less reliable in older people or during illness [1, 2, 6, 12].

Hydration does not need to become a bottle-counting contest. For most healthy adults, a practical approach is to notice thirst, meals, activity, and the weather, then spread drinks through the day without forcing large amounts [1, 3, 4]. People with some medical conditions need individual advice, as explained below.

Why daily fluid requirements vary and cannot be a single fixed number

Fluid needs change from one day to another and from one person to another. Body size and muscle mass affect the amount of water held in the body's tissues. Someone who is larger or has more muscle and is physically active may need more fluid than someone with a smaller body or a quiet routine [2, 3, 6]. This is one reason a single target cannot describe everyone.

Weather and activity also matter. Heat and humidity increase sweating, while the body also loses water through breathing. Work at a desk and physical work outdoors can therefore lead to very different losses [2, 3, 4, 11]. Clothing, exercise length, and an individual's sweat rate can change needs further, so the same person may drink quite different amounts on two different days.

Fever, diarrhoea, and vomiting can quickly remove both water and salts. Replacement needs to match the loss, and severe symptoms may need oral rehydration solution or medical advice rather than plain water alone [3, 4, 5, 11]. Pregnancy and breastfeeding can also raise fluid needs because water supports pregnancy tissues and milk production [3, 4, 5, 11].

What counts toward your daily fluid intake? More than plain water

Daily fluid comes from more than tap or bottled water. Research on ordinary eating patterns suggests that food often provides about 20% to 30% of total water intake, although the share varies with what a person eats [2, 4, 6]. The rest can come from plain water and other drinks.

Fruit and vegetables such as cucumber, lettuce, tomatoes, watermelon, and oranges contain a lot of water as well as nutrients. Soups, yoghurt, and milk also add fluid [2, 4, 6]. They are part of the daily total, but the overall diet still matters; a salty soup or a sugary dessert is not nutritionally the same as plain water.

Tea, coffee, and unsweetened herbal drinks also count. Caffeine can briefly increase urine output in some people, especially those who do not use it often, but a moderate cup still provides fluid [2, 4, 6]. Sugary soft drinks and packaged juices also contain water, yet frequent use adds sugar and calories, so they are not the best main source of hydration [2, 4, 10].

Your body's signals: thirst, urine colour and InBody assessment

Instead of counting cups alone, use several practical clues. Thirst is the main signal that tells a healthy adult to drink when the blood becomes more concentrated. For many healthy adults, responding to thirst is a reasonable guide, but it may be less dependable in older age, illness, pregnancy, heavy exercise, or very hot weather [1, 2, 7, 12].

Urine colour can offer a rough clue, not a diagnosis. Pale yellow often goes with adequate hydration, while darker yellow urine may suggest that more fluid is needed. Medicines, vitamins, foods, and some illnesses can also change the colour or smell [3, 4, 5, 11]. Clear urine once in a while is not a problem, but water-clear urine throughout the day may mean intake is more than the body needs.

Hydration can also affect bioelectrical body-composition tests. Changes in body water alter electrical resistance and can shift the estimated muscle and fat readings, so follow the stated InBody preparation instructions [3, 5, 11]. Dry mouth, tiredness, and lightheadedness can occur with dehydration, but they have other causes too. Persistent or severe symptoms need assessment rather than relying on urine colour alone.

Kidney and metabolic health: when fluid volume truly matters

The kidneys remove wastes, help control blood pressure, and keep water and minerals in balance. When intake falls, they make urine more concentrated to save water. More concentrated urine can make it easier for stone-forming substances such as calcium, oxalate, and uric acid to form crystals [2, 6, 9, 12].

For people who form kidney stones, enough fluid is an important part of prevention. Guidance often uses a urine-output goal of about 2 to 2.5 litres a day, which may require drinking more than that because some water is lost in sweat and breathing [6, 9, 11]. The right plan can differ with the stone type, climate, medical history, and medicines. Our guide on kidney stones and symptoms explains prevention and the warning signs of renal colic that need prompt assessment.

Research is also studying links between long-term low fluid intake, vasopressin, kidney health, blood pressure, and metabolic health. These studies suggest possible associations, but they do not show that forcing extra water prevents every kidney or metabolic problem [2, 6, 9, 10]. Adequate fluid may help prevent constipation, while drinking beyond the body's needs does not keep adding benefit.

Overhydration and water intoxication: the risks of forced drinking

Drinking a very large amount of plain water in a short time can dilute sodium in the blood and cause hyponatraemia, sometimes called water intoxication [1, 7, 8, 12]. The often-quoted figure that healthy kidneys can clear roughly 800 to 1,000 millilitres an hour is only an estimate, not a safe hourly target. Capacity changes with body size, kidney function, medicines, exercise, and other factors, so forcing water close to that rate can still be unsafe [1, 7, 8, 12].

Sodium helps control the movement of water between the blood and cells. Hyponatraemia is generally defined as blood sodium below 135 mmol/L. If sodium falls quickly, water can move into cells; swelling in the brain may cause headache, nausea, vomiting, confusion, seizures, or loss of consciousness [7, 8, 12]. These symptoms after heavy water intake need same-day urgent medical care, and some people need hospital admission.

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia is a particular risk during long endurance events when someone drinks beyond thirst and gains water weight. It can also occur with extreme “detox” routines or certain illnesses and medicines [7, 8]. For many endurance settings, consensus guidance recommends drinking to thirst rather than following a forced schedule, while allowing for individual medical and event advice.

Medical conditions requiring fluid limits: heart, kidneys and liver

Advice to drink a lot of water does not suit everyone. Some people with heart failure need a fluid limit because the heart cannot move blood efficiently and extra fluid can add to breathlessness or swelling [6, 8, 12]. A sudden increase in breathlessness, swelling, or rapid weight gain needs medical advice.

Advanced kidney disease and liver cirrhosis with fluid in the abdomen can also cause water and salt retention. Forcing extra water may worsen fluid overload and can lead to hospital treatment [6, 8, 11, 12]. A clinician may set a daily allowance that includes water, tea, soup, and other drinks. The limit is individual; it should not be copied from another patient's plan.

Some psychiatric medicines, water tablets (diuretics), and blood-pressure medicines also change how the kidneys handle water and sodium [5, 6, 8, 12]. If you have heart, kidney, or liver disease, follow the fluid advice from your treating team rather than increasing intake on your own. New shortness of breath, confusion, or sudden swelling needs same-day medical advice, and severe breathing difficulty needs emergency care.

Structuring daily fluid intake and sensible next steps

A simple routine is usually enough: keep water available, drink when thirsty, and add fluids during hot weather or exercise. Fruit, vegetables, soups, milk, tea, and coffee can all contribute, depending on the rest of the diet [1, 3, 4, 6]. There is no need to start every day with a prescribed glass or to drink between meals unless that helps you personally.

If you have weight, blood-sugar, or other health goals, a therapeutic nutrition visit can help review your meals, activity, medicines, and medical conditions. The clinician can suggest a practical fluid range rather than claim to calculate one exact requirement from body composition alone [2, 3, 6, 10]. People with heart, kidney, or liver disease should bring the fluid instructions from their treating doctor.

Persistent intense thirst, frequent urination, repeated night-time urination, unexplained weight loss, dizziness, or swelling deserves medical assessment. These symptoms can have several causes, including high blood sugar, kidney problems, medicines, or hormone conditions [5, 8, 11, 12]. Large amounts of caffeine may cause other problems, so this does not mean intake is unlimited.

There is no good evidence that drinking litres of water on an empty stomach burns fat from the abdomen or “detoxes” the body. The liver and kidneys normally process and remove many waste products [1, 2, 6, 10]. A morning glass is fine if you enjoy it, but it does not replace a balanced eating plan or evidence-based weight care.

Thirst sensation and the kidneys' ability to conserve water can become less reliable with age. Medicines, memory problems, mobility limits, and illness can add to the risk [2, 5, 6, 11]. Regular access to drinks and reminders may help, but anyone with a prescribed fluid limit needs to follow their clinician's plan.
