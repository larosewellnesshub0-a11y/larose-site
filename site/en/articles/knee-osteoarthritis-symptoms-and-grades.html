Knee osteoarthritis: symptoms and X-ray grades

What knee osteoarthritis really is

Knee osteoarthritis is a chronic condition in which the tissues of the knee joint change gradually. It is not simply bone rubbing on bone, and it is not an inevitable result of getting older. The cartilage covering the ends of the bones can thin and change, but osteoarthritis also involves the bone beneath the cartilage, the joint lining, ligaments, tendons and the meniscus. Small bony growths called osteophytes can form, and the shape and movement of the joint may change over time [1, 3].

Symptoms and structural changes usually develop slowly, but their pace and impact differ greatly from one person to another. Some people have marked changes on an X-ray with little pain, while others have substantial pain with limited radiographic change. A clinician therefore does not diagnose or plan care from the image alone. The symptom history, examination, effect on walking, sleep and work, and the person's priorities all matter alongside any imaging [2, 13].

It is also important to separate knee osteoarthritis from knee pain in general. Tendon problems, meniscal injury, gout, rheumatoid arthritis, infection, or pain referred from the hip or back can produce overlapping symptoms. Pain that appears suddenly, or a knee that quickly becomes hot and swollen, is not the usual pattern of slowly developing osteoarthritis and needs a different assessment [1, 6].

Knee osteoarthritis symptoms and early clues

The most common symptom is knee pain during movement or weight-bearing. It may first appear when climbing or descending stairs, walking farther than usual, rising from a low chair, squatting, or standing for a long time. Early pain may settle with rest and return with a particular activity. As the condition progresses, pain can continue after activity or disturb sleep. New, severe or persistent night pain still deserves assessment because the clinician may need to exclude another cause [1, 5, 11].

Stiffness after waking or after sitting for a long time is another common clue. In the typical osteoarthritis pattern, morning stiffness is brief, usually lasting no more than 30 minutes, and gradually eases with movement. A person may also notice reduced range of motion, difficulty fully straightening or bending the knee, mild swelling after activity, a sense that the knee is unstable or may give way, and weakness in the thigh muscles as activity declines. Cracking or grating can occur, but noise alone, without pain, swelling or impaired function, is not enough to establish osteoarthritis [1, 2, 5].

Early symptoms do not have to appear together or remain constant every day. Quiet periods can alternate with symptom flares after increased activity, a minor injury, or a change in the load placed on the joint. The pattern matters: is pain related to movement, is it limiting walking or stair use, how long does stiffness last, and is there swelling or instability? Recording those details for two weeks can give a clinician more useful information than the word “pain” on its own [2, 5, 10].

Risk factors in women and younger adults

There is no single cause behind every case. Age increases risk, but osteoarthritis does not mean a knee is “finished” simply because a person is older. Women are more likely than men to develop osteoarthritis, particularly after age 50, although the full reason for this difference is not known. Joint anatomy, hormones, muscle strength, previous injury and body weight may interact, so knee osteoarthritis in women should not be reduced to one hormonal explanation, and not every pain after menopause is osteoarthritis [1, 4, 10].

Overweight and obesity raise the risk of knee osteoarthritis by increasing mechanical load on a weight-bearing joint, while metabolic and inflammatory effects of fat tissue may also contribute. An earlier anterior cruciate ligament or meniscal injury, previous knee surgery, differences in joint shape or alignment, family history, and work or sport involving repeated bending, squatting or loading can also increase risk. Having a risk factor does not prove osteoarthritis, and having none does not rule it out [1, 4, 10].

In younger adults, knee pain has many possible causes other than osteoarthritis. Early osteoarthritis can nevertheless develop after a major or repeated injury, with unusual joint structure, or with certain inherited factors. The clinician will ask about sports injuries, accidents, operations, the exact site of pain, true locking and swelling. Labelling a younger person from a crackling sound or an image that does not match the symptoms can create anxiety and lead to inappropriate care, so clinical assessment is especially important [1, 4, 9].

How clinicians distinguish osteoarthritis from other knee pain

Diagnosis starts with conversation and examination, not an MRI for every patient. The clinician asks when pain began, whether onset was gradual or sudden, how it relates to movement and rest, how long morning stiffness lasts, whether there is swelling or heat, whether an injury occurred, and how the problem affects sleep, walking and work. Examination may cover gait, knee alignment and shape, swelling, areas of tenderness, bending and straightening, ligament stability, and the strength of muscles around the joint [1, 2, 11].

Under NICE guidance, a person aged 45 or over with activity-related joint pain and either no morning stiffness or stiffness lasting no longer than 30 minutes can receive a clinical diagnosis without routine imaging when there are no atypical features. That age-and-symptom pattern is not a final home test; the clinician still considers the whole picture and excludes other possibilities. Prolonged morning stiffness, prominent swelling in several joints, systemic symptoms, or an inflammatory pattern may lead to a different investigation [2].

Blood tests do not confirm knee osteoarthritis. A clinician may request them when the history or examination suggests rheumatoid arthritis, gout, infection, or another condition that needs exclusion. Vitamin D deficiency may coexist with general pain or weakness, but it does not automatically explain localised knee pain or prove osteoarthritis. The purpose of assessment is not merely to give a name to an X-ray; it is to identify the likely source of symptoms and understand their effect on the person [2, 3, 11].

When is a knee X-ray or MRI needed?

An X-ray is not routinely necessary when the clinical pattern is typical. It does not show cartilage directly, and it may reveal changes in someone without pain or fail to explain substantial symptoms. Guidelines advise against routine imaging simply to confirm osteoarthritis in a typical presentation and against repeated imaging to monitor nonsurgical care. Imaging becomes useful when symptoms or age are atypical, after injury, with progressive deformity, when the diagnosis is uncertain, or when the result will change a clinical decision such as surgical planning [2, 9, 13].

When a clinician requests radiographs, weight-bearing knee views can show the apparent space between bones, osteophytes, increased density of bone beneath cartilage, and changes in the shape of the bone ends. Positioning matters, and more than one view may be needed to assess the different compartments of the knee. The report should be interpreted beside the symptoms and examination rather than as a separate verdict [7, 9, 12].

MRI is not an automatic next step and is not required merely to “see the cartilage better” in every patient. A clinician may request it when another soft-tissue problem is suspected, when persistent pain remains unexplained after assessment and radiographs, or before a specific decision. Repeating scans just to learn whether a number has changed is unlikely to help when the result would not alter the care plan [2, 9].

Kellgren–Lawrence knee osteoarthritis grades 1 to 4

The Kellgren–Lawrence system describes the severity of changes visible on a plain radiograph from grade 0 to grade 4. Grade 0 means there are no radiographic features of osteoarthritis. Grade 1 means doubtful joint-space narrowing with possible early osteophyte formation, so it is described as doubtful change. Grade 2 shows a definite osteophyte and may include possible joint-space narrowing; research often uses this point as the threshold for radiographic osteoarthritis [7, 12].

Grade 3 describes multiple moderate osteophytes and definite joint-space narrowing, with possible increased density of bone beneath cartilage and possible early deformity of the bone ends. Grade 4 describes large osteophytes, marked joint-space narrowing, severe subchondral sclerosis and definite deformity of the bone ends. These terms describe an image, not the person's experience, and they do not mean every knee will inevitably pass through each grade at the same rate [7].

Most importantly, the grade cannot be read in isolation. A person with grade 2 changes may hurt more than someone with grade 3 because symptoms can reflect the joint lining, underlying bone, muscles, sleep, nervous-system sensitivity, or another coexisting problem. Conversely, a high radiographic grade can produce fewer symptoms than expected. Scientific reviews confirm that pain and radiographic change are related but do not match perfectly, especially at milder grades. Treatment decisions should therefore reflect symptoms, function and preferences, not the X-ray number alone [13].

A hot, swollen knee: when is it urgent?

Knee osteoarthritis can cause mild swelling or a sense of warmth after increased activity, but a clearly hot, red or rapidly swollen knee that is extremely painful or difficult to move should not automatically be called an “osteoarthritis flare.” If these signs occur with fever, shivering, or feeling generally unwell, seek urgent same-day medical assessment. Joint infection is an important possibility that must be excluded promptly. Gout or another crystal arthritis can produce a similar picture, and a clinician determines the appropriate tests [6].

After a fall or injury, inability to stand or bear weight, visible deformity, very severe pain, tingling, or loss of sensation around the joint are reasons to attend emergency care. True locking, where the knee remains stuck and cannot straighten, particularly after injury, also needs prompt assessment. Swelling of the whole leg, calf pain, or shortness of breath are not ordinary osteoarthritis symptoms and require urgent help according to severity [6].

Even without an emergency, arrange an appointment if pain or swelling is worsening or recurring, affects sleep or normal activity, or persists despite simple measures for two weeks. Assessment is also important when morning stiffness lasts longer than 30 minutes, several joints are swollen, or there is unexplained weight loss, because that pattern may require investigation for another diagnosis [2, 6].

After diagnosis: how to use the result

Knee osteoarthritis has no treatment that fully restores changed cartilage to its original state, but that does not mean nothing can be done. Realistic goals are to reduce pain, improve movement and strength, maintain activity, and review the plan when symptoms change. Guidelines place appropriate therapeutic exercise, support with weight management when relevant, and understanding the condition among the foundations of care. A clinician then selects further options according to other health conditions, risks and preferences. Surgery is not an automatic consequence of one X-ray grade; it is considered when symptoms, function and quality of life are substantially affected and nonsurgical care is unsuitable or insufficient [2, 8].

Do not stop moving completely because of the word “osteoarthritis,” and do not start a pain medicine, injection or supplement solely from an X-ray grade without reviewing your individual situation. The type and dose of activity and any medicine need to fit your age, health conditions, current medicines and the true source of pain. This article is intentionally about recognising symptoms and understanding grades; the detailed treatment ladder and comparison of options need their own assessment so an image number does not become a generic prescription [2, 8].
