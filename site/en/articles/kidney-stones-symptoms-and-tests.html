Kidney stone symptoms, scans and warning signs

Published: 7 September 2026 10 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Kidney stone symptoms: the pattern matters, not one symptom

A kidney stone can remain silent inside the kidney. Symptoms often begin when it moves or enters the ureter, the tube carrying urine to the bladder. A typical pattern is intense pain in the side or back below the ribs that may travel into the lower abdomen or groin, come in waves and vary in severity. Nausea or vomiting, burning during urination, urgency, passing small amounts, and pink, red or brown urine may accompany it. Not everyone has every symptom, and pain intensity does not reliably measure stone size [1, 2, 3, 4].

These symptoms are not unique to stones. Urinary or kidney infection, digestive or muscular problems, and some surgical or pregnancy-related conditions can produce similar pain. Blood in urine also has several causes. Pain location or urine colour cannot establish the diagnosis, especially when a new episode differs from an old one [1, 3, 5].

When describing the episode, say when pain began, whether it travels or comes in waves, and whether fever, chills, vomiting or urinary change is present. Also mention prior stones, a single kidney, kidney disease, possible pregnancy, digestive-tract surgery, and new medicines or supplements. These details help the clinician judge whether a stone is likely, which test fits the question and how urgently assessment is needed [2, 5, 6].

When do possible stone symptoms need urgent assessment?

Do not wait for a routine appointment when flank pain is severe and not controlled by the prescribed plan, or when there is fever or chills, repeated vomiting that prevents fluids, obvious deterioration, confusion or fainting. Pain with possible infection and obstruction needs a prompt decision because drainage and treatment may matter more than waiting for a stone to pass. Difficulty passing urine, a marked reduction in urine or inability to urinate, particularly with pain, also needs urgent assessment [1, 4, 6, 7].

Visible blood in urine deserves prompt medical contact and should not automatically be attributed to a stone. Priority is higher with one kidney, known kidney disease, reduced immunity, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, or pain that is entirely different from earlier episodes. Pregnancy changes both imaging and medicine choices, so do not arrange imaging independently or delay review while attempting a home diagnosis [2, 5, 6].

If you are unstable, follow local emergency-service instructions and do not drive yourself. An old normal ultrasound does not guarantee that the current situation is minor; a report answers a question at a particular time. Conversely, severe pain alone does not prove dangerous obstruction. Urgent assessment combines vital signs, examination, urine and blood testing, and imaging when indicated to distinguish these possibilities [5, 6, 7].

Urine and blood tests: what do they answer?

Assessment begins with the history and examination, not an image alone. A clinician may request urinalysis to look for blood, white cells or other signs of infection, crystals, and other information relevant to the situation. No blood on testing does not conclusively exclude a stone, while blood does not prove one. If infection is suspected, urine culture can help identify bacteria and guide treatment, but an urgent decision should not wait for the final result when the person is clinically unwell [1, 5, 6].

Blood tests may include kidney function and markers of infection, with electrolytes or other targeted tests according to the history and examination. During an acute episode, the purpose is to assess impact and identify infection, dehydration or another disturbance that changes management, not to perform every stone-formation test at once. A previously normal creatinine result does not replace current assessment if urine output falls or vomiting continues [5, 6, 8].

If a stone passes, keep it in an appropriate container when the clinician asks because laboratory analysis of its composition can guide prevention. Recurrent stones or particular risk factors may lead to targeted blood and urine testing, sometimes including a 24-hour urine collection. That is a different stage from managing acute pain or excluding infection and obstruction, so the priority and sequence are individual decisions [6, 8, 9].

Ultrasound or CT: why does the choice differ?

Ultrasound uses no ionising radiation and may show selected stones, urinary-tract dilatation or indirect evidence of obstruction. It can nevertheless miss a small stone or one in part of the ureter, and image quality is affected by technical and anatomical factors. A normal ultrasound therefore does not always end the assessment when symptoms and examination remain concerning [5, 6, 11].

Non-contrast CT usually provides more precise detail about a stone's presence, location and size, and may reveal another cause of pain, but it uses radiation. The need for CT depends on age, pregnancy, severity, alternative diagnoses, prior imaging and whether the result will change management. A large randomised emergency-department trial found that starting with ultrasound reduced cumulative radiation exposure without significantly changing major clinical outcomes compared with starting with CT [6, 11].

Do not request a test because one is described as universally “stronger.” Ask which question needs answering: Are we looking for a stone, obstruction, infection or an alternative cause, and will the result change the next action? The abdominal and pelvic ultrasound guide explains the wider capabilities and limits of ultrasound without suggesting that every episode of flank pain should follow the same pathway [5, 6].

How should an imaging result be read without false reassurance or alarm?

An imaging report must be interpreted in the context of why the examination was requested. Stone location and size matter, but they are not the whole decision. The clinician also considers dilatation or obstruction, evidence of infection, kidney function, symptom severity and the other kidney. A stone found incidentally within a kidney may not explain the current pain, while evidence of obstruction with a matching presentation may change the speed of action [5, 6, 8].

If the report says that no stone is visible or that ultrasound is normal, ask whether the ureter was adequately seen and whether the original question was answered. The guide to a normal ultrasound with continuing pain explains why a reassuring result does not cancel a persistent or changing symptom. This does not mean CT is automatically required; it means the clinician reviews probability, examination quality and warning features before choosing observation or another test [5, 6].

Bring the report and images when available, along with prior imaging for comparison. Do not rely on a measurement taken from a phone image or a non-clinical interpretation, and do not start an antibiotic or a stone-passage medicine from an old prescription. Ask directly whether there is obstruction, whether infection is possible, whether the stone explains the symptoms and which change should bring you back early. Those answers matter more than memorising terminology [5, 6, 8].

After a stone is found: observation still needs a plan

Not every stone needs a procedure, and not every stone is suitable for waiting at home. The likelihood and plan depend on size and location, pain control, the ability to drink, obstruction or infection, kidney function and individual health circumstances. If home observation is appropriate, you should know the prescribed medicine and how to use it, the review date, whether urine should be strained to collect the stone, and which changes require an immediate return [6, 7, 8].

Do not try to force a stone through by suddenly drinking an excessive amount, particularly if you are vomiting or have heart- or kidney-related fluid restrictions. Drink according to the advice given for your situation and use only prescribed pain relief or other medicines. If pain becomes stronger, fever appears, urine output falls or fluids cannot be kept down, the old plan is no longer sufficient and the need for assessment changes [1, 7, 8].

The separate answer to can a small kidney stone pass at home provides a concise discussion so this article does not become a treatment manual. The central point is that “small” is not itself a management decision and safe observation includes a defined review point and a way to establish whether the stone has passed or remains clinically important. Any medicine or procedure remains an individual decision with the treating clinician [6, 8].

Prevention starts with stone type and individual circumstances

After the acute episode settles, ask whether stone passage or kidney function needs confirmation and whether the stone is available for analysis. Recurrent stones, multiple stones, a young age at the first episode or particular medical circumstances may lead the clinician to propose a wider evaluation. The aim is to base prevention on the likely cause and stone type rather than a universal restriction list found online [6, 8, 9].

Adequate fluid intake is commonly central to prevention, but the suitable amount varies with climate, activity, heart or kidney disease and clinical advice. Food changes also depend on stone type; sodium, animal protein, oxalate and dietary calcium are not altered in the same way for everyone. Do not remove dairy or an entire food group, and do not start supplements or preventive medicine independently, because random restriction can cause harm without preventing the stone type you have [8, 9, 10].

Write down questions for follow-up: the stone type if known, urine and blood results, the appropriate fluid target, and whether a 24-hour urine collection or follow-up imaging is needed. A realistic plan uses sustainable steps and a review point; it does not promise that a stone can never recur. If warning features occur during any new episode, return to the urgent-assessment pathway rather than relying only on the prevention plan [6, 8, 9].

The next step: prepare the information before review

Before review, bring prior imaging reports and images, urine and blood results, and a list of medicines and supplements. Record when pain began, its site and movement, fever, vomiting, urine appearance and output, and which treatment you used and what happened. If a stone passed and you retained it, ask the medical facility how to submit it for analysis. This information helps the clinician distinguish follow-up of a stable episode from a problem needing faster imaging or referral [5, 6, 8].

At the clinic, medical assessment can connect symptoms with examination and laboratory results, then decide whether ultrasound is sufficient, CT or another test is needed, or urology referral is appropriate. The clinic is mentioned here only as an organisational step, not as a promise that one test will explain every pain or that every stone will pass without intervention. The final decision remains with the treating clinician after reviewing the full situation [5, 6].

If there are no warning features and you need to begin a structured assessment, you can use the request for a medical consultation and state that the question concerns kidney-stone symptoms or an ultrasound or CT result. If fever accompanies pain, vomiting is persistent, urine output falls, there is clear deterioration or pain is uncontrolled, do not wait for a clinic appointment; follow the urgent-care pathway appropriate to your location [1, 6, 7].
