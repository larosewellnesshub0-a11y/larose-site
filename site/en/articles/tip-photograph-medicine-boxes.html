Photograph medicine boxes before your visit

Published: 1 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

A photograph is an excellent start, but not the whole record

When someone asks during a visit, “Which medicines do you take?”, it is easy to forget a name, remember only the colour of a box, or say “the small blood-pressure tablet” without the active ingredient or strength. Photographing packages before the appointment preserves useful label information, especially if you use several products or see more than one clinician. A photograph alone, however, may not show how many tablets you actually take, at what time, why the medicine was prescribed, or that you stopped it last week. Photos should therefore support an up-to-date written list, not replace it. The FDA advises including prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and supplements, together with each name, strength, purpose and dosing instructions [1].

An accurate list helps clinicians and pharmacists compare what you really use with prescriptions and new decisions. That process is medication reconciliation: building the best possible medication history, confirming it with the person or reliable sources, and making any intended addition, discontinuation or difference clear [2, 3]. Photos are a useful visual reference, but no photograph can decide whether two medicines are safe together or whether an old dose is still appropriate. That decision remains with a clinician or pharmacist who knows your conditions, test results, symptoms, allergies and previous adverse effects [6, 9, 10].

Exactly what should you photograph?

The day before the visit, gather the products you currently use and make one clearly labelled album. For each box or bottle, photograph the front and the back or side showing the active ingredient, strength, directions and visible warnings. If the pharmacy label carries your name and prescribed directions, capture it without cropping important information. Photograph the blister strip when its name, strength or appearance differs from the outer box, and photograph the inner supplement container if that is where the ingredients appear. An over-the-counter Drug Facts label identifies the active ingredient, purpose, warnings, directions and storage information. Similar brand names can also cover products with different ingredients, so the label needs to be checked each time [7].

Use good light, hold the camera straight and make sure the text remains readable when enlarged. Review each image before putting the packages away, and retake blurred photographs. If the packaging is not in Arabic or English, keep the original image rather than trying to translate the ingredient yourself. Supplements matter as much as medicines: the biotin before blood tests page explains why the product front, ingredient panel and dose can matter before some tests. The FDA warns that biotin in certain supplements can interfere with some laboratory methods and produce incorrect results, so tell the clinician and laboratory instead of choosing a stopping interval yourself [11].

Write down what the camera cannot see

Beside each photograph, write the medicine name if clear, its strength, the dose you actually take, how often you take it, and its timing around meals or sleep when that is part of the instructions. Add why you use it, who prescribed it if known, and when you started. For an “as needed” medicine, record what you use it for, when you last took it and roughly how often you used it over the last week or month as appropriate. If you miss doses, split a tablet, or struggle with an inhaler or injection, say so honestly; that information is more useful than presenting a perfect-looking record [1, 4, 5].

Keep a separate part for medicines stopped recently, including who advised the change and when, because an old package at home does not prove the medicine remains in the plan. Add an allergy or previous-reaction section: name the product, describe what happened and give the approximate date. “Allergy” is not a synonym for every side effect. Drug reactions can be adverse effects, interactions or true allergies, and describing the event helps the team distinguish them [10]. Record important conditions and pregnancy or pregnancy planning when relevant, because some interactions involve a health condition rather than two medicines alone [10]. If you do not know a detail, write “not sure” instead of guessing and ask the clinician or pharmacist to check it with you.

Include the products people often forget to call medicines

A complete record is not limited to prescription tablets. Add pain relievers, cold and allergy products, antacids, laxatives and anything bought without a prescription. Include vitamins, herbs, protein or other supplements, especially products marketed for hair, weight loss or sport. Add insulin and weekly injections, inhalers, eye and nasal drops, creams, patches and medicines used intermittently. The FDA and NCCIH stress that clinicians and pharmacists need to know about prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, herbs and supplements because interactions can change effectiveness or increase adverse effects [5, 6, 8, 10].

Do not stop at descriptions such as “hair vitamin,” “painkiller when needed” or “diabetes injection.” The name, ingredient, strength and timing matter. Check the active ingredient in combination cold or pain products. Photographing the label can help the team spot when two products contain the same ingredient, but it is not an instruction to diagnose duplication or stop either product yourself [7, 9]. If you live with diabetes, the whole-health diabetes review explains why medicine names, doses, timing, missed doses and possible low-glucose episodes belong to the wider clinical picture rather than being minor details. If a product is not yet being taken but is part of a written plan to start later, place it under “not started yet” so it is not mistakenly recorded as current.

Update the record after every change or hospital discharge

The most important quality of a medication list is that it is current. Revise it whenever a medicine starts, a dose changes, treatment stops, or the package and strength change. Put a “last updated” date at the top. After an emergency visit, hospital admission, operation or transfer between clinicians, compare the discharge document with the old list item by item with a clinician or pharmacist: What continues? What stopped? What is new? Which change is temporary? The World Health Organization identifies transitions between care settings as an important point for medication discrepancies and supports an updated list with patient and family involvement [2]. AHRQ likewise describes reconciliation as comparing home medicines with admission and discharge orders, documenting changes and communicating them to the next provider [3].

Do not merge every old list into one document without distinguishing current from previous treatment. Keep a dated archive if useful, but have one clearly marked “current” copy. If several clinicians prescribe for you, show each of them that same copy. When blood-pressure follow-up is part of the visit, the blood-pressure pattern guide explains the value of recording dose timing with readings and symptoms without changing a dose because of one isolated number. A structured review does not imply that every medicine is wrong; it makes sure any difference is known and intentional rather than caused by an old document or incomplete memory [2, 3, 9].

Keep the record available without broadcasting private details

Keep the list and photographs somewhere you can open quickly, and maintain a paper copy or backup in case the phone is lost or has no power. The FDA and Joint Commission note that a current list can help in an emergency and that sharing a copy with a trusted person can be useful if you cannot communicate for yourself [1, 4]. Choose someone who knows which copy is current and update their copy when the plan changes. Pharmacy labels may display your name, record number or other personal data, however, so send images to the care team through the method the clinic specifies and do not post them in a public group or on social media.

Before a laboratory test or procedure, show the list to the clinician and laboratory and ask whether any medicine or supplement needs specific instructions. Do not stop prescribed treatment or delay an urgent test because of an online post [5, 6, 11]. In an emergency, do not spend time arranging an album: contact the appropriate emergency service immediately for difficulty breathing, rapidly developing swelling of the face or tongue, fainting, severe confusion, a seizure, severe chest pain or sudden deterioration after a medicine. Take the package or images if that is safe and readily possible, but do not delay care to collect them. A photograph helps the team identify a product; it does not replace urgent assessment or provide a way to treat a severe reaction at home [1, 10].

Turn the photographs into useful questions at the visit

Prepare your questions before the appointment: Is this medicine still needed? Is this dose appropriate for my current kidney or liver function? What should I do if I miss a dose? Should it be taken with food? Does it require a test or monitoring? Could a new medicine interact with anything on the list? The FDA suggests asking about purpose, method and duration of use, missed doses, foods, drinks or supplements to avoid, adverse effects and monitoring [5]. A pharmacist can explain the label, administration and interactions, while a clinician connects that information with diagnosis, examination and results [6].

Record what is making the plan difficult: dizziness, sleepiness, a rash, stomach upset, trouble swallowing, difficulty opening the container, or a schedule that conflicts with work or fasting. Do not stop or reduce treatment solely because you are worried about an adverse effect; discuss the timing, severity and whether it began after a particular change [8, 9, 10]. If the question concerns long-term acid treatment, the long-term acid-reflux medicine article explains why the original reason, dose, response and warning signs matter more than duration alone. At the end of the visit, ask for a clear copy of the plan: what continues, what changed and whom to contact if a new symptom occurs. Update your list immediately, before the details are forgotten [3, 5].

Your next step: one list and a clear review

Start today with one simple step: take a readable photograph of every current product and make a table with its name, strength, dose, timing and purpose, then clearly mark anything stopped or taken only when needed. Review the list with a trusted person if you help an older relative, while involving the person taking the treatment as much as possible. Paper lists, a phone and electronic forms are all reasonable choices; the best system is the one you will actually update and can reach when needed [1, 4]. However organised the record becomes, it is a tool for discussion—not a new prescription or permission to alter treatment [3, 9].

If medicines come from several clinicians, you have recently left hospital, or you are unsure which version of the plan is current, bring the photographs, prescriptions and discharge document to a review. At the clinic, an internal-medicine clinician can review names and doses alongside conditions, symptoms and results, then identify questions for a pharmacist or the specialty that prescribed the treatment. You can use the request an internal-medicine appointment page to begin a structured review, but seek urgent care instead of waiting for a routine visit if a severe warning sign or sudden deterioration occurs. The aim is one understandable record, with every change intentional, documented and agreed [2, 3].
