Healthy weight gain: should you take an appetite stimulant?

Published: 27 July 2026 9 min read Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

The short answer: do not start an appetite stimulant before understanding why

If your weight is low and does not rise however much you feel you eat, the short answer is: do not start an appetite stimulant on your own. A scale number is not a diagnosis, and body mass index is only an initial screening tool interpreted alongside weight history, strength, symptoms, actual intake, age and health. Guidelines use a BMI below 18.5 as one criterion for possible malnutrition in adults, but that does not automatically mean every person below that value is ill or needs medicine [3, 4].

The key distinction is between somebody who has had a slim, stable build for years and somebody who has recently lost weight without trying or noticed an appetite change. It also matters whether portions are small, fullness comes unusually early, eating causes pain or nausea, swallowing is difficult, or energy needs are higher because of activity or illness. That pattern guides the next step; another person's experience with a pharmacy product does not [1, 2, 4].

Healthy weight gain is not simply the fastest rise in fat or the largest scale number. The aim is enough energy and nutrients, better strength and function, and no important symptom being hidden. When low weight is stable and there are no concerning symptoms, a gradual food plan is often the starting point. When loss is new or there are signs of illness, identifying and treating the cause comes first [2, 3].

When does low weight need medical assessment?

Unintentional weight loss, especially when ongoing or noticeable, needs medical review rather than an attempt to cover it with more calories. Seek assessment when it comes with persistent diarrhoea, greasy stools or abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, painful or difficult swallowing, blood in vomit or stool, fever or night sweats, a new lump, marked fatigue, or worsening weakness. Unplanned loss can occur with digestive and absorption disorders such as coeliac disease, and with conditions including hyperthyroidism or diabetes. Accompanying symptoms guide examination and testing; everybody does not need the same panel [2, 5, 6, 7].

Palpitations, tremor and heat intolerance alongside loss deserve thyroid review, while increased thirst and urination with fatigue or loss may prompt diabetes assessment. Persistent diarrhoea, bloating, anaemia or a characteristic rash may lead a clinician to consider malabsorption. These examples guide assessment; they are not a way to diagnose yourself, because each symptom has many possible explanations [5, 6, 7].

Seek urgent care if you cannot keep fluids down or develop dehydration, fainting, bloody vomit, black stool, severe pain, confusion, or rapid deterioration in eating and mobility. Ask for early specialist help when fear of weight gain, self-induced vomiting, compulsive exercise or food restriction is the barrier; eating-disorder guidance stresses that assessment must not rely on BMI alone [13]. For a fuller discussion without duplicating it here, read how to tell whether being underweight is inherited or caused by a health problem.

How can you add energy without turning the day into a battle with food?

Once assessment is reassuring, gaining weight requires more energy coming in than the body uses, but a gradual approach is usually easier to sustain. The NHS suggests that adults may try adding roughly 300 to 500 calories a day and adjust according to response; this is a general range, not a fixed prescription for every person. When appetite is small, five or six smaller eating opportunities may be more manageable than three very large plates [1].

Start with something repeatable: add olive oil or tahini to a meal, use full-fat yoghurt or cheese when suitable, include nuts or nut butter when there is no allergy, or place a small sandwich between meals. Try nourishing drinks between meals if drinking before food fills you up, and include both energy and a protein source at each eating opportunity. The short guide enrich one meal instead of enlarging the day offers one practical first step; the broader task here is building a complete pattern you can continue [1, 3].

Adding energy does not mean relying all day on sweets and sugary drinks. Variety still matters for fibre, vitamins, minerals and the quality of dietary fats, even when weight gain is the goal [10]. If you live with diabetes, kidney disease, allergy, swallowing difficulty or digestive symptoms, fortification and drink choices need individual adjustment. Guidelines include fortified food, snacks, nourishing drinks and oral supplements within nutrition support, but the choice follows assessment of needs and ability to eat [3].

Make the gain support muscle and strength

Calories matter, but protein and movement influence the type of gain and how the body functions. Include an appropriate protein source in meals and snacks: eggs, milk, yoghurt or cheese; fish, poultry or meat; or pulses and soy according to preference and tolerance. The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand explains that adequate protein alongside resistance exercise supports muscle building and training adaptation, while needs vary with activity, age, health and total energy intake [8]. A single number found online therefore cannot suit everybody, particularly when kidney disease or another condition changes requirements.

Resistance activity may use free weights, machines, bands or body-weight movements. The World Health Organization recommends muscle-strengthening activity involving the major muscle groups on two or more days each week as part of adults' overall activity, but the starting point should reflect fitness, dizziness, weakness and chronic illness [9]. If you are inactive or weak, begin more gently and with suitable guidance rather than suddenly pushing hard.

Follow practical abilities such as climbing stairs, rising from a chair, carrying what you need and progressing in exercise, not only the scale. The guide to muscle assessment in nutrition care explains why strength, body composition and clinical context belong together, preventing the scale rise from becoming the only goal. Sleep and rest also support recovery, while training beyond what food and recovery can sustain may use up the surplus you are trying to create [8, 9].

Appetite stimulants and supplements: when might they be part of a plan?

The term “appetite stimulant” covers different medicines and products, and they do not share the same evidence, use or safety profile. A systematic review of cyproheptadine found weight gain in varied populations, but the studies were highly heterogeneous and sedation was the most commonly reported adverse effect; those findings do not make it an automatic choice for every adult with low weight [11]. Drug references also describe sedating and anticholinergic effects, interactions and contraindications that require clinical review, so do not start it or combine it with other products without a clinician who knows your medicines and health [12].

The useful question is not “Which stimulant is strongest?” but “What is limiting eating or gain?” Treating dental pain, reflux, constipation, nausea, a mood disorder, another medicine's effect or a swallowing problem may help more than trying to force appetite. A clinician may sometimes select a medicine for a specific situation after weighing benefits and harms, but that is an individual treatment decision rather than a promised outcome [2, 3, 11].

Oral nutrition drinks or supplements are different from appetite-stimulating medicines. They can help when ordinary fortified food is insufficient or chewing and portion size are barriers, but guidelines place them inside a complete nutrition plan with goals and review, not as an indefinitely unmonitored substitute [3]. Choose them with a professional if you have diabetes, kidney disease, allergy or malabsorption. A “natural” label on any product does not guarantee safety or freedom from interactions.

Follow the trend and adjust instead of judging one day

Weigh under broadly similar conditions at sensible intervals, perhaps weekly if monitoring does not increase anxiety, and look at the direction across several weeks. Weight changes from day to day because of fluid, salt and bowel contents, so one reading cannot show success or failure. Also record how many eating opportunities you managed, early fullness, nausea or diarrhoea, energy and strength. That information shows whether to add a small step, alter the timing of drinks, or address a symptom that limits intake [1, 3].

If the trend remains flat despite genuine implementation, do not assume the answer is doubling every portion. Review measurements, consistency and symptoms with a professional: needs may be higher than expected, a medical or absorption problem may be present, or the plan may simply be impractical. If weight rises rapidly alongside leg swelling, breathlessness or sudden puffiness, the change may be fluid rather than nutritional gain and needs medical assessment.

Define success more broadly than weight: a better appetite, more regular meals, improved exercise ability, greater strength, better sleep, or the return of periods when they were absent and other causes have been medically reviewed. If weighing or food tracking increases fear or compulsive behaviour, stop that monitoring method and seek support; guidance considers behaviour and psychological and physical symptoms together rather than relying on BMI alone [13]. Regular review lets the plan change safely with the body's response [3].

The next step: a plan fitted to the reason for low weight and your life

Before the visit, note the direction and timing of weight change, two ordinary days of food and drink, symptoms, medicines and supplements, and previous attempts. Do not artificially change your eating for the record; an accurate picture is more useful. Assessment at the clinic reviews whether low weight is longstanding or new, what limits appetite or absorption, and whether examination or targeted tests are indicated. It can then define a workable food plan, a review goal, and the role of activity or a supplement when appropriate [2, 3].

A good plan begins with one clear, manageable step, such as a consistent addition or a new eating opportunity, and a review point to learn what helped and what caused difficulty. No single menu or appetite stimulant suits everybody, and rapid promises are inappropriate. If you have a warning sign described above, choose urgent care or the relevant specialty rather than waiting for a routine nutrition appointment. If your situation is stable and you want a structured assessment, you can book a clinical nutrition consultation to discuss the reason, your goal and the safest next step [1, 3].
