Abdominal and pelvic ultrasound: what does it show?

Published: 11 September 2026 10 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Abdominal and pelvic ultrasound is not one picture of everything

When a clinician requests an abdominal and pelvic ultrasound, the aim is not a universal screening examination that guarantees every disease is absent. Ultrasound uses sound waves to create real-time images of tissues suited to the technique, without the ionising radiation used in X-rays or CT. Its value begins with a focused question: Are gallstones present? Are the bile ducts dilated? Is there urinary retention or kidney dilatation? Is there pelvic fluid or a mass? That question determines the required areas, preparation and meaning of the result [1, 7, 10].

The abdominal component may include the liver, gallbladder, bile ducts, kidneys, spleen and pancreas with varying visibility, as well as the aorta and other large vessels when requested. The pelvic component commonly focuses on the bladder and may assess the uterus and ovaries or the prostate and nearby structures according to a person's anatomy, symptoms and examination technique. The words “abdomen and pelvis” on a request do not mean every organ was seen equally well, nor that ultrasound can answer questions about the lining of the stomach and bowel or every cause of pain [2, 5, 6].

To make the examination useful, ask beforehand what question it is intended to answer and whether it is comprehensive or targeted to one organ. Afterwards, ask what was seen adequately and what had limited visibility. This makes the report one part of an integrated assessment rather than a list of conditions from which to choose your own diagnosis [1, 2, 11].

What can an abdominal ultrasound show?

Abdominal ultrasound is highly useful when the question suits the technique. It may show gallstones, gallbladder-wall changes or surrounding fluid in the appropriate context, dilated bile ducts, differences in liver or spleen size and appearance, kidney cysts, dilatation of the urinary collecting system, some stones, free fluid and certain masses. Doppler can add information about the direction or speed of blood flow in selected vessels when requested. The ability to display a finding does not mean that finding necessarily causes a person's symptom [2, 5, 8, 10].

For example, ultrasound is a leading test for detecting gallstones, but a report of silent stones does not by itself prove that every episode of bloating or heartburn comes from the gallbladder. The pain pattern, examination and laboratory results still need to fit. A report may also describe an appearance consistent with fatty liver, but ultrasound alone cannot determine the cause of fat, microscopic inflammation or the stage of fibrosis. The fatty liver on ultrasound guide addresses that question without turning this general imaging article into a complete liver-treatment guide [8, 9].

A report may also mention an incidental finding that was not the reason for the scan, such as a simple cyst or an anatomical variant. “Incidental” does not mean dangerous, and it does not mean irrelevant. Useful questions are whether the finding explains the symptom, whether its features are reassuring and whether it needs defined follow-up. The answer comes from the report's description, clinical history, examination and relevant tests, not from one short label alone [2, 3, 5].

Pelvic ultrasound findings depend on the examination route

Pelvic ultrasound can assess the bladder before and after urination for selected indications and look for urinary retention or visible bladder-wall changes. In someone with a uterus and ovaries, it can help assess size and shape, fibroids, some ovarian cysts or masses, and endometrial thickness when that measurement is appropriate to the clinical context. In someone with a prostate, it may estimate prostate size and residual urine, but it does not automatically replace clinical assessment or tests selected by the treating clinician [4, 6].

There is an important difference between an external transabdominal examination and an internal transvaginal or transrectal examination. The external route commonly uses a full bladder as an acoustic window to display deeper pelvic organs. An internal route places the probe closer to pelvic structures and may provide different detail, but it is not required for everyone and should not proceed without explanation and consent. You can ask why that route is proposed, what alternatives exist and what will happen, and you can ask for the examination to stop at any time. Privacy and an appropriate chaperone follow the imaging facility's policy [1, 4, 6].

If pregnancy is possible and there is lower abdominal pain or bleeding, state this clearly before the examination because the route, timing and interpretation may change. A normal pelvic scan also does not exclude every cause of pelvic pain; some situations need clinical examination, a pregnancy or urine test, or different targeted imaging. The treating clinician connects the result with the pain location, menstrual history, urination and accompanying symptoms [4, 6, 11].

Ultrasound limits: why a normal result may not end an assessment

Sound does not travel readily through bowel gas, so gas may obscure the pancreas or other structures and create shadowing and artefacts. Tissue depth, movement, pain that prevents probe pressure, an inadequately filled bladder and eating before an examination that requires fasting can all reduce image quality. A responsible report may state that visualisation was “limited” or that an organ was “not fully seen.” This is not a frightening diagnosis, but it is information to clarify: Which area was limited, and was the original question answered? [5, 11, 12].

Ultrasound is also not endoscopy. It does not display the lining of the stomach or colon in the same way, does not diagnose reflux or irritable bowel syndrome from an image and cannot exclude every inflammatory, early-stage, muscular or nerve-related cause of pain. Some problems are better assessed with blood or urine tests, endoscopy, CT or MRI. Selection depends on the clinical question and warning features, not on one test being universally stronger than another [1, 3, 11].

When ultrasound is normal but pain remains, this does not mean the pain is imagined or the report is wrong. It means the organs adequately visualised and the question for which the examination was requested did not reveal a clear explanation. The guide to a normal ultrasound with continuing pain explains the next step in detail. Return to the clinician if the symptom persists or changes, taking the report and images and noting the pain's site and relationship to food, bowel movements or urination [1, 5, 11].

How do you prepare, and what happens during the scan?

Preparation depends on the organ and clinical question. Examination of the gallbladder and upper abdomen may require fasting so the gallbladder is distended and the effects of food and gas are reduced, while some external pelvic examinations require a full bladder. If the request combines abdomen and pelvis, instructions may involve both fasting and water at specified times. Do not rely on a generic online schedule because duration and water volume vary by protocol and individual circumstances. Follow the imaging facility's instructions and ask if the written directions are unclear [1, 2, 5, 6, 11].

Do not independently stop an essential medicine or change insulin or diabetes treatment to accommodate fasting. If you have diabetes, difficulty fasting, vomiting, heart- or kidney-related fluid restrictions, or possible pregnancy, contact the facility or treating clinician before the appointment for suitable instructions. The abdominal ultrasound preparation card gathers practical questions to ask but does not replace your own booking directions [1, 2, 5].

During an external scan, you will usually lie down while gel is placed on the skin and a transducer is moved over the requested area. The examiner may ask you to turn onto one side, breathe in deeply or hold your breath briefly, or use the toilet between parts of the examination. Pressure can be uncomfortable over a tender area, so say if it becomes painful. After the scan, wipe away the gel and ask when the report will be ready and who will review it with you. Some results need a follow-up appointment even though imaging itself is complete [1, 2, 10].

How should you read an ultrasound report without panic or false reassurance?

Start with the indication, then the descriptive findings, and finally the conclusion or impression. The indication states the question to be answered; the findings record what was seen; the impression summarises the main imaging meaning. Reading one isolated word from the findings while ignoring the impression may magnify a minor detail. Reading “no acute abnormality” as “everything is normal” can create false reassurance. The report is interpreted with symptoms, examination and laboratory results and does not replace them [1, 2, 10].

Phrases such as “clinical correlation is advised” mean the image needs to be connected with your clinical situation; they do not mean the report avoided an answer. “Limited visualisation due to bowel gas” describes a technical constraint, not a new disease. If “follow-up is recommended,” clarify the method, interval and clinician responsible. Do not repeat ultrasound or arrange different imaging independently, because the choice depends on the medical probability and whether a result would change management [3, 5, 11, 12].

Before review, note what has changed since the scan was requested and bring the images if available, previous reports and a list of medicines and supplements. Ask three direct questions: Does this result explain my complaint? Is there a finding that needs follow-up even if it does not cause the symptom? What is the next step if symptoms continue? If recurrent bloating is the main issue, the persistent bloating guide explains when a separate digestive assessment is more useful than relying on ultrasound alone [1, 3, 12].

When should you not wait for a routine ultrasound appointment?

Ultrasound is valuable, but it is not a gateway that must be completed before urgent care. Seek urgent assessment for sudden severe pain or pain that is rapidly worsening, a very tender or rigid abdomen, fainting or severe dizziness, breathlessness, persistent vomiting that prevents fluids, vomiting blood, black or bloody stool, obvious jaundice, fever with general deterioration, or painful distension with complete inability to pass stool and gas. These features require a prompt clinical decision and may need blood tests or different imaging according to the situation [3, 11].

If pregnancy is possible with pelvic pain or bleeding, do not wait for a routine scan without medical direction; some causes are time-sensitive and need an urgent assessment pathway. Heavy bleeding, sudden severe pelvic pain, marked weakness or fainting also require urgent care. Do not drive yourself if you are unstable, and follow the instructions of local emergency services [4, 6, 11].

Other features are not necessarily emergencies but warrant an early appointment: persistent or recurrent pain, unintentional weight loss, new early fullness after eating, repeated vomiting, a lasting change in bowel movements or urination, a palpable lump, or symptoms that return despite a reassuring result. Urgency depends on severity, speed of onset and your overall condition. If uncertain, contact a clinician or urgent medical service rather than trying to interpret the report alone [1, 3, 11].

The next step: connect the image with the symptoms

After the result, the right next step is not always another test. Sometimes the report answers the question and symptom follow-up is enough; sometimes targeted laboratory tests or interval review are appropriate; sometimes a different examination becomes the priority. The individual decision belongs with the treating clinician because they know the indication, pain location and duration, examination findings, medicines and previous results. Do not start treatment or stop a medicine because of one phrase in a report without review [1, 3, 11].

Prepare for the visit by bringing the imaging request if available, the report and images, earlier test results and a list of medicines and supplements. Note when the symptom began, what worsens or relieves it and whether it relates to eating, bowel movements or urination. If the report says an area was not seen well, ask whether that area was central to the original question. If it recommends follow-up, ask why, when and which changes should prompt an earlier return [1, 2, 10].

At the clinic, an internal-medicine or gastroenterology assessment can connect symptoms and examination with the report and determine whether abdominal ultrasound is suitable or whether pelvic imaging or another specialty is needed. The clinic is mentioned here only as an organisational next step, without promising that ultrasound will find the cause or that one visit will settle every case. If you need to begin a structured assessment, you can use the request for a medical consultation and state that you have an abdominal and pelvic ultrasound report [1, 2].
