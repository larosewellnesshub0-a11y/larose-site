What can an abdominal ultrasound show—and what can it not show?

Published: 7 September 2026 8 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

On this page

  2. The structures ultrasound can assess
  3. Gas, bone and many conditions create limits
  4. Preparation depends on the area being examined
  5. The report is one step in assessment, not the final word

A picture made with sound waves, without ionising radiation

Ultrasound sends sound waves into the body and turns their returning echoes into live images. A probe moves over the skin with gel to help the waves pass through, while the examiner changes the angle or asks you to take a deep breath for a clearer view. A standard diagnostic examination does not use x-rays.

Image quality depends on the organ, the clinical question and preparation, as well as gas or tissue that may block the view. Ultrasound is therefore more than a single still picture: the examination and choice of views are part of the result, and the report must be read alongside symptoms, examination and laboratory tests.

The structures ultrasound can assess

Abdominal ultrasound can assess the shape and size of the liver, gallbladder, bile ducts, kidneys and spleen, along with parts of the pancreas and major vessels when visible. It may detect gallstones, some kidney stones or signs of obstruction, dilated bile ducts, fluid collections, or a change in an organ's size or texture.

A finding is not a complete diagnosis on its own. Fatty change in the liver, for example, needs to be connected with metabolic risk and blood tests, while a gallstone is interpreted against the location and timing of pain. Ultrasound sometimes finds an incidental feature that does not explain the complaint; your clinician decides whether it needs follow-up or another test.

Gas, bone and many conditions create limits

Sound waves are disrupted by air and gas and do not pass through bone, so ultrasound is not the best test for the stomach wall or gas-filled bowel, and part of the pancreas may be obscured. A normal result also does not rule out reflux, irritable bowel syndrome or a stomach ulcer, because these conditions rely on the history, examination and sometimes laboratory tests or endoscopy.

A small or awkwardly positioned stone may be missed, while some masses or vascular concerns require CT, MRI or Doppler depending on the question. This does not mean ultrasound has failed. Every test has a suitable question, and the clinician moves to another test when the result cannot answer it.

Preparation depends on the area being examined

Gallbladder and upper-abdominal imaging may require a period without food, while some kidney or pelvic examinations may need a full bladder. Instructions are not identical for every scan, so follow the directions for your particular booking and do not stop prescribed medicine on your own. If you have diabetes or fasting is difficult, explain this before the appointment so the instructions can be adapted safely.

Bring earlier reports or images and mention the location and timing of pain, previous operations and medicines. Comfortable clothing makes access to the abdomen easier. The probe may cause mild pressure over a tender area; tell the examiner if pain is severe rather than enduring it silently.

The report is one step in assessment, not the final word

After the examination, the report summarises what was seen and the main impression. It may recommend comparison with older imaging, follow-up or a different type of scan. A word such as 'normal' applies to what the examination could assess and does not erase a persistent symptom. An unfamiliar term in a report does not automatically mean a serious problem either.

Review the result with the clinician who knows why the scan was requested, especially when pain continues or laboratory results are abnormal. Sudden severe pain, jaundice, repeated vomiting, fever with abdominal pain, fainting or bleeding need urgent assessment rather than waiting for a routine ultrasound appointment.
