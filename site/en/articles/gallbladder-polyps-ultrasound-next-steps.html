Gallbladder polyps on ultrasound: what happens next?

Published: 27 September 2026 10 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Start with the exact report: a protrusion is not always a true polyp

If the ultrasound says “protrusion,” “polyp,” “polypoid lesion” or “a focus arising from the gallbladder wall,” do not turn that word alone into a final diagnosis. A polypoid finding describes an appearance inside the gallbladder, but it may represent a true polyp, a benign cholesterol deposit, adherent sludge, a fold, or a change such as adenomyomatosis. The first step is therefore to read the whole sentence rather than search one isolated word. Transabdominal ultrasound is the usual first test, but it cannot perfectly distinguish a true polyp from its mimics, and a systematic review comparing ultrasound with pathology after gallbladder removal found a high false-positive rate [1, 3, 4].

Keep the original report and images if available. Look for the maximum size in millimetres, number and location of lesions, whether there is a stalk or a broad base, and whether focal wall thickening is present. Also check whether the focus stayed fixed when position changed, whether it cast an acoustic shadow, and whether stones or sludge were described beside it. If the wording is brief or uncertain, ask whether the images need review, whether a well-prepared repeat scan would clarify the finding, or whether planned surveillance is enough. A line that only says “gallbladder polyps” without measurements and morphology is not enough to choose surveillance or surgery [1, 3, 4].

How is a polyp different from a stone, sludge or a wall change?

A classic polyp on ultrasound appears as a fixed projection arising from the wall. It usually does not move when body position changes and does not cast a posterior acoustic shadow. A stone usually moves with gravity and casts a shadow, although a tiny or impacted stone can be harder to separate. Sludge generally layers or moves slowly, but tumefactive adherent sludge can resemble a polyp. Gallbladder wall thickening is a different description: it may be diffuse or focal and has several possible causes, so the report should distinguish it from a projection into the lumen [3, 4].

These features help, but they are not a home diagnostic test or an infallible two-line rule. Equipment quality, gallbladder distension, patient position, operator experience and lesion size all affect the image. A stone and a polyp can coexist, while a “polyp” that disappears later may have been sludge or may reflect differences in visualisation and measurement; it does not necessarily mean that a dangerous disease vanished. If your main question concerns silent stones without symptoms, or pain attacks and the decision about gallstone surgery, those are separate pathways. This article stays focused on an incidental protrusion or polyp and the factors governing its follow-up [1, 3, 4].

Size, shape and context: what changes the level of concern?

Most small projections, particularly those under 10 mm, are benign non-neoplastic changes such as cholesterol polyps, and the presence of a small polyp does not mean cancer. Large datasets show that risk is related more strongly to size than to the word “polyp” alone, and that cancer risk in small polyps is very low. No single number replaces the whole picture, however: society recommendations combine measurement, morphology and clinical context, and different groups use different thresholds or categories [1, 2, 6, 7].

Details that matter include the largest diameter in millimetres, whether the lesion has a broad sessile base rather than a clear stalk, whether focal wall thickening is present, and whether size has increased across comparable examinations. The European guideline treats age over 60 years, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), Asian ethnicity, and sessile morphology or focal wall thickening over 4 mm as factors that change management for some 6–9 mm lesions. PSC in particular follows a specialist pathway with a different risk profile, so a general surveillance table should not be applied without hepatology input [1, 11].

Symptoms require assessment when present, but pain alongside a polyp does not prove that the polyp caused it. General factors such as family history or blood-test results may also affect clinical evaluation, but they do not replace imaging detail. The goal is not to accumulate frightening risk factors; it is to identify which guidance fits your setting, whether the initial description is reliable, and whether the decision needs radiology, gastroenterology or surgical input [1, 2, 3].

Surveillance is not one schedule for everyone

The updated European guideline offers a useful example of the reasoning, not a schedule to prescribe for yourself. In that pathway, a polyp of 5 mm or less without specified risk factors needs no surveillance. A 6–9 mm polyp without those risk factors, or a lesion of 5 mm or less with a risk factor, is scanned at 6 months, 1 year and 2 years, with surveillance stopped after 2 years if there is no growth. A 6–9 mm polyp with a specified risk factor, or a lesion reaching 10 mm, prompts discussion of gallbladder removal according to surgical fitness, patient acceptance and individual review [1].

This is not the only worldwide schedule. The US Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound recommendations begin with polyp morphology and place lesions into risk categories, while recent Korean recommendations use their own combination of size, morphology and context. Two reports or health systems can therefore suggest different intervals without one necessarily being careless. The clinician should identify the locally adopted guidance and explain why it applies to your case [2, 3, 12].

Do not arrange random scans every few months, and do not cancel written surveillance because you found a less intensive table online. Ask directly: What is the baseline measurement? Which risk factors genuinely apply? When is the next scan and what question will it answer? When can surveillance stop if the finding remains stable? A sound plan has a starting point, a purpose and a stopping point, and it can change if the original description was uncertain or a meaningful new feature appears [1, 2, 3].

If the measurement increases: separate true growth from measurement variation

A difference of one or two millimetres between scans does not automatically mean dangerous transformation. Tiny projections are difficult to measure with absolute precision, and image angle, gallbladder distension, equipment and operator can change the number. Long-term follow-up studies found that some polyps enlarge slightly, some shrink and some disappear, while a large cohort showed that growth of 2 mm can be part of the natural history even for small lesions [7, 8].

In the European pathway, growth of 2 mm or more within 2 years leads the clinician to reassess current size together with risk factors rather than treating growth alone as an automatic indication for surgery. Reaching 10 mm prompts a surgical discussion. If the lesion disappears, the guideline allows surveillance to stop. Korean recommendations likewise emphasize comparison of morphology and size, rather than reducing suspicion to one number without considering concerning features such as wall thickening or a clearly suspicious shape [1, 3].

For a useful comparison, bring the earlier report and images if available, and have follow-up performed as your clinician advises at a service able to compare with the prior study. Ask whether both measurements refer to the same lesion and axis, whether the difference exceeds likely measurement variation, and whether morphology changed as well as size. If the new report says “slight increase,” request the old and new measurements and dates. The decision depends on the amount and timing of change plus context, not the word “growth” alone [1, 3, 7].

Can a polyp cause pain, and when should you not wait for surveillance?

A gallbladder polyp itself is unlikely to cause pain, so finding one incidentally does not prove that it caused cramps, bloating or indigestion. The European guideline allows gallbladder removal to be discussed when symptoms could plausibly arise from the gallbladder and no alternative cause is found, but it also requires counselling that symptoms may persist after surgery if the polyp was not their true cause. Pain assessment includes its pattern and duration, relationship to meals, examination, blood tests, and the presence of stones, inflammation or another diagnosis outside the gallbladder [1].

Do not wait for a routine surveillance scan if you develop severe or persistent right-upper-abdominal pain, particularly with fever or chills, repeated vomiting, yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or pale stools. These features can indicate gallbladder inflammation, bile-duct obstruction or a pancreatic problem and need urgent assessment; they do not mean that a polyp has turned into cancer. Sudden severe pain or a marked deterioration in your general condition also requires urgent care through the appropriate local service [9, 10].

If pain is recurrent but not an emergency, record its site, duration and associated features and take the report to your clinician rather than assuming every symptom comes from the polyp. Details of gallstone attacks and surgery decisions for symptomatic stones belong to a separate guide because a moving stone or acute inflammation follows a different pathway from a fixed incidental protrusion [1, 9, 10].

When are additional imaging or surgical review considered?

Most lesions begin with transabdominal ultrasound, and extra imaging is not routine for every small polyp. In difficult cases, where suitable expertise exists, a clinician may consider endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) or contrast-enhanced ultrasound to characterize the lesion. A Cochrane review, however, found insufficient evidence that EUS consistently outperforms ordinary ultrasound in distinguishing every polyp type, so any additional test should answer a defined question and be likely to change the decision [1, 5].

A surgical opinion is not the same as a decision to operate. Its purpose is to review the likely benefit against the risks of gallbladder removal, confirm size, morphology and risk factors, and discuss alternatives. The European guideline recommends discussing removal at 10 mm or above for a person fit for and accepting surgery, and recommends it for some 6–9 mm lesions with specified risk factors. SRU guidance uses different morphology-based categories and thresholds, while people with PSC need a specialist decision based on lesion size, growth and clinical status [1, 2, 11, 12].

Do not stop medicines, begin a restrictive diet, or assume gallbladder removal will prevent every episode of pain based on the report alone. If surgery is offered, ask: Is this likely to be a true polyp? Which guideline is being used? What is the specific reason for surgery in my case? Could specialist repeat imaging change the decision? What is the chance that symptoms persist? The final choice is individual and combines imaging, general health, clinical advice and your preferences after a clear explanation [1, 2, 5].

Your next step: a short checklist before clinical review

Before the appointment, gather the full ultrasound report and images if possible, any older scan for comparison, and a list of symptoms, medicines and relevant conditions. Note the largest measurement in millimetres, whether the report describes a stalk, broad base or wall thickening, and whether a stone or sludge is present. Mention any diagnosis of PSC or specialist liver follow-up. Do not rely on a version remembered from a conversation: a word such as “mobile” or “fixed,” and a difference of a few millimetres, can change the risk category [1, 3, 4].

Ask five practical questions: Does this most likely represent a true polyp or a mimic? Which guideline applies here? Do I need surveillance, and what are its timing, purpose and stopping point? Which changes mean returning earlier? Do my symptoms need a separate assessment pathway? If the original report lacks detail, reviewing the images or repeating a good-quality ultrasound may be more useful than jumping directly to complex imaging or surgery [1, 3, 5].

The medical team at La Rose can review the report alongside your symptoms and health history and help define the next step, without treating one ultrasound line as a final diagnosis or promising an outcome. If you have the warning signs described above, seek urgent care rather than waiting for a routine booking. If your condition is stable, book a consultation and bring every relevant report so the decision can rest on clear comparison and an individual discussion [1, 9, 10].
