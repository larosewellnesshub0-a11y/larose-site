Gallbladder polyps on ultrasound: what happens next?

Start with the exact report: a protrusion is not always a true polyp

If the ultrasound says “protrusion,” “polyp,” “polypoid lesion” or “a focus arising from the gallbladder wall,” do not turn that word alone into a final diagnosis. A polypoid finding describes an appearance inside the gallbladder. It might be a true polyp, a harmless cholesterol spot, sticky bile sludge, a normal tissue fold, or a benign wall change like adenomyomatosis. The first step is to read the full report rather than worry over a single word. Transabdominal ultrasound (a standard belly scan) is usually the first test. However, it cannot always tell a true polyp apart from these lookalikes. In fact, a large review comparing ultrasound results with pathology checks after surgery found a high false-positive rate [1, 3, 4].

Keep your original report and scan pictures if you have them. Look for the largest size in millimetres, how many spots were seen, and where they sit. Check whether the spot has a thin stalk or a broad base, and whether any part of the gallbladder wall looks thickened. It also helps to note if the spot stayed still when you moved, whether it cast a shadow on the scan, and if any stones or sludge were nearby. If the report is brief or unclear, ask your doctor if the pictures need a second look. You can also ask whether a repeat scan with good preparation would help, or if scheduled check-ups are enough. A report that simply says “gallbladder polyps” without size or shape details is not enough to decide on surveillance or surgery [1, 3, 4].

How is a polyp different from a stone, sludge or a wall change?

A classic polyp on ultrasound appears as a fixed projection arising from the wall. It usually does not move when body position changes and does not cast a posterior acoustic shadow. A stone usually moves with gravity and casts a shadow, although a tiny or impacted stone can be harder to separate. Sludge generally layers or moves slowly, but tumefactive adherent sludge can resemble a polyp. Gallbladder wall thickening is a different description: it may be diffuse or focal and has several possible causes, so the report should distinguish it from a projection into the lumen [3, 4].

These features help, but they are not a home diagnostic test or an infallible two-line rule. Equipment quality, gallbladder distension, patient position, operator experience and lesion size all affect the image. A stone and a polyp can coexist, while a “polyp” that disappears later may have been sludge or may reflect differences in visualisation and measurement; it does not necessarily mean that a dangerous disease vanished. If your main question concerns silent stones without symptoms, or pain attacks and the decision about gallstone surgery, those are separate pathways. This article stays focused on an incidental protrusion or polyp and the factors governing its follow-up [1, 3, 4].

Size, shape and context: what changes the level of concern?

Most small growths, especially those under 10 mm, are non-cancerous changes like harmless cholesterol polyps. Having a small polyp does not mean you have cancer. Large studies show that cancer risk depends much more on the size than on the word “polyp” itself. For small polyps, that risk is very low. Even so, no single measurement tells the whole story. Medical guidelines look at size, overall shape, and your personal health history together, and different expert groups use slightly different cutoff points [1, 2, 6, 7].

Key details include the widest measurement in millimetres and whether the polyp has a broad flat base or a narrow stalk. Your doctor will also look for any local thickening in the gallbladder wall and check if the polyp has grown since earlier scans. The European guideline considers age over 60 years, primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), Asian ethnicity, and a broad base or wall thickening over 4 mm as factors that guide next steps for some 6–9 mm polyps. Because PSC carries distinct risks, it follows a specialist plan, so a general follow-up table should not be used without a liver specialist's input [1, 11].

Any symptoms you have need careful evaluation. Still, having belly pain along with a polyp does not automatically mean the polyp is causing it. Other clues like family health history or blood tests help your care team, but they cannot replace clear imaging details. The goal is not to collect worrying risk factors. Instead, it is to see which guidelines apply to your care, make sure the scan description is accurate, and decide if you need advice from an imaging specialist, a gastroenterologist, or a surgeon [1, 2, 3].

Surveillance is not one schedule for everyone

The updated European guideline provides a helpful example of how doctors think through these cases, rather than a schedule you should choose on your own. Under this plan, a polyp of 5 mm or less without specific risk factors needs no routine follow-up scans. A 6–9 mm polyp without risk factors, or a growth of 5 mm or less with a risk factor, is checked by ultrasound at 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. Monitoring ends after 2 years if the size does not change. A 6–9 mm polyp with a specific risk factor, or any growth reaching 10 mm, calls for a conversation about gallbladder surgery based on your general health, your wishes, and a personal review [1].

This is not the only schedule used around the world. Guidelines from the US Society of Radiologists in Ultrasound start with the shape of the polyp and sort findings into risk groups. Recent Korean recommendations use their own mix of size, shape, and patient health. Because of these differences, two imaging centers or health systems might suggest slightly different follow-up timelines without either being wrong. Your doctor should explain which local guidelines they follow and why that plan fits your situation [2, 3, 12].

Avoid booking random scans every few months, and do not stop a planned check-up just because you found a looser schedule online. Instead, ask your doctor directly: What was the starting measurement? Which risk factors actually apply to me? When is my next scan due, and what will it check? When can we safely stop monitoring if the polyp stays stable? A solid plan has a clear start, an ongoing goal, and an agreed stopping point. It can also be updated if an earlier scan was unclear or if a new feature shows up [1, 2, 3].

If the measurement increases: separate true growth from measurement variation

A difference of one or two millimetres between ultrasound scans does not automatically mean something dangerous is happening. Tiny growths are tricky to measure with pinpoint accuracy. The scan angle, how full the gallbladder is, the machine used, and the person scanning can all nudge the number up or down. Long-term studies show that some polyps grow a little, some shrink, and some vanish completely. In fact, one large group study found that a 2 mm change in size can simply be part of the natural life of a small polyp [7, 8].

In the European guideline, growth of 2 mm or more within 2 years leads your doctor to review the overall size and your risk factors, rather than rushing straight into surgery. Reaching 10 mm does lead to a discussion about surgery. If the polyp disappears on a later scan, follow-up can safely end. Korean guidelines also stress looking at both shape and size together, rather than focusing on a single number while overlooking features like wall thickening or an irregular outline [1, 3].

To get an accurate comparison, bring your past scan reports and images with you. It helps to have follow-up scans done where doctors can review your earlier records side by side. Ask your doctor if both scans measured the same spot along the same angle, if the difference is within normal scanner variation, and whether the shape has changed. If a new report simply mentions a “slight increase,” ask for the exact dates and numbers. Medical decisions depend on how much changed, how fast it changed, and your overall health, not just the word “growth” alone [1, 3, 7].

Can a polyp cause pain, and when should you not wait for surveillance?

A gallbladder polyp itself is unlikely to cause pain, so finding one incidentally does not prove that it caused cramps, bloating or indigestion. The European guideline allows gallbladder removal to be discussed when symptoms could plausibly arise from the gallbladder and no alternative cause is found, but it also requires counselling that symptoms may persist after surgery if the polyp was not their true cause. Pain assessment includes its pattern and duration, relationship to meals, examination, blood tests, and the presence of stones, inflammation or another diagnosis outside the gallbladder [1].

Do not wait for a routine surveillance scan if you develop severe or persistent right-upper-abdominal pain, particularly with fever or chills, repeated vomiting, yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or pale stools. These features can indicate gallbladder inflammation, bile-duct obstruction or a pancreatic problem and need urgent assessment; they do not mean that a polyp has turned into cancer. Sudden severe pain or a marked deterioration in your general condition also requires urgent care through the appropriate local service [9, 10].

If pain is recurrent but not an emergency, record its site, duration and associated features and take the report to your clinician rather than assuming every symptom comes from the polyp. Details of gallstone attacks and surgery decisions for symptomatic stones belong to a separate guide because a moving stone or acute inflammation follows a different pathway from a fixed incidental protrusion [1, 9, 10].

When are additional imaging or surgical review considered?

Most evaluations start with a standard belly ultrasound, and extra imaging tests are not needed for every small polyp. In challenging cases where specialist skills are available, your doctor might consider an endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) or contrast ultrasound to examine the area more closely. Still, a Cochrane review found no clear proof that EUS regularly outperforms a routine ultrasound for every kind of polyp. Any extra test should answer a specific question and be likely to change the decision [1, 5].

Meeting with a surgeon does not mean an operation is already decided. The goal is to weigh the benefits against the risks of removing your gallbladder, confirm the polyp's size and shape, check your risk factors, and discuss other choices. The European guideline suggests discussing surgery if a polyp reaches 10 mm or larger in someone healthy enough for the procedure. It also suggests it for certain 6–9 mm polyps with specific risk factors. SRU guidance uses different shape-based categories, while anyone with PSC needs a tailored plan from a specialist based on size, growth, and general health [1, 2, 11, 12].

Do not stop your medications, start a strict diet, or assume removing the gallbladder will stop every episode of pain based only on a scan report. If an operation is suggested, ask: Is this likely a real polyp or a harmless mimic? Which guideline is guiding this choice? What is the exact reason for surgery in my situation? Could a repeat scan by an imaging specialist change the plan? What is the chance that my discomfort might continue? Your final decision should combine scan results, your overall health, medical advice, and your own comfort after an open discussion [1, 2, 5].

Your next step: a short checklist before clinical review

Before the appointment, gather the full ultrasound report and images if possible, any older scan for comparison, and a list of symptoms, medicines and relevant conditions. Note the largest measurement in millimetres, whether the report describes a stalk, broad base or wall thickening, and whether a stone or sludge is present. Mention any diagnosis of PSC or specialist liver follow-up. Do not rely on a version remembered from a conversation: a word such as “mobile” or “fixed,” and a difference of a few millimetres, can change the risk category [1, 3, 4].

Ask five practical questions: Does this most likely represent a true polyp or a mimic? Which guideline applies here? Do I need surveillance, and what are its timing, purpose and stopping point? Which changes mean returning earlier? Do my symptoms need a separate assessment pathway? If the original report lacks detail, reviewing the images or repeating a good-quality ultrasound may be more useful than jumping directly to complex imaging or surgery [1, 3, 5].

The medical team at La Rose can review the report alongside your symptoms and health history and help define the next step, without treating one ultrasound line as a final diagnosis or promising an outcome. If you have the warning signs described above, seek urgent care rather than waiting for a routine booking. If your condition is stable, book a consultation and bring every relevant report so the decision can rest on clear comparison and an individual discussion [1, 9, 10].
