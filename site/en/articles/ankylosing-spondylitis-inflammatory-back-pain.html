Ankylosing spondylitis: when is back pain inflammatory?

Published: 1 October 2026 10 min read Dr. Shimaa Sherif

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Ankylosing spondylitis is part of a wider spectrum

Ankylosing spondylitis is a familiar name for an inflammatory rheumatic disease that mainly affects the sacroiliac joints between the lower spine and pelvis. It can also involve the spine, other joints and other sites. The broader current term is axial spondyloarthritis, or axSpA. This spectrum includes a form with definite sacroiliac-joint changes on plain X-ray, traditionally called ankylosing spondylitis, and a non-radiographic form. “Non-radiographic” does not mean that the pain is unreal or that inflammation is absent. It means that plain X-rays do not show the structural changes required for that label, while the history, examination, and sometimes MRI and laboratory findings may still provide important evidence [1, 2, 4, 6].

The disease is not simply a matter of “fused vertebrae,” and that older name can give an unduly alarming or late-stage picture. Symptoms, pace and impact differ between people, and pain and stiffness may flare and settle. A common presentation is gradually developing pain or stiffness in the lower back or buttocks, often beginning in younger or middle adulthood before age 45. Age alone is not diagnostic, however. Mechanical back pain is very common, and diagnosis depends on assembling the symptom pattern, associated features, examination and test results rather than relying on one sign or one blood test [1, 3, 5].

How does inflammatory back pain differ from mechanical pain?

Mechanical back pain often follows exertion, a movement or a particular posture, and it may improve with rest. Inflammatory back pain has a different pattern: onset is gradual, symptoms persist for weeks or months, rest does not provide good relief, and movement may help. Morning stiffness, pain that wakes someone in the second half of the night, and buttock pain that may alternate from side to side are details that can raise suspicion of axial spondyloarthritis. The ASAS research criteria for inflammatory back pain include onset before age 40, gradual onset, improvement with exercise, no improvement with rest, and night pain that improves on getting up. A cluster of features raises suspicion, but these criteria identify a pattern; they do not establish a diagnosis [5, 6, 9].

The distinction is not always neat. A person with axial spondyloarthritis can also have a muscle strain or disc problem, while someone with mechanical pain may feel stiff after sitting. That is why a clinician asks when the pain began, what improves or worsens it, whether morning stiffness is present, whether sleep is interrupted, whether buttock pain alternates, and whether the pattern has persisted for more than three months. A consistent set of answers supports rheumatology assessment, particularly when symptoms started before age 45, but it does not confirm the diagnosis. Equally, the absence of one feature, such as obvious relief with movement, cannot by itself exclude the disease [3, 6, 9].

Features that may accompany the back pain

Axial spondyloarthritis can have features beyond the back. Some people develop pain or swelling in a joint such as the knee or hip, or inflammation where a tendon attaches to bone, which can present as pain at the back or underside of the heel. Inflammation of the joints linking the ribs and breastbone can cause discomfort with a deep breath or reduce chest flexibility. Fatigue is also recognised, but it is a very general symptom with many possible causes and should not be attributed to this disease without assessment [1, 5].

It is important to ask about a suddenly painful red eye with light sensitivity or blurred vision, psoriasis, persistent diarrhoea or blood in the stool, a previous diagnosis of inflammatory bowel disease, and relatives with spondyloarthritis, psoriasis or bowel inflammation. These details are not proof of complications; they are parts of a clinical picture that can inform a rheumatology assessment. A painful red eye or change in vision needs urgent eye assessment because of possible acute anterior uveitis. Separately from the disease pattern, new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the saddle area, or new and worsening leg weakness with back pain requires immediate emergency care. Those are neurological emergency signs and should not wait for a routine appointment [1, 3, 5, 13].

Assessment starts with the history and examination, not random tests

A rheumatologist connects the history before selecting tests. The question is not simply whether the back hurts. The age at onset, duration, night and morning pattern, response to movement and rest, buttock pain, and any joint, heel, eye, skin or bowel features all matter. Examination may assess movement of the lower back and neck, the sacroiliac region, peripheral joints, tendon attachment sites, posture and chest expansion. No single manoeuvre can prove or exclude the disease, but examination adds context and helps the clinician consider other causes [2, 3].

Inflammatory markers such as CRP and ESR may support the overall picture and can help monitor some patients, but they may be normal in axial spondyloarthritis. A clinician may request a blood count, liver and kidney tests or other investigations according to symptoms, alternative diagnoses and possible treatment; they are not a fixed panel for every backache. NICE guidance explicitly advises that a negative HLA-B27 result or normal CRP and ESR should not be used to rule out axial spondyloarthritis. The central point is that no single blood test diagnoses the condition, and no single normal result closes the question when the clinical pattern remains convincing [2, 3, 6].

What does an HLA-B27 result mean?

HLA-B27 is a genetic variant associated with a higher likelihood of axial spondyloarthritis, but it is not a test in which positive means disease and negative means healthy. Many people carry the variant and never develop the disease, while axial spondyloarthritis can occur without it. The prevalence of HLA-B27 and the strength of its association with disease vary between populations, so one percentage does not apply to every country or individual. A positive result adds weight when the history and examination are compatible; a negative result may lower probability in some settings but does not eliminate it [1, 3, 8].

Testing is therefore most useful when a clinician already sees a reasonable clinical possibility and wants another piece of evidence. Using it as broad screening in someone without an inflammatory pain pattern can create anxiety and self-diagnosis without benefit. A positive result does not measure disease severity, predict that the spine will fuse, or select one treatment by itself. It is interpreted alongside age at pain onset, associated features, family history, inflammatory markers and imaging. There is no reason to repeat HLA-B27 to monitor activity because it is a genetic trait, not a measurement that changes as pain improves or worsens [2, 3, 8].

X-ray and MRI answer different questions

Plain X-rays of the sacroiliac joints show structural changes that have developed over time, but they may be normal early in the disease. When X-rays are inconclusive and suspicion remains, an MRI with appropriate sacroiliac-joint sequences may show active inflammation before definite X-ray changes appear. NICE guidance describes a pathway that generally begins with sacroiliac X-rays and proceeds to a dedicated MRI when X-rays do not establish sacroiliitis, with every image interpreted in the context of symptoms and examination [2, 3, 6].

MRI is not a self-ordered test for every backache, and an area of oedema or high signal is not a diagnosis by itself. Similar changes can occur for other reasons, so sacroiliac imaging requires appropriate technique, context and experienced interpretation. Conversely, one normal MRI does not always end the assessment if clinical suspicion is strong; the clinician may reconsider timing, alternative diagnoses or follow-up. CT and ultrasound have specific uses, but neither automatically replaces the recommended pathway for evaluating axial disease. The imaging choice depends on the clinical question, stage and prior findings, not on an assumption that the most expensive test is best for everyone [3, 6, 10].

Treatment principles: regular movement and medication matched to activity

Treatment aims to reduce symptoms and inflammation, preserve movement and function, and monitor how the disease affects daily life. Education, regular exercise and physiotherapy are core parts of care rather than additions after medication. The programme is tailored and may include flexibility, strengthening, posture, breathing and aerobic work. American guidance favours active physical-therapy interventions and supervised exercise over passive modalities alone, while ASAS–EULAR guidance places exercise and physiotherapy among the central management principles [2, 7, 11, 12].

For medication, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are commonly the first pharmacological option when medically suitable, with attention to gastrointestinal, kidney and cardiovascular risks and other medicines. Persistent active disease despite an appropriate initial plan may lead to a rheumatology discussion about targeted or biologic therapy. Selection depends on disease activity, objective findings, response to previous treatment, and associated conditions such as recurrent uveitis, psoriasis or inflammatory bowel disease. Conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs have a limited role in purely axial symptoms, although their role may differ when peripheral joints are inflamed. This article does not provide individual drug selection or doses; do not start, stop or change treatment without the prescribing clinician [3, 7, 11, 12].

What can you do now, and when should you book?

If back pain persists, keep a short record of when pain and stiffness occur, how long morning stiffness lasts, the effects of movement and rest, and any night pain or eye, skin, bowel or heel symptoms. These notes can make the consultation more precise, but there is no need to test yourself forcefully or do strenuous exercises to “prove” the pattern. Keep gently and regularly active within your ability, break up prolonged sitting, and seek professional advice before a new programme if you have an injury, weakness, balance problems, a relevant chronic condition, or pain that worsens with exercise. Suitable exercise is progressed gradually; it should not become a test of pain tolerance [2, 7, 11].

Arrange a medical assessment when pain began before age 45, has persisted for more than three months, and especially when it worsens with rest, wakes you late at night, or accompanies morning stiffness, buttock or heel pain, eye symptoms, psoriasis or inflammatory bowel disease. The medical team at La Rose can review the pattern and examination and decide whether rheumatology assessment or selected tests are appropriate, at the Maadi and Fifth Settlement, New Cairo branches. Assessment is not a promise of a particular diagnosis; its purpose is to choose the next step calmly. A painful red eye with blurred vision, new leg weakness, saddle-area numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control is not a routine booking matter and needs immediate urgent care [1, 3, 5, 13].
