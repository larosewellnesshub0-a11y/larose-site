Cellulite is a natural skin structure feature: causes, anatomy and treatment limits

Published: 12 August 2026 9 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Cellulite reflects natural skin architecture, not obesity

Cellulite is not a disease, a sign of poor hygiene, or trapped toxins. It is a common feature of the skin and the fat beneath it, reported in about 80 to 90 percent of women after puberty. It can appear in a lean or athletic person as well as in someone with more body fat, and it does not harm general health [1, 2, 4].

The skin is connected to deeper tissue by collagen-rich fibrous bands, called fibrous septae. These bands pull the skin inward at fixed points while small areas of fat push outward between them. That combination creates the familiar dimples or orange-peel texture [1, 2, 4, 7].

Cellulite therefore does not measure total body fat or personal discipline, and it does not need treatment for health reasons. Understanding its structure can help you set realistic expectations if its appearance bothers you [1, 2, 7, 10].

Why does it appear predominantly in women? Anatomy and hormones

Cellulite is much more common in women, while some studies report it in fewer than 2 percent of men. One reason may be the different pattern of the fibrous bands and the thickness of the skin. In women, these bands often run more vertically, allowing fat between them to push upward while each band pulls the skin down [2, 4, 7].

In men, the bands more often form a crossing network and the skin tends to be thicker. Researchers think this structure spreads pressure differently and makes visible dimpling less likely, even when body-fat levels are higher [2, 4, 10].

Hormones may also play a part. Estrogen is linked with fat storage around the hips and thighs and may affect connective tissue and fluid movement. Changes around puberty, pregnancy, breastfeeding, or hormonal contraception may make dimpling more noticeable in some people, but they do not affect everyone in the same way [2, 4, 7, 11].

Cellulite grading: clinical assessment between standing and lying down

Clinicians may use a grading system such as the Nurnberger-Muller scale while the person stands relaxed in even lighting. Grade 0 means the skin looks smooth while standing and lying down, without dimpling when the skin is gently pinched [2, 4, 7, 9].

In Grade 1, the skin looks smooth at rest and dimples appear only when it is pinched or the buttock muscle contracts. Grade 2 means dimples are visible while standing but become less visible or disappear while lying down [2, 4, 7, 9].

In Grade 3, deeper dimples remain visible while standing and lying down, often with tighter fibrous bands and some loose skin. Grading describes appearance; it does not turn cellulite into a disease. It can help avoid unnecessary treatment for changes seen only with pressure and make expectations clearer when dimpling is more visible [2, 4, 8, 9].

The role of weight and exercise: why weight loss may not erase it

Weight gain can enlarge fat cells beneath the skin and make dimples more noticeable. Balanced nutrition and moderate weight loss may soften the appearance for some people. Rapid or large weight loss is not a guaranteed solution, however, because looser skin can make existing dimples look deeper [2, 5, 7, 10].

The fibrous bands may remain after fat loss, so the basic structure does not simply disappear. Resistance exercise can help protect or build the muscles of the hips and thighs during weight loss, which may give the skin a firmer base, but it cannot promise smooth skin [2, 5, 7, 10].

It is also useful to tell cellulite apart from loose skin or localised fat after weight loss. A clinical assessment can help identify what is contributing to the appearance and whether any proposed treatment matches that concern [2, 5, 8, 11].

In-clinic treatment options: what the evidence supports and their limits

The American Academy of Dermatology reviews cellulite treatments by the quality of their evidence. Subcision has evidence for selected deep dimples: a trained clinician uses a needle, small blade, or vacuum-assisted tool to release tight fibrous bands. Studies report improvement lasting two to three years in some patients, but the procedure needs local anaesthetic and can cause bruising and soreness [2, 5, 8, 9].

Acoustic wave therapy uses mechanical pulses and may improve the surface appearance after a course of treatment. Studies often used 6 to 10 sessions, and maintenance may be needed. Laser fibres placed beneath the skin and radiofrequency have also been studied for skin tightening and collagen change, with mixed results and different risks [2, 5, 6, 12].

Fat freezing is designed for pockets of fat, not the fibrous bands that form cellulite. Mesotherapy injections do not have strong evidence for cellulite and have safety concerns. Read our guide on body-contouring safety for risks and questions to discuss with an independent qualified clinician before considering a procedure [3, 5, 6, 7].

Creams, massage and home devices: the reality without exaggeration

Creams and serums are often advertised as quick cellulite fixes, but their effects are limited. Caffeine creams may briefly change surface hydration and make the skin look a little tighter for a few hours. They do not change the fibrous bands or remove fat cells [2, 5, 6, 10].

Retinol used regularly for at least six months may slightly thicken the skin and make some dimples less noticeable. It can also cause redness, peeling, or contact irritation. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding should ask their clinician before using a retinoid product [5, 6, 7, 10].

Dry brushing, suction rollers, and home massage may briefly change blood flow or tissue fluid, so the skin can look smoother for a short time. Evidence does not show that they cut fibrous bands or remove toxins. Hard rubbing can irritate the skin, damage its protective barrier, and leave dark marks after inflammation [1, 2, 5, 7].

When is a change not cellulite? Red flags and alternative diagnoses

Ordinary cellulite is usually painless and is not inflamed. New severe pain, spreading warmth or redness, or swelling in one leg is not typical and needs same-day urgent medical care; some people need hospital admission. Possible causes include bacterial cellulitis, which is a skin and soft-tissue infection, or a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [1, 3, 5, 11].

A firm lump that grows quickly or a sudden change over a few weeks also needs prompt examination. A clinician may arrange soft-tissue ultrasound depending on the findings. Other conditions can resemble cellulite, including lipedema, which may cause painful symmetrical fat distribution and easy bruising, and long-term lymphoedema caused by poor lymph drainage [2, 4, 7, 11].

Strong massage or energy-based fat treatment may worsen pain, bruising, or swelling when the cause is lipedema, lymphoedema, infection, or a clot. Get the change assessed before considering cosmetic treatment [2, 3, 7, 11].

Structuring sensible next steps realistically without false promises

If cellulite bothers you, set a realistic goal: treatment may make some dimples less visible, but it cannot promise completely smooth skin. Before deciding, ask about the likely degree of change, risks, and how long any result may last [1, 2, 5, 8].

Stable weight, balanced eating, and resistance exercise may support the appearance of the skin and underlying muscle, but they are not guaranteed cellulite treatments. If you are considering a cosmetic procedure, ask a qualified clinician about the evidence, number of sessions, duration of benefit, side effects, and alternatives [2, 5, 8, 9].

At our clinics, nutrition and weight consultations can include a review of your health history, medicines, and available tests, with internal-medicine input when needed. This service does not promise a cosmetic cellulite procedure. You can book a clinic consultation to discuss weight stability or nutrition, and the clinician can explain if a separate assessment from an appropriate cosmetic provider is needed [2, 5, 8, 12].
