Scientific update: repeated safety questions before surgery are deliberate

Repeated questions are part of the safety system, not a sign the team forgot

When you are asked your name, your planned procedure, its side and your medicine allergies more than once on the same day, that repetition is deliberate. Each stage involves different people, different information and different risks, and saying it out loud prevents reliance on memory or on the assumption that someone else already checked. [1, 2]

This is not one hospital's habit. The World Health Organization developed a surgical safety checklist that brings the team together at set pauses before anaesthesia, before the skin incision and before the patient leaves the operating room, and at each pause written items are reviewed aloud. [1, 2]

That is why the same question may come from the admitting nurse, then the anaesthetist, then the surgeon. It is not confusion; it means each person confirms for themselves before the step they are responsible for. [1, 6]

This article explains what the checklist contains, what research has found about its effect, what makes it work in practice and your role in it as a patient. [2, 4]

Where the checklist came from

The WHO introduced the surgical safety checklist as part of an initiative called Safe Surgery Saves Lives, aiming to reduce avoidable complications and deaths from surgery around the world. [2]

The checklist is deliberately short and divided into three phases: one before anaesthesia, one before the skin incision and one before the patient leaves the operating room. Each phase has a few specific items spoken aloud. [1, 2]

The WHO encourages hospitals to adapt the checklist to their own setting, adding items or changing the order, without removing the core safety steps. That is why the format may look a little different from one hospital to another. [1, 2]

The checklist is not designed to cover everything in an operation. It is a tool that secures a minimum set of important steps, while the other procedures for sterility, anaesthesia and nursing continue alongside it as usual. [1, 2]

The core idea is that critical steps are not left to memory or to the assumption that someone must have done them, and that anyone on the team can stop the step if something is not right. [2, 6]

Before anaesthesia: confirming the basics

Before anaesthesia starts, the team confirms your identity, the planned procedure, the site and that you have signed the consent. If the operation has a right and left side, the site may be marked with a pen beforehand. [1, 2]

They ask again about medicine allergies and confirm that the anaesthesia equipment has been checked and that the oxygen monitor is attached and working. These steps happen before you are asleep, while you can still answer and correct. [1, 2]

The team also asks itself about expected risks: is a difficult airway or a risk of aspiration expected? Is there a chance of major blood loss that needs fluids or blood prepared? These questions prepare the team before a problem happens rather than after. [1, 2]

The principle is simple: do not enter a stage that is hard to reverse until the key information is clear. If you hear something wrong or incomplete, say so, even if you answered the same question earlier. [1, 6]

Before the incision: the whole team pauses

This pause happens while you are asleep, and the surgeon, anaesthetist and nursing staff take part together. Each team member introduces themselves by name and role, which makes it easier for anyone to speak up if they see a problem. [1, 2]

They then reconfirm the patient, procedure and site, check that preventive antibiotics were given at the right time if needed, and confirm that essential imaging is displayed and ready if the operation needs it. [1, 2]

Each discipline states what it expects: the surgeon describes the critical steps, expected duration and possible blood loss, the anaesthetist raises any patient-specific concerns, and nursing confirms sterility and equipment readiness. [1, 2]

Research shows this pause should not be a rapid recitation by one person. Attention, team participation and how the pause is carried out are what make the difference, and a signed form alone does not prove a useful conversation took place. [4, 8]

Before leaving the operating room

The third phase happens before the patient leaves the operating room. The nurse confirms aloud the name of the procedure actually performed, because it sometimes differs from the plan if the surgeon found something unexpected. [1, 2]

The team confirms that the counts of instruments, swabs and needles are correct, so nothing is left inside the body, and that any specimen is labelled with the correct patient name before it goes to the laboratory. [1, 2]

Any equipment problem during the operation is recorded so it can be fixed before anyone else uses it. This is part of safety for the patients who come after you too, because equipment that failed once may fail again in a later operation if nobody reports it. [1]

Finally, the surgeon, anaesthetist and nursing staff review together the key points that need attention in recovery and after the operation, so the team receiving the patient knows what to focus on. [1, 2]

What the research found

The best-known study of the checklist was published in 2009 and took place in eight hospitals in eight cities around the world, in both high-income and lower-income countries. The researchers compared more than 3,700 patients before the checklist was introduced with about 3,900 patients afterwards. [3]

In that study, the death rate after surgery fell from 1.5% to 0.8%, and the rate of inpatient complications fell from 11% to 7%. That is a large difference, but the study compared before and after rather than being a randomised trial, so other factors may have contributed to the improvement. [3]

Systematic reviews that later pooled many studies found the checklist is often associated with fewer complications and better team communication, but the results are not the same everywhere, and the effect depends heavily on how it is implemented. [4, 5, 8]

A study from Finland asked operating teams for their views, and many felt the checklist improved patient safety and communication in the operating room. So the benefit is not only in the numbers but also in the team feeling able to speak up. [6]

Keep in mind that these percentages are averages for many patients in different hospitals, not a figure for you personally. The risk of any operation varies with its type and your health, and your surgeon and anaesthetist are the ones who can explain the risks in your own case. [3, 5]

Why how it is used matters more than the paper

The checklist is not magic. If it is treated as a form that simply has to be signed, or read quickly while everyone is busy with something else, it loses much of its value. [4, 7]

Research on barriers found that time pressure, hierarchy that makes junior staff hesitant to speak, lack of training and a feeling among some staff that the checklist is pointless repetition all reduce compliance. In contrast, leadership support, training and whole-team participation increase it. [7]

The way the checklist is introduced into a hospital also matters. Research shows that training, adapting the checklist to the local setting and having champions within the team raise the rate of proper use. [9]

That is why many hospitals run periodic audits to see whether the checklist is completed in full or whether some items are skipped. These audits help the team find the steps that need improvement instead of assuming everything is fine. [10]

Your role as a patient

You are part of this safety system. State your full name, the agreed procedure, the side and any allergies clearly every time you are asked. If what you hear does not match your understanding, stop and ask before anything continues. [1, 2]

Bring an up-to-date list of all your medicines and supplements, and tell the team about any change in your health since the operation was planned, such as a cold, a fever or a new medicine. [2, 6]

The checklist does not guarantee an operation without complications, but it reduces avoidable errors and supports communication. Repeated questions should not be read as doubt or disorder; they are a sign the team is working to a system. [3, 4]

Remember that the checklist confirms you have signed the consent, but understanding the operation itself happens earlier, in your conversation with the surgeon. Ask about the reason for the operation, the alternatives, the expected risks and when you will go home before you sign, and do not hesitate to ask for another explanation if something is unclear. [1, 2]

If you are having gallbladder surgery, read our gallbladder operation-day guide. If you have a bulge in the abdomen, read the abdominal-wall hernia symptoms guide and abdominal hernia warning signs. If you need a surgical assessment, you can book an appointment with the surgeon. [1, 2]
