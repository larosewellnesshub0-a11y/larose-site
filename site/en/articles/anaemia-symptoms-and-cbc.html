Anaemia symptoms and CBC results

What anaemia means, and why it is not a final diagnosis

Anaemia means that the concentration of haemoglobin in the blood is below the appropriate threshold for that person. Haemoglobin is the protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues. When it falls, a person may experience tiredness, weakness, dizziness, headache, palpitations or breathlessness on exertion. These symptoms are nonspecific, however, and mild anaemia may produce no obvious symptoms. Fatigue alone cannot diagnose anaemia; a blood count is the starting point [1, 2].

Anaemia is also not one disease and is not automatically the same as iron deficiency. The body may lose blood, make too few red cells, or destroy them faster than they can be replaced. Possible causes include iron, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, inflammation or chronic disease, kidney disease, bleeding, inherited haemoglobin disorders, and bone-marrow conditions. More than one factor can coexist, so the task is to identify the pattern and its cause rather than treat an isolated number [1, 4].

There is no single haemoglobin reference threshold for every person. Age, sex, pregnancy, altitude, smoking and laboratory method can all affect interpretation. Use the interval printed on the report, but do not rely on it alone when pregnancy, chronic illness or marked symptoms are present. A clinician relates the number to the clinical context and earlier results to decide whether the fall is new, stable or changing quickly [1, 3].

How to read Hb, MCV and the rest of the CBC as one pattern

Start with haemoglobin (Hb), the main measurement used to establish whether anaemia is present. Haematocrit (Hct) describes the proportion of blood occupied by red cells, while the red-cell count adds information but is not a substitute for haemoglobin. Dehydration or excess body fluid can alter measured concentrations, so an unexpected isolated result may need repeating at a time chosen by the clinician rather than prompting an immediate conclusion [3, 10].

Next consider mean corpuscular volume (MCV). A value below the laboratory interval describes a microcytic pattern, one within it a normocytic pattern, and one above it a macrocytic pattern. This classification narrows possible causes but does not make the diagnosis. MCH and MCHC describe the amount and concentration of haemoglobin within a cell; RDW reflects variation in cell size. A clinician may add a reticulocyte count, because these young red cells indicate whether the marrow is responding or production is reduced [3, 10, 13].

MCV must not be interpreted alone. Early iron deficiency can remain normocytic, anaemia of inflammation can be normocytic or microcytic, and combined iron and B12 deficiencies can produce an apparently average MCV despite two problems. Vitamin B12 deficiency cannot be excluded simply because MCV is not high. Trends, a blood film, reticulocytes and selected follow-up tests carry more information than one box on the report [10, 12].

Low MCV: iron deficiency matters, but it is not the only possibility

A microcytic pattern commonly occurs with iron deficiency, particularly with heavy periods, pregnancy, frequent blood donation, a restricted diet, impaired absorption or gastrointestinal blood loss. A low MCV alone does not prove depleted iron stores. Thalassaemia and other haemoglobin disorders, and sometimes chronic inflammation, can produce a similar pattern. Starting iron from the shape of the CBC alone can therefore delay identification of the real cause [4, 5, 8].

Assessment commonly includes ferritin and, depending on the history, transferrin saturation and inflammatory markers. Low ferritin supports depleted stores, but a normal or high result does not always exclude deficiency during inflammation because ferritin can rise as part of the inflammatory response. The red-cell count, RDW, family history and ancestry may raise suspicion of an inherited disorder, but diagnosis requires the appropriate test rather than an online calculation [5, 8, 13].

When iron deficiency is confirmed, a more important question remains: why did it happen? Blood loss from heavy menstruation or the gastrointestinal tract, impaired absorption, increased requirements and insufficient intake are different pathways. Replacing iron without investigating the cause may improve a number temporarily while the problem continues. The separate guide to low ferritin with a normal CBC explains the stage that can precede anaemia; this article is concerned with established anaemia and its CBC pattern. Do not start an iron supplement solely because of fatigue or a low MCV before appropriate assessment [5, 8].

Normal MCV: the CBC can still show anaemia

A normal MCV does not cancel anaemia when haemoglobin is low. This pattern can occur with recent blood loss, inflammation or chronic disease, kidney disease, red-cell destruction, a bone-marrow problem, or early iron deficiency. Two causes with opposite effects on cell size can also produce a normal average. A report where “everything is normal except Hb” needs interpretation, not dismissal [4, 11, 13].

The reticulocyte count is useful in this setting. A raised count may show that the marrow is trying to compensate for loss or destruction, whereas a low response relative to the degree of anaemia points more toward reduced production. Kidney function matters because the kidneys produce erythropoietin, a hormone that stimulates the marrow. Chronic inflammation can also restrict iron availability and suppress red-cell production even when apparent stores are not low [11, 13].

The clinician selects tests from the history: kidney function, inflammatory markers, a blood film, bilirubin, LDH and other haemolysis tests, or further targeted investigations when a clue is present. Anaemia accompanied by low white cells or platelets, or unusual blood-film findings, can change the priority and warrant specialist assessment. The aim is not to order every test at once, but to let each step answer a defined question and guide the next one [3, 13].

High MCV: B12 and folate are part of the picture, not all of it

A macrocytic pattern can result from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, but it may also be associated with certain medicines, alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, a reticulocyte rise after bleeding or haemolysis, and bone-marrow disorders. Reviewing medicines and supplements is essential. It is unsafe to reduce every high MCV result to a B12 injection without identifying the cause [6, 7, 9].

Vitamin B12 deficiency deserves particular attention because numbness, pins and needles, balance difficulty or cognitive changes can occur without obvious anaemia or a raised MCV. A normal MCV is not sufficient to exclude it. Folate and B12 deficiencies can create similar blood findings, but giving folate without excluding B12 deficiency may improve the blood picture while neurological injury continues. The clinician therefore chooses testing and treatment from symptoms and risk factors, not cell size alone [6, 7, 12].

Risk factors for B12 deficiency include a plant-based diet without a reliable source, pernicious anaemia, stomach or bowel surgery, conditions that impair absorption, and prolonged use of some medicines. Folate deficiency has a different set of causes, including insufficient intake, increased need and certain medicines. The plant-based B12 guide covers dietary planning; the key point here is that the cause must be identified before the form and route of replacement are chosen [6, 7, 9].

Which questions and tests help identify the cause?

A useful assessment begins before any test bundle. The clinician asks when fatigue began and whether there is visible bleeding, heavier or longer periods, black stool, abdominal pain, changed bowel habits, unintended weight loss, fever, night sweats, recent infection, pregnancy, blood donation or surgery. Diet, kidney, liver and thyroid disease, family history, and every medicine are parts of the same history [3, 4, 14].

After examination and the CBC, testing is selected from the pattern. It may include ferritin and transferrin saturation, B12 and folate, reticulocytes and a blood film, kidney, liver and thyroid tests, or markers of inflammation and red-cell destruction. If blood loss is possible, the clinician looks for its source and decides whether gastrointestinal investigation, gynaecological assessment or another test is appropriate. Not everyone needs the same list, and not every anaemia requires endoscopy or bone-marrow testing [3, 5, 11, 14].

Bring the complete report and any older results, and list every medicine and supplement with the amount taken. Do not start iron, B12 or folate before the decision has a clear basis; a supplement can alter test results, cause adverse effects or conceal part of the problem without treating its cause. The guide to targeted supplements explains ingredient and interaction checks, but it cannot replace diagnosis of the anaemia type [5, 6, 7].

When anaemia is urgent, and what to do next

Seek urgent care for chest pain, severe breathlessness or breathlessness at rest, fainting, confusion, a persistently rapid heartbeat with dizziness, rapidly worsening weakness, or obvious bleeding. Vomiting blood or material that resembles coffee grounds, black tarry stool, or blood in the stool with dizziness or fainting should not wait for a routine appointment. Pregnancy, heart or lung disease, and continuing blood loss can make assessment more urgent according to the circumstances [2, 11, 14].

If symptoms are stable, arrange a timely review and bring the complete CBC, older results, and a list of medicines and supplements. Do not judge severity from an Hb number posted online; the speed of decline, cause, symptoms and overall condition all change the decision. Do not stop a prescribed medicine or start a supplement dose on your own. The treating clinician decides whether the count should be repeated, targeted tests are needed, or another specialty should be involved [3, 4].

The medical team at La Rose can review symptoms and the CBC within the wider health context and choose the appropriate diagnostic next step without assuming that every anaemia is iron deficiency.
