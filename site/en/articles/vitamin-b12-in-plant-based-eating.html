Vitamin B12 for plant-based eating: plan a reliable source

Published: 25 August 2026 8 min read Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

Why B12 needs particular attention

The US National Institutes of Health fact sheet explains that natural vitamin B12 sources are animal foods, while plant foods contain it only when fortified. [1] A fully vegan pattern therefore needs a reliable, continuing source from fortified food or an appropriate supplement; adding more vegetables and pulses alone does not solve this particular gap.

Deficiency may appear as fatigue or anaemia and can affect the nervous system even without obvious anaemia. The position of specialist medical bodies is clear: a well-planned vegetarian diet, including a fully vegan one, is healthful and appropriate for every life stage, including pregnancy, breastfeeding and childhood, provided there is a reliable vitamin B12 source. [2] This is not an argument against plant-based eating; it is a well-established part of planning it safely.

It is also worth distinguishing between patterns: a lacto-ovo vegetarian diet that includes eggs and dairy provides less B12 than a diet with meat and fish, but not zero, unlike a fully vegan diet — meaning the risk is higher than an omnivore's but lower than a fully vegan person's. Even so, a lacto-ovo vegetarian still needs to confirm that their actual egg and dairy intake is sufficient, rather than assuming it by default.

Vitamin B12 is the plant-based exception that cannot be left to plant variety alone because reliable natural sources are mainly animal foods. A vegan needs fortified foods with a clear label amount or a suitable supplement; someone who eats eggs or dairy may still have an inconsistent intake when amounts are small [1, 6, 9].

Daily amounts and practical fortified sources

The reference daily intake for adults is around 2.4 micrograms, rising to about 2.6 micrograms in pregnancy and 2.8 micrograms in breastfeeding, according to the US National Institutes of Health fact sheet. [1] This reference applies to someone without an absorption problem; someone with a risk factor such as previous bariatric surgery or long-term metformin may need different monitoring and doses set by a clinician.

Practical fortified sources include fortified plant milk, fortified breakfast cereal, and some brands of nutritional yeast fortified with B12 (plain, unfortified nutritional yeast does not reliably contain it). Always read the label rather than assuming. If testing confirms a genuine deficiency, treatment may be higher-dose tablets or injections depending on the severity of deficiency and the cause of poor absorption, and this is a medical decision, not a personal choice made at the pharmacy counter.

Pregnancy, breastfeeding and infancy need extra planning

The placenta transfers vitamin B12 to the fetus, and after birth the infant depends on hepatic stores from birth plus breast milk content. If the mother follows a fully vegan diet, or a vegetarian diet without a reliable B12 source, the infant's stores are lower and breast milk content is inadequate, even when the mother herself has no obvious symptoms. [3]

Symptoms of infant B12 deficiency typically appear between two and twelve months of age and include vomiting, lethargy, failure to gain weight, low muscle tone, and regression of developmental skills such as sitting or babbling. Early diagnosis matters greatly because treatment corrects the metabolic problem quickly, but delay can leave a partially irreversible neurological effect. [3]

This is why vitamin B12 for a pregnant or breastfeeding person on a fully vegan diet is not a luxury or purely personal choice; it is part of the infant's own health follow-up. If your diet is vegetarian or vegan and you are pregnant or breastfeeding, discuss a clear B12 source with the medical team early in pregnancy, not after a problem appears.

Attention does not stop after breastfeeding ends. A child who grows up on a fully vegan diet after weaning still needs a reliable, continuing B12 source through every stage of growth, not only in the first year; the amount needed changes with age, but the need itself does not disappear. If a child has started complementary feeding, a reliable B12 source needs to continue within the new eating plan, not stop once solid foods are introduced.

Other causes of B12 deficiency beyond a plant-based diet

Diet is not the only cause. Bariatric surgery changes vitamin B12 absorption, and international guidance recommends lifelong vitamin and mineral monitoring and supplementation after these procedures, regardless of diet type. [4] A history of bariatric surgery is an additional risk factor worth following up even if meals include meat and dairy.

Metformin, very commonly used to treat type 2 diabetes, is linked to B12 deficiency with long-term use, and the American Diabetes Association recommends periodic testing of vitamin B12 concentrations for patients on metformin, often every two to three years. [5] Other medicines, such as proton pump inhibitors used for acid reflux, and some stomach or bowel conditions, can also reduce absorption. Comprehensive diabetes follow-up takes this into account rather than relying on HbA1c alone.

Chronic gastritis, including that caused by long-standing untreated H. pylori infection, can reduce secretion of the intrinsic factor needed to absorb B12 from the gut — a medical cause entirely separate from diet type. Older adults are more prone to reduced B12 absorption even on a diet that includes meat, because of age-related changes in the stomach. The useful question is therefore not only 'do you eat meat?' but 'do you have any of these: a fully vegan diet, bariatric surgery, long-term metformin, a stomach or bowel condition, or older age?'

One blood test is not always enough; how B12 is actually followed

A blood B12 test is the starting point, but a borderline result may not tell the whole story. In these cases, a clinician may request additional tests such as methylmalonic acid or homocysteine, because these rise more clearly when there is genuine tissue-level deficiency, even if the B12 number itself looks relatively normal. [3] This is why a 'normal B12 number' does not always close the question if symptoms continue and risk factors are present.

Neurological symptoms can precede anaemia or appear without it at all: numbness or tingling in the limbs, balance problems, difficulty concentrating, or mood changes. These deserve assessment, not just fatigue or pallor. If a supplement is started based on a diagnosis, follow-up determines whether the dose is adequate and whether the underlying cause — diet, medicine, or an absorption problem — needs separate attention.

Testing should be done before replacement when the clinical situation allows because a supplement can raise the result and obscure the cause. A borderline result is not always a final answer; the clinician may relate it to the blood count and symptoms and use a marker such as methylmalonic acid when appropriate. Clear neurological symptoms should not be left waiting for every result [6, 7].

How to read a product and build a reliable routine

Read the vitamin B12 line itself, not the words “vegan” or “nerve support” on the front. Fortified foods vary widely in amount and serving size, so count the portions actually consumed rather than assuming that owning the product is enough. Algae, spirulina and fermented foods are not reliable substitutes for a proven B12 source, and some tests may count inactive analogues as if they were useful vitamin [6, 8].

Write the practical plan simply: source name, label amount, frequency and any B12 already present in other products. Do not stack several supplements without review because different products may contain the same nutrient. If the diet continues to exclude animal sources, the planned source also needs to continue; treatment of confirmed deficiency or malabsorption is a clinical decision, not a shelf choice [6, 7].

Plan the source instead of guessing

Check the labels of fortified foods and supplements to confirm that they actually contain B12; do not rely on a product name or appearance. Dose and route vary with age, diet, confirmed deficiency, stomach or bowel conditions and previous surgery.

Seek an assessment if you have numbness, weakness, balance problems, unexplained anaemia, or a fully vegan diet without a reliable source. Bring a food and supplement list to the visit so the plan reflects what you actually take, not an assumed diet.

The goal is a known source and a clear review plan, not a random dose or reliance on a product name [6, 7].

If you need to connect this information with your health history and current plan, you can book an assessment and review the decision with the clinician or dietitian.
