H. pylori, reflux or IBS? How to separate heartburn, bloating and bowel symptoms

Published: 1 September 2026 13 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Start with the pattern, not a disease name

When someone says “my stomach” or “my colon,” they may mean burning in the chest, upper-abdominal pain, fullness after food, gas, bloating, constipation or diarrhoea. These symptoms overlap, and more than one problem can coexist. There is therefore no single symptom that safely separates H. pylori infection, reflux and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). [1, 2, 3]

A more useful starting question is where the sensation is, when it happens, and whether it relates to lying down, a meal or opening the bowels. Note new medicines and supplements, and any clear change in weight or stool. That story helps a clinician select the smallest set of tests that answers a real question instead of a random test or pharmacy treatment experiment. [2, 3, 7]

When is H. pylori the useful question?

H. pylori is a bacterium that can cause inflammation or ulcers in the stomach or duodenum, although many infected people have no symptoms. Upper-abdominal pain or burning, nausea and fullness can make infection a reasonable question, but they do not prove it because the same symptoms can occur with dyspepsia and other causes. [1, 4, 7]

When the history fits, a clinician may use a urea breath test or stool-antigen test. Antibiotics, bismuth and acid-suppressing medicines can make results inaccurate; do not stop treatment independently, but ask the service ordering the test about preparation and timing. Diagnosing and treating infection follows a different pathway from treating bloating alone. [1, 4, 7]

When does the pattern resemble reflux?

Reflux means stomach contents move back into the oesophagus. Burning behind the breastbone, a sour or bitter taste, or regurgitation, particularly after a large meal or when lying down, can fit that pattern. Cough or hoarseness can occur, but neither proves reflux by itself because both have several causes. [2, 5, 7]

Smaller meals, noticing what repeatedly triggers symptoms, and avoiding lying down after a large meal may help some people. Temporary relief from an antacid does not establish the diagnosis, however, and long-term medicine use should not become a plan without review. Progressively difficult swallowing or new severe chest pain should not automatically be labelled heartburn. [2, 5, 7]

When can the pattern fit IBS?

IBS is a disorder of gut–brain interaction, not a label for every episode of gas or bloating. The pattern clinicians look for includes recurrent abdominal pain related to bowel movements or a change in stool frequency or form; constipation, diarrhoea or both may predominate. Symptoms are real even when another test does not show inflammation or an ulcer. [3, 6, 9]

Bloating alone, or two days of abdominal pain, is not enough to diagnose IBS. A short record of pain timing, stool form and frequency, sleep, stress and medicines is more useful than removing many foods at once. Food can be a factor for some people, but it is not a home test that establishes one particular cause. [3, 6, 9]

Bloating is a clue that needs context, not a diagnosis

Gas and bloating can be influenced by eating pattern, constipation, certain foods or drinks, individual intolerance, medicines and several digestive disorders. Their presence with burning does not prove H. pylori, and their presence with altered bowel habits does not prove IBS. What matters is what happened before the symptom, what accompanies it and what has changed from usual. [3, 8, 10]

Do not start a gluten-free diet simply for bloating if coeliac disease is a possible question; changing the diet before testing can make results harder to interpret. Equally, relief after removing one food for a day or two does not establish an allergy or make that food the only cause. If a trial is appropriate, it should be defined and time-limited within a clear plan. [3, 9, 10]

Every test should answer a different question

There is no single “colon test,” and no ultrasound identifies the cause of every episode of bloating. An H. pylori test looks for a specific infection under specific preparation conditions. Blood or stool tests may be useful when the pattern suggests inflammation, anaemia or another cause. The decision about endoscopy or imaging depends on the clinical question, age, warning features and history, not anxiety alone. [1, 3, 6, 7]

Ultrasound can be appropriate for the liver, gallbladder, kidneys or another abdominal structure, but it does not diagnose reflux or IBS by itself. Conversely, a normal report does not mean symptoms are unreal or remove the need to review a persistent pattern. The value of a result comes from connecting it to the question it was ordered to answer. [3, 6, 7]

What should you record before a review?

You do not need a complicated diary. Record where pain or burning occurs, when it comes, its relation to food, lying down or bowel movements, and whether it wakes you at night. Note stool form and frequency, bloating, vomiting if present, and medicine and supplement names with doses. Bring earlier tests or reports rather than relying on memory. [2, 3, 6]

Those details do not ask you to diagnose yourself. They help a clinician distinguish a question needing an H. pylori test from a reflux pattern, a bowel-motility issue or a reason for broader assessment. Report pregnancy, earlier surgery and any family history of bowel cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or coeliac disease when relevant. [1, 3, 6, 9]

Calm steps while the plan becomes clear

When warning features are absent, structuring meals may be more useful than starving yourself or removing broad food groups: eat slowly, notice large meals or timing, keep appropriate fluids and pay attention to constipation. The goal is to identify a pattern worth discussing, not to test ten treatments and foods in one week. [2, 3, 9, 10]

Do not start antibiotics from an old prescription or repeat an H. pylori course because bloating continues. Do not stop acid medicine before a possible test without specific instructions; changing it can affect accuracy or your comfort. When symptoms recur or affect eating or sleep, arrange review rather than extending guesswork. [1, 2, 4, 7]

When do you need faster assessment?

Seek timely medical assessment when symptoms persist or clearly change, or when there is difficult swallowing, repeated vomiting, unexplained anaemia, unintentional weight loss or meaningful family history. These features do not diagnose one disease from an article, but they make it unsafe to apply an IBS or heartburn label without review. [2, 3, 7, 9]

Seek urgent care for new or severe chest pain, especially with breathlessness, sweating or spread to the arm or jaw; for vomiting blood, black stool, visible bleeding, severe constant abdominal pain, fainting or inability to keep fluids down. Do not wait to try a diet or for a routine test appointment with these signs. [2, 5, 7]

The next step should be a defined question

A useful review does not end with a broad label such as “stomach” or “colon.” Ask which pattern seems closest in your case, which feature could change the plan, and whether the proposed test answers a defined question. The next step may be properly prepared H. pylori testing, a reflux plan, assessment of an IBS pattern or another investigation when the story differs. [1, 2, 3, 7]

The goal is not to chase a disease name online. It is to reach a practical, safe explanation and a clear follow-up: what to try, when to review and when to make earlier contact if symptoms change. In this way, bloating or burning becomes useful information for the clinician rather than a reason to impose one treatment on different causes. [1, 2, 3]
