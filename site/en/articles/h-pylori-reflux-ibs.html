H. pylori, reflux or IBS? How to separate heartburn, bloating and bowel symptoms

Published: 1 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Start with the pattern, not a disease name

When you say “my stomach” or “my colon,” you may be describing very different things: burning behind the breastbone, pain or burning high in the abdomen, early fullness, nausea, gas, bloating, constipation or diarrhoea. These symptoms overlap, and more than one condition can be present at the same time. No single symptom therefore proves H. pylori infection, reflux or irritable bowel syndrome without an appropriate history and assessment. [2, 3, 5, 6]

Begin with three simple questions: exactly where is the sensation, when does it appear, and how does it relate to food, sleep or a bowel movement? Burning that rises into the chest or throat differs from upper-abdominal pain, and both differ from lower-abdominal cramping that changes after opening the bowels. Also record stool form and frequency, new medicines and any painkillers you use; these details help a clinician select a defined question instead of ordering random tests. [4, 6, 7, 9]

Treat this article as a sorting map, not a self-diagnosis test. A pattern guides probability, but diagnosis also depends on your age, symptom duration, medical and family history, medicines and warning features. Even when one disease name seems to fit, individual decisions and treatment belong with the clinician caring for you. [2, 3, 8]

When is H. pylori a reasonable question?

H. pylori is a bacterium that lives in the stomach lining and can be associated with gastritis or an ulcer in the stomach or duodenum. Its presence does not mean every episode of bloating or heartburn comes from it, and some people with infection have no clear symptoms. Upper-abdominal pain or burning, early satiety, post-meal fullness, nausea or an ulcer history can make H. pylori a reasonable question, but symptoms alone do not confirm infection. [1, 4, 8]

If the history supports testing, a clinician may choose a urea breath test or stool-antigen test, and sometimes a biopsy during endoscopy for a defined clinical reason. A blood antibody test does not always distinguish current from past infection, so test selection matters. Proton-pump inhibitors, antibiotics and bismuth can also affect the accuracy of some tests; do not stop treatment on your own, and obtain preparation and timing instructions from the clinician or laboratory. [1, 4, 7]

If the result is positive, treatment is not a standard course to repeat whenever bloating returns. Regimen choice depends on earlier treatment, medicine allergy, antibiotic resistance and individual factors, and treatment should be followed by a properly timed plan to confirm eradication. Our page on H. pylori diagnosis and treatment explains that pathway in detail without turning this comparison into a general prescription. [1, 4]

When is the pattern closer to reflux?

Reflux occurs when stomach contents move back into the oesophagus. A typical pattern may include burning behind the breastbone or regurgitation of liquid or food with a sour or bitter taste, particularly after a large meal, bending or lying down. Some people have chest pain, nausea or swallowing difficulty, and cough or hoarseness can occur, but these symptoms have other causes and do not establish reflux by themselves. [2, 5]

Location and direction help: burning that rises toward the throat is closer to a reflux pattern than lower-abdominal bloating that changes after a bowel movement. New chest pain should never automatically be labelled heartburn, especially when it is severe or accompanied by breathlessness, sweating or spread to the arm or jaw. Progressive swallowing difficulty, persistent vomiting, bleeding or weight loss also require medical assessment rather than a self-directed medicine trial. [2, 5, 7]

Smaller meals, avoiding lying down immediately after food and noticing repeatable triggers may help some people. A response to an antacid can form part of the history, but it is not a diagnostic test. If you have used treatment for a while, neither stop it abruptly nor continue indefinitely without review; read about long-term reflux medicine use and discuss the reason for treatment, the lowest effective dose and follow-up plan with your clinician. [2, 7]

When should an IBS pattern be considered?

Irritable bowel syndrome is a disorder of gut–brain interaction, not a name for every episode of gas or cramping. Its core pattern involves recurrent abdominal pain related to bowel movements or accompanied by a change in stool frequency or form. Constipation may predominate, diarrhoea may predominate, or the two may alternate. Bloating is common and food may worsen symptoms, but two days of bloating or one episode of relief after a bowel movement is not enough for diagnosis. [3, 6, 9]

A clinician makes a positive diagnosis from the pattern and asks about duration, onset, triggers and the effect on daily life. Depending on the story, selected tests may help exclude other conditions, such as a full blood count, inflammatory markers or coeliac testing. Not everyone needs endoscopy or ultrasound to prove IBS, and a normal result does not make pain unreal; each result is interpreted with the whole history. [3, 6, 9]

For two weeks or longer, record pain timing, bowel movements and stool form, bloating, meals, sleep and medicines without changing ten variables at once. This can reveal a pattern worth discussing and help the clinician separate IBS from other causes. Because warning features change the pathway, our page on IBS symptoms and warning signs explains when the pattern fits and when broader assessment is needed. [3, 6, 9]

Why can heartburn and bloating be misleading?

Bloating is a sensation of fullness or pressure and may occur with visible abdominal distension or without it. Eating behaviour, swallowed air, some carbohydrates, constipation, bowel motility, medicines and different digestive conditions can all contribute. Bloating with heartburn therefore does not prove H. pylori, and bloating with diarrhoea does not automatically prove IBS. [6, 9, 10]

Functional dyspepsia is another possibility: upper-abdominal pain or burning, early satiety or uncomfortable fullness after eating without a structural cause found after appropriate assessment. Its symptoms can overlap with reflux or IBS, while H. pylori may remain a separate question. This helps explain why finding one name for every sensation often fails and why temporary improvement after removing a food or taking a medicine is not final proof. [7, 8]

If bloating is the dominant symptom, note whether it builds through the day, accompanies constipation or a persistent bowel change, and involves visible distension or localised pain. Do not start a highly restrictive diet or remove gluten before discussing testing when coeliac disease is a possibility. For the symptom’s wider assessment, our page on persistent bloating and when to see a doctor covers that scope without repeating it here. [6, 9, 10]

The right test answers a defined question

There is no single “stomach and colon test” that separates all three conditions. A urea breath or stool-antigen test asks about active H. pylori infection. Reflux diagnosis often begins with symptoms and history, while selected situations may require endoscopy or reflux monitoring. IBS diagnosis depends on a symptom pattern with targeted tests according to the story, not on an ultrasound that proves abnormal bowel function. [2, 4, 6, 9]

Endoscopy is not automatic for every episode of heartburn, and ultrasound may be useful for a question about the liver, gallbladder or kidneys but is not a test that establishes reflux or IBS. The clinician decides whether testing is needed from age, symptom onset and persistence, examination, family history and warning features. The aim is not to perform the largest number of tests; each test should change a decision or exclude an important possibility. [2, 6, 7, 8]

Before review, write down medicine and supplement names and doses, especially painkillers, acid medicines and recent antibiotics. Bring older reports and results instead of repeating them without a reason, and mention earlier surgery or a family history of ulcer, bleeding, inflammatory bowel disease or coeliac disease. If H. pylori testing is possible, ask about preparation before changing a medicine; incorrect timing can produce a misleading result. [1, 4, 7]

What can you do now, and when should you not wait?

When no warning feature is present, keep each change simple and measurable: try smaller meals if large meals worsen burning, leave enough time between eating and sleep, eat more slowly, maintain appropriate fluids and address constipation. Change one variable at a time and record the result. Do not start antibiotics from an old prescription, repeat H. pylori treatment because bloating persists, or remove many food groups without a plan and follow-up. [1, 7, 9, 10]

Arrange timely assessment when symptoms persist or clearly change, or when there is difficult or painful swallowing, repeated vomiting, appetite loss, unintentional weight loss, unexplained anaemia, bleeding, important family history, or new symptoms unlike your usual pattern. These features do not automatically mean a serious disease, but they do mean that labelling the problem “IBS” or “heartburn” without review is unsafe. [5, 7, 8, 9]

Seek urgent care for new or severe chest pain, particularly with breathlessness, sweating or spread to the arm or jaw; vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds; black stool or visible bleeding; severe constant abdominal pain; fainting; or inability to keep fluids down. Do not wait for a diet trial or routine appointment when these symptoms are present. [4, 5]

Make the next step specific

At review, go beyond a broad description such as “my stomach is upset.” Explain the symptom’s location, duration and frequency, what starts or relieves it, whether bowel habits changed, and whether it wakes you. Ask the clinician: which pattern is closest, what other important possibilities remain, will the proposed test change the plan, and which new feature should prompt earlier contact? [2, 3, 6, 8]

The appropriate next step may be correctly prepared H. pylori testing, a defined reflux plan, a positive assessment of an IBS pattern, or a different investigation because the story does not fit any of the three. A useful outcome is more than a disease name: it is an understandable explanation, a treatment trial suited to your situation and a clear time to review the response. Decisions about medicines, endoscopy or a therapeutic diet should be made with the treating clinician. [1, 2, 3, 7]

If symptoms recur or affect eating, sleep or daily life, you can request a gastroenterology consultation to begin a structured clinical assessment. The clinic is mentioned here only as a practical next step, not as a promise of a diagnosis or outcome; if an urgent feature listed above is present, seek appropriate urgent care rather than waiting for a routine booking. [4, 5, 6]
