Leg and foot swelling: causes and when to worry

Understanding leg swelling: the vital difference between unilateral and bilateral edema

Leg edema means swelling caused by fluid collecting in the tissues under the skin. It is most noticeable in the feet, ankles, and lower legs [1, 2, 8]. Fluid is always moving between the smallest blood vessels and the nearby tissues. Blood pressure inside those vessels pushes some fluid out, while proteins in the blood, especially albumin, help draw fluid back in [2, 8, 9]. Tiny lymph vessels collect much of the fluid that is left and return it to the bloodstream [2, 8, 11]. Swelling develops when more fluid leaves than the veins and lymph system can carry away. This can happen because pressure inside a vein is high, the albumin level is low, lymph drainage is blocked, or inflammation makes small blood vessels leakier [1, 2, 8, 11]. Gravity then makes the fluid more obvious in the lowest parts of the body. That is why shoes may feel tight or the top of the foot and ankle may look puffy.

One of the first questions is whether one leg or both legs are swollen [8, 9, 10, 11]. Swelling in one leg usually points to a problem in that leg. Possible causes include a deep vein thrombosis (DVT), damage to a leg vein, cellulitis (a skin and soft-tissue infection), a Baker cyst that has burst behind the knee, or an injury [8, 9, 10, 11]. Swelling in both legs more often suggests a cause that affects the whole body, such as heart, kidney, or liver disease. Some medicines can also cause it. Long periods of sitting or standing can make swelling from poor vein function worse in both legs [1, 2, 8, 9]. This one-leg-or-two distinction does not give a diagnosis by itself. It helps the doctor decide what to ask, examine, and test first, so an urgent cause is not missed [8, 9, 11].

Sudden one-sided leg swelling: deep vein thrombosis signs and red flags

Sudden swelling in one leg needs prompt medical assessment, especially when the calf also hurts, cramps, or feels unusually full. A deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is the urgent cause that doctors need to rule out [3, 5, 10]. A DVT is a blood clot in one of the deep veins of the leg or thigh. The clot can partly or fully block blood returning toward the heart. Pressure then rises behind the blockage and fluid can collect in the leg [3, 8, 10]. Possible signs include swelling, a deep ache or tenderness, warmth, red or darker skin, and surface veins that look more noticeable than usual. However, symptoms alone cannot confirm or exclude a clot [3, 5, 10].

Doctors use the Wells score to estimate how likely DVT is before choosing the next test [5, 10, 14]. The score considers findings such as active cancer, recent leg paralysis or a plaster cast, being in bed for several days, recent major surgery, tenderness along a deep vein, swelling of the whole leg, one-sided pitting swelling, and a clear difference in calf size. It also considers whether another diagnosis is at least as likely [5, 10]. The score is a guide, not a home test and not proof that a clot is present. Its result helps determine how ultrasound and a D-dimer blood test should be used [5, 10, 14].

A DVT can become dangerous if part of the clot breaks away and travels to the lungs. This is called a pulmonary embolism [3, 5, 10]. Call emergency services or go to an emergency department immediately if leg swelling comes with sudden shortness of breath, chest pain or tightness, coughing blood, a very noticeable fast heartbeat, light-headedness, or fainting [3, 5, 8].

A clot is not the only cause of sudden swelling in one leg. Cellulitis can make an area red, hot, painful, and sometimes cause fever or chills. A Baker cyst behind the knee can burst and release joint fluid into the calf, causing pain and swelling that can look like DVT [8, 9, 11]. Because these conditions can look similar, a clinician uses the history, examination, Wells probability, D-dimer when appropriate, and compression ultrasound in the recommended sequence. A positive D-dimer does not prove DVT, and some people need a repeat scan after an initial negative result [5, 10, 14].

Bilateral leg swelling: systemic causes involving the heart, kidneys, and liver

When both legs are swollen in a similar way, the doctor looks for problems that can affect fluid balance throughout the body. The heart, kidneys, and liver are important possibilities, although they are not the only ones [1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8]. In heart failure, the heart does not pump or fill as well as it should. Pressure can build in the veins, and fluid can collect in the feet and legs [2, 4, 8, 9]. This swelling often leaves a dent when pressed and may become more noticeable later in the day. Other clues include shortness of breath during ordinary activity, trouble breathing while lying flat, waking at night short of breath or coughing, and unusual tiredness [1, 4, 8, 9]. These symptoms need medical assessment because leg swelling alone cannot show whether heart failure is present.

Kidney disease can cause swelling in more than one way. When kidney function falls, the body may have trouble removing extra salt and water. In nephrotic syndrome, damaged kidney filters allow a large amount of protein to pass into the urine [2, 6, 8, 9]. The blood level of albumin then falls. Albumin normally helps keep fluid inside blood vessels, so a low level makes it easier for fluid to move into nearby tissues [6, 8, 9]. This can cause swelling in the legs and ankles. Puffy eyelids, especially on waking, and foamy urine are other possible clues [2, 6, 8].

Cirrhosis, which is long-term scarring of the liver, can also upset fluid balance. Scarring makes it harder for blood to flow through the liver and raises pressure in the portal vein, the vein that carries blood from the digestive organs to the liver. A damaged liver may also make less albumin [2, 7, 8]. Together with the body's response to these changes, this can lead to fluid in the abdomen, called ascites, as well as swelling in both legs [2, 7, 8]. Blood tests, a urine test, and imaging chosen for the person's symptoms help separate these causes. Treating swelling safely depends on finding the cause rather than only trying to remove fluid [7, 8, 9].

Chronic venous insufficiency and varicose veins: when venous valves lose efficiency

Chronic venous insufficiency is a common cause of long-lasting leg swelling, especially in older adults. It is a problem with the leg veins rather than proof of heart, kidney, or liver failure [1, 8, 10, 11, 12]. Leg veins must carry blood upward against gravity. One-way valves help stop blood from falling backward, and the calf muscles squeeze the veins as you walk to help push blood toward the heart [10, 11, 12]. If the valves do not close well, some blood flows backward and pools in the lower leg. This is called venous reflux, and it raises pressure in the veins over time [8, 11, 12].

People may notice a dull ache, heaviness, throbbing, burning, or swelling. Symptoms often become worse after sitting or standing still for a long time and improve after lying down or raising the legs [1, 8, 11, 12]. If high vein pressure continues for years, red blood cells can leak into nearby tissues. Iron left behind as those cells break down can make the skin around the inner ankle look brown or rusty. The skin may also become dry, itchy, inflamed, firm, or thick. In more advanced disease, a sore near the ankle may be slow to heal [8, 11, 12]. These skin changes should be assessed rather than treated only with home creams.

A venous duplex ultrasound uses sound waves to show whether the veins are open and whether blood is flowing backward through faulty valves. It can examine the surface veins, deep veins, and connecting veins [11, 12]. The result helps the clinician choose a plan for the person rather than treating every swollen leg in the same way. Depending on the findings and arterial blood flow, that plan may include regular walking and calf movement, prescribed graduated compression stockings, leg elevation, and careful skin care [8, 11, 12].

Common medications that cause leg swelling: blood pressure drugs and pain relievers

Medicines are a common and sometimes overlooked cause of ankle and leg swelling. The swelling can be worrying, but it does not automatically mean that the heart or kidneys have suddenly failed [8, 9, 13]. Amlodipine and similar calcium channel blockers, which are often used for high blood pressure, are well-known examples [8, 9, 13]. These drugs relax the small arteries that carry blood into the tiny vessels in the tissues more than they relax the small veins that carry it away. The uneven change raises pressure in those tiny vessels, so fluid moves into the tissues around the ankles [8, 13]. This is a local pressure effect, not the same as the whole body holding too much fluid.

That difference matters because adding a strong water pill, such as furosemide, may start an unhelpful prescribing cycle [13]. A diuretic removes salt and water from the bloodstream, but it does not correct the pressure imbalance caused by the calcium channel blocker. It may do little for the ankle swelling while causing dehydration, low blood pressure on standing, low sodium or potassium, or kidney strain [8, 13]. A doctor may instead lower the dose, change the medicine, or consider an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker when that choice is suitable for the person's blood pressure and health [8, 13].

Other medicines can also contribute to leg swelling. They include anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen and diclofenac, corticosteroids, gabapentin or pregabalin for nerve pain, and thiazolidinedione medicines used for diabetes [8, 9, 13]. Some of these medicines make the body retain salt and water, while others cause swelling in a different way. Do not stop or change a prescribed blood pressure, heart, pain, or diabetes medicine by yourself. Ask the prescribing doctor to review when the swelling started, the dose, and your other symptoms. That allows a safer change without leaving the original condition untreated [8, 9, 13].

Pitting versus non-pitting edema: clinical examination and necessary diagnostic tests

During an examination, the doctor may press a finger over the shin or near the inner ankle and then let go [1, 2, 8]. If a dent remains for a while, this is called pitting edema [2, 8, 9]. The dent forms because fluid can move within the tissues. Pitting is often seen with heart failure, kidney or liver disease, high pressure in the leg veins, and swelling caused by some medicines [1, 2, 8, 9]. It is a useful clue, but it does not identify the cause on its own.

If the swollen tissue feels firm and does not leave a dent, it is called non-pitting edema [2, 8, 11]. This pattern is often seen in later lymphedema, when protein-rich lymph fluid has remained in the tissues and the skin and tissue have become thicker. Early lymphedema can still pit, so the finger test cannot rule it out [2, 8, 11]. Lymphedema can be present from birth or develop after lymph-node surgery, damage, or certain long-term infections. A clinician may also check the Stemmer sign by trying to lift a fold of skin at the base of the second toe; being unable to lift it supports lymphedema but is not the only part of the diagnosis [8, 11]. Severe underactive thyroid disease can cause firm, non-pitting swelling and thick, dry skin [8, 9].

The rest of the assessment depends on the history and examination. It may include blood pressure, listening to the heart and lungs, a urine test for protein, kidney tests such as creatinine and estimated GFR, liver tests and albumin, and a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) test. If heart failure is suspected, the doctor may request BNP or NT-proBNP and heart tests. If a vein problem is suspected, a venous duplex ultrasound may be needed [1, 2, 5, 8, 9, 11]. Not everyone needs every test; the pattern of swelling and other symptoms guide the choice.

Safe everyday steps to ease leg swelling: what helps and what causes harm

After a clinician has ruled out an emergency and serious worsening of heart, kidney, or liver disease, simple measures may ease swelling and discomfort. The right advice still depends on the cause [1, 2, 8, 11, 12]:

1. Raise the legs: When resting, support the legs on pillows so they are raised comfortably. Elevation uses gravity to help blood and lymph fluid move away from the lower legs. It can be especially helpful when vein-related swelling gets worse while the legs are down [1, 8, 11]. No single elevation schedule suits everyone, so follow the clinician's advice for your condition.

2. Move regularly: Gentle walking and ankle movements make the calf muscles squeeze the leg veins and help push blood upward [8, 11, 12]. If work involves long periods of sitting or standing, take regular short movement breaks instead of staying still for hours [1, 8, 12]. Choose activity that is safe for your mobility and other health conditions.

3. Use compression only after suitable assessment: Graduated compression stockings are tighter at the ankle and become less tight higher up the leg. This can reduce pooling in people with venous disease [1, 8, 11, 12]. However, compression can be unsafe when the arteries are not carrying enough blood to the foot. A clinician should check for peripheral artery disease and select the right stocking type and pressure before use [8, 11, 12].

4. Limit excess salt: Cutting down on added salt, heavily processed foods, and cured meats may help reduce fluid retention in some heart and kidney conditions [1, 2, 8]. Ask for personal advice if you have kidney disease, heart failure, or another condition that affects diet or fluid intake.

5. Do not start water pills yourself: Non-prescribed diuretics and herbal products may cause dehydration, low sodium or potassium, and kidney problems. They also do not correct local causes such as weak vein valves, lymphedema, or calcium channel blocker swelling [1, 8, 13]. Use a diuretic only when a clinician has found a reason for it and can monitor the response.

When to worry about leg swelling: red flags and clinical evaluation at La Rose

Some patterns of leg swelling need emergency care, while others need a clinic appointment soon [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9]. Go to an emergency department immediately if one leg suddenly becomes swollen and painful, hot, or red, because DVT must be considered. Also seek emergency help if swelling comes with sudden shortness of breath, chest pain or pressure, severe trouble breathing, coughing blood, light-headedness, or fainting. These can be signs of a clot in the lung or a serious heart or lung problem [1, 3, 4, 5, 8].

Arrange a medical appointment soon if swelling in both legs lasts, keeps getting worse, or does not improve with simple measures. Other reasons to book an assessment include less urine or foamy urine, a swollen abdomen, yellow skin or eyes, known heart, kidney, or liver disease, or swelling that began after a new medicine [1, 2, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9]. A sore, cracked area, or ulcer that is not healing also needs professional care, especially if the skin is red, hot, or painful [1, 8, 11, 12].

At La Rose, the internal medicine team can review when the swelling began, whether it affects one leg or both, other symptoms, existing health conditions, and all current medicines. The clinician can then examine you and choose blood tests, a urine test, heart assessment, or imaging based on the likely cause [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 11]. Appointments are available at the Maadi and Fifth Settlement branches. If emergency warning signs are present, go directly to emergency care rather than waiting for a clinic visit.
