Hair loss in women: the causes that matter in assessment

The pattern of hair loss in women is the first clue

Hair loss in women is not a single diagnosis. One woman may notice a gradually widening part, another may suddenly see much more hair in the shower, and another may develop a smooth patch or breakage at the ends. The distinction matters because each pattern raises different possibilities and requires a different assessment. The NHS explains that hair loss may be temporary after illness, stress, weight loss, or iron deficiency, while inherited pattern loss can be persistent [5]. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that overall thinning or a widening part is a common first sign of female pattern hair loss, whereas alopecia areata often begins with a smooth round or oval patch [2, 10]. Rather than counting every hair, watch for meaningful change: Is the part wider? Is the ponytail thinner? Is the scalp visible in one area? Is there itch, scale, or pain? Photograph the front, part, and sides in consistent light each month, and record when change began. Normal hair sheds daily, so one wash-day photograph can magnify anxiety without showing a real trend [5, 6]. Pattern, timing, and examination are the starting points; a shampoo or supplement name does not reveal the cause.

A gradually widening part may be female pattern hair loss

In female pattern hair loss, thinning often appears over the top of the scalp, the part gradually widens, and ponytail density may fall. It is progressive, but it does not mean that a woman will lose all her hair. The AAD describes it as the most common cause of hair loss in women, explains that genes can come from either parent, and stresses diagnosis because other causes can look similar [2]. Family history supports the possibility but is neither required nor conclusive. Female pattern loss may also coexist with telogen shedding after illness or weight change, making progression seem faster. Examination distinguishes miniaturized hairs from diffuse shedding or breakage. If treatment is appropriate, selection depends on diagnosis, overall health, and pregnancy or breastfeeding plans. A clinician may discuss topical treatment, but even non-prescription options have adverse effects and instructions, and some choices are unsuitable during pregnancy or while planning pregnancy [2, 3]. A woman should therefore not copy a friend's treatment or begin hormonal medicine independently. Results are not immediate because the hair cycle is slow, and any plan needs realistic follow-up rather than a promise of a particular density in a short time [2, 3].

Diffuse shedding asks: what happened beforehand?

When more hair sheds diffusely across the scalp, especially after fever, surgery, childbirth, major stress, weight loss, or restricted eating, a clinician may consider telogen effluvium. More follicles enter a resting phase, and shedding is often noticed after a delay rather than on the day of the trigger [4]. Assessment therefore looks back over preceding months, not only the latest week. A scientific review of telogen effluvium includes calorie restriction and severe protein or zinc deficiency among potential triggers [12]. Diffuse shedding may be temporary when the trigger resolves, but persistence or repeated triggers deserve reassessment. The related guide sudden hair shedding and telogen effluvium explains the difference between shedding and ongoing hair loss without replacing examination [4, 12]. It is also unsafe to assume that childbirth or weight change is the only cause in every woman; hereditary thinning, iron deficiency, or a scalp disorder may coexist. Record illness, operations, medicines, weight change, menstrual changes, and diet, and bring earlier photographs where available. This chronology helps the clinician connect an event with a pattern rather than relying on a guess [4, 7].

Associated symptoms should guide testing

Fatigue, pallor, breathlessness, or palpitations may prompt evaluation for iron deficiency or anaemia; the NHS includes hair loss among possible features of iron deficiency [9]. Irregular periods with increased facial hair or acne should be reported because they may support clinician-directed hormonal evaluation, but one symptom alone does not establish a diagnosis. Cold intolerance, weight change, constipation, or other clues may direct thyroid assessment; MedlinePlus lists thyroid disease among causes of hair loss [6, 7]. The important point is that questions and examination select testing rather than every woman receiving the same panel. The related guide blood tests before hair-loss supplements explains why blood count, iron, thyroid, or other tests are requested when clues support them, not simply because someone is worried [3, 7]. Diet, weight change, heavy bleeding, pregnancy and childbirth, and chronic illness also need review. Do not start iron, zinc, or biotin at random; the AAD advises supplementation when testing confirms deficiency because excess can cause harm, and biotin can interfere with some laboratory results [3]. A low or borderline number needs interpretation within the woman's whole clinical picture, and addressing the cause matters more than chasing every number.

Patches, inflammation, and breakage need scalp examination

A smooth round or oval patch that appears suddenly can fit alopecia areata, an immune-mediated disease affecting follicles that may also involve eyebrows, eyelashes, or other body hair [10, 11]. Itching, tingling, or burning can precede a patch, although the skin in a typical patch often lacks scale or swelling [10]. In contrast, redness, scale, pain, discharge, or broken hairs raises concern for inflammation, infection, or another disorder. Shiny skin with loss of follicular openings, or a receding hairline with eyebrow loss, deserves earlier assessment because some scarring alopecias can cause permanent loss if diagnosis is delayed. Breakage is also different from shedding at the root. Heat, chemical straightening, colouring, and ongoing traction can leave hairs of different lengths. Painful or tight styles cause traction alopecia, and the AAD warns that some cases become permanent if traction continues [8]. Reduce traction and heat while awaiting review, but do not treat a routine change as a cure for every pattern. The clinician examines scalp and shafts and may use magnification or targeted testing. A phone photograph is useful for chronology, but cannot diagnose inflammation or scarring by itself [3, 7].

Weight change and medicines are possible contributors, not a verdict

Marked weight loss or reduced intake may precede diffuse shedding, and some medicines are also recognized among causes of hair loss [1, 5, 7]. Yet shedding after a medicine starts does not prove that medicine is the only cause. For weight-loss medicines specifically, the AAD notes reports of thinning and explains that rapid loss or lower protein and micronutrient intake may contribute, while research has not settled whether there is a direct effect [1]. The related article hair shedding during medical weight loss explains how timing, nutrition, and the treatment plan can be reviewed with the clinician [1]. Do not stop prescribed treatment independently or impose more severe restriction to accelerate weight loss. Record every medicine and supplement, start date, change, and onset of shedding. The clinician can assess whether the sequence is plausible and whether alternative or simultaneous causes exist. If a woman has persistent vomiting, cannot eat or drink, or is losing faster than planned, contact with the team should not wait for a routine hair appointment. The aim is general health and a sustainable plan, not sacrificing nutrition for a scale result. At the same time, not every shed during weight management resolves through dietary change; the scalp pattern and examination remain essential [1, 3].

When to book and what assessment involves

Arrange a medical assessment at the clinic when the part is widening, density is clearly declining, or shedding persists without explanation. Bring earlier and current photographs, all medicines and supplements, available results, and notes on menstrual changes, illness, operations, weight, and eating. MedlinePlus explains that careful history and examination of hair and scalp are usually central to diagnosis, after which tests or biopsy are selected if indicated [7]. Treatment depends on the cause, overall health, and pregnancy plans; no single intervention suits every woman [2, 3]. Seek faster assessment for sudden patches, pain or burning, redness or discharge, severe scale, shiny or scarred skin, eyebrow or eyelash loss, or very rapid shedding. Mayo Clinic advises medical review for sudden or patchy loss because it can signal an underlying condition needing treatment [13]. Severe fatigue, fainting, breathlessness, marked palpitations, or heavy bleeding also needs prompt medical assessment for the systemic symptoms. Booking is a step toward diagnosis and follow-up, not a promise of a particular density or fixed recovery date. If symptoms are severe or deterioration is rapid, use an appropriate urgent-care service rather than waiting for a routine appointment [7, 13].
