Gastroscopy vs colonoscopy: what to expect

Published: 11 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Two tests for different areas, not two names for one scope

Gastroscopy, also called upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, uses a thin flexible camera passed through the mouth. It lets the clinician inspect the lining of the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, the first part of the small bowel. Colonoscopy enters through the anus to inspect the rectum and colon and may reach the end of the small bowel when the clinical question requires it. Gastroscopy therefore does not inspect the colon, and colonoscopy does not inspect inside the stomach. [1, 2, 3, 4]

The main difference is not that one test is larger or more important. Each answers questions in a different part of the digestive tract. The location of pain alone cannot select the test because symptoms can overlap or be felt away from their source. A clinician considers the symptom pattern and duration, examination, earlier tests, medicines and family history before deciding whether endoscopy is likely to provide information that changes care. [3, 4, 5, 6]

Sometimes one procedure is appropriate, sometimes both are considered, and sometimes neither is the first step. Blood tests, H. pylori testing, stool tests, imaging or planned follow-up may be more useful in a particular situation. This article explains the distinction and what to expect; it is not a tool for choosing a procedure yourself. The treating clinician makes the final decision after assessing you. [3, 4, 11]

When might a clinician consider gastroscopy?

Gastroscopy may be considered when assessing difficulty or pain with swallowing, persistent vomiting, suspected upper gastrointestinal bleeding, black stool, or anaemia that needs a possible source investigated. It may also be considered for persistent dyspepsia or reflux when concerning features are present or the initial assessment has not answered the clinical question. During the examination, the clinician can identify inflammation, ulcers, narrowing or lining changes, take small samples, and sometimes treat a source of bleeding. [1, 3, 5, 7]

A biopsy does not automatically mean that cancer is suspected. Samples are obtained for many reasons, including assessing inflammation, microscopic changes or a specific possible cause. Some visual findings can be discussed after the procedure, whereas tissue results require separate laboratory analysis. A normal result can reassure about the area and question examined, but it does not necessarily explain every abdominal symptom. [1, 3, 5]

For a fuller explanation of the route, throat spray, sedation and biopsy, read the gastroscopy procedure details. This comparison only summarises that experience so it does not duplicate the procedure-day guide. Do not independently start or stop acid-suppression treatment or a blood thinner in preparation; the requesting team decides what is needed according to the reason for testing, your medicines and your health. [3, 5, 10]

When might a clinician consider colonoscopy?

Colonoscopy may help assess rectal bleeding, a persistent unexplained change in bowel habit, chronic diarrhoea in an appropriate context, anaemia, unintentional weight loss, or possible colonic inflammation. It is also used for surveillance after certain findings or polyps and for colorectal cancer screening according to age, individual risk and applicable guidance. A single symptom does not by itself establish that colonoscopy is required. [2, 4, 6, 8, 11]

The camera shows the colon lining, and the clinician may take a biopsy or remove a polyp during the same procedure when appropriate and consented. Removing a polyp does not mean cancer is present; polyps have different types, and laboratory analysis establishes their nature. Conversely, a normal colonoscopy does not automatically exclude every cause of diarrhoea, constipation or bloating because some causes are functional, medicine-related or outside the area the camera examined. [2, 4, 6, 8]

Bloating or constipation without warning features is not a reason to book a scope yourself. The clinician considers the pattern, duration, age, family history, blood or stool results, and whether bleeding, weight loss or night symptoms are present. The aim is not the largest number of tests. It is to select an examination that answers a defined question and whose result can change follow-up or treatment. [4, 8, 11]

Preparation differs: fasting for the stomach and cleansing for the colon

Before gastroscopy, the stomach must be empty for visibility and safety, so the centre gives clear stopping times for food and drink. Do not copy a number of fasting hours from a page or a relative because timing can differ with your appointment, sedation plan and health. If you ate or drank after the stated time, tell the team plainly. Hiding that information can create risk or make the examination incomplete. [1, 3, 5]

Colonoscopy requires bowel cleansing with a laxative preparation and specific food and fluid instructions. Cleansing quality affects the view of the lining and detection of polyps, and inadequate preparation may limit the examination or make a repeat necessary. Follow the product, volume, split and timing written for you. Do not add a dose, another laxative or a home remedy, and do not use another person’s plan. [2, 4, 6]

If the procedure is already planned, the colonoscopy preparation guide covers the practical questions in detail. Contact the centre early if instructions are missing, repeated vomiting occurs, you cannot keep fluids down, you have kidney, heart or diabetes conditions, or preparation is not progressing as expected. The team may adjust the plan; guessing or taking an extra dose yourself is not a safe solution. [4, 6, 12]

Medicines, sedation and consent: what must the team know?

Before either procedure, list every medicine, supplement and dose, especially anticoagulants or antiplatelets, diabetes medicines and insulin, iron, and diuretics. Tell the team about allergies, possible pregnancy, heart, lung or kidney disease, sleep apnoea, and any previous difficulty with sedation or anaesthesia. These details can alter fasting, medicine and monitoring plans. [3, 4, 9, 10]

Do not stop a blood thinner or diabetes medicine yourself. Bleeding risk from a biopsy or polyp removal must be balanced against the risk of interrupting treatment, which requires knowing why you take the medicine and what procedure is planned. Gastroscopy may use local throat spray, while either examination may involve analgesia or different depths of sedation. Sedation is not always general anaesthesia, and available choices vary by centre and patient. [1, 2, 9, 10]

Before starting, ask why the test is recommended, what alternatives exist, whether biopsy or treatment is anticipated, what sedation and monitoring will be used, and when results will arrive. Consent is not a hurried signature; it is an opportunity to understand likely benefit, recognised risks and the test’s limits. Mention anxiety or a difficult previous experience early so the team can discuss comfort and an appropriate plan without promising that you will feel nothing. [5, 6, 9]

What happens on the procedure day and afterwards?

On the day, the team confirms your identity, indication, preparation, medicines and allergies and records baseline observations. For gastroscopy, you commonly lie on your side while the tube passes through the mouth without blocking the airway. For colonoscopy, the flexible tube passes through the rectum and advances through the colon. Air or carbon dioxide expands the tract for visibility, so pressure, fullness or cramping can occur depending on the procedure and sedation plan. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]

Camera time is not the whole visit. Registration, preparation and consent occur first, followed by observation, recovery and discharge instructions. Procedure length changes with ease of passage, visibility, washing, biopsy or polyp removal, and any treatment performed. A longer or shorter time alone does not establish that a finding is serious or the examination incomplete. Ask your centre for an approximate time window and keep the rest of the day flexible. [3, 4, 5, 6]

After gastroscopy, a mild sore throat or temporary bloating can occur. After colonoscopy, gas or mild cramps can occur. Your discharge sheet is the reference for food, drink, activity and medicines. If you received a sedative, do not drive or make important decisions for the period the team specifies, and arrange an escort and transport as instructed even if you feel fully awake. [1, 2, 5, 6, 9]

Read the result as a question and answer, not a verdict

An endoscopy report usually records the indication, how far the camera reached, visibility quality, what was seen and any sample or treatment performed. Ask which question was answered and which result is still pending. The lining’s appearance may be discussed immediately, but biopsies and removed polyps require laboratory analysis. Do not compare a word in your report with an image or another person’s report because meaning changes with location, context and pathology results. [3, 4, 5, 6]

A normal result is reassuring within the area examined and the examination’s quality, but it is not a certificate that the entire digestive system is normal or that symptoms are unreal. Gastroscopy does not assess the colon, and colonoscopy does not assess the stomach. Some symptoms arise from gut movement, visceral sensitivity, medicines or organs outside the digestive lumen. Follow-up may involve explanation, a targeted treatment trial, laboratory testing or a different examination. [3, 4, 7, 8]

Bring the report, medicine list and earlier results to follow-up. Ask whether preparation and visibility were adequate, when a tissue result is due, whether medicines change, what follow-up is appropriate, and which signs need earlier contact. If symptoms persist despite a normal report, return to the clinician rather than repeating a procedure or starting a restrictive diet or medicine independently. [3, 4, 5, 6]

Warning signs and your practical next step

Before endoscopy, seek urgent assessment rather than waiting for a routine appointment if there is vomiting blood, black tarry stool, heavy bleeding, fainting, severe or worsening pain, progressively difficult swallowing, breathing difficulty, clear dehydration or rapid deterioration. These signs do not establish one diagnosis, but a professional must determine the urgency and setting of care, with emergency services for severe symptoms. Do not use an article to decide that bleeding is minor or safe. [1, 2, 3, 4]

After either procedure, follow the discharge sheet and contact the centre or seek urgent care according to severity for severe or increasing chest or abdominal pain, fever, repeated vomiting, breathing difficulty, marked dizziness or fainting, heavy or persistent bleeding, black stool, or severe distension that is not improving. Serious complications are uncommon, but knowing the contact plan is part of safe care. The meaning of symptoms depends on what was performed and your health. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]

If the unresolved question is whether you need gastroscopy or colonoscopy, gather the symptom pattern and duration, reports, test results, medicine list and family history, and let the clinician select the next step. You can Book a consultation with the medical team at La Rose to review that information and plan an appropriate assessment pathway. The clinic is mentioned only as the next step; the treating clinician and endoscopy centre make final decisions about testing, preparation and sedation after assessing you. [3, 4, 9, 10]
