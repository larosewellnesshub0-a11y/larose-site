Child vomiting and dehydration: warning signs

Published: 11 September 2026 10 min read Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Vomiting is a symptom: start with the whole picture

Vomiting is common in children, but it is not one disease and does not automatically mean gastroenteritis. Infection is a frequent cause, especially when vomiting comes with diarrhoea, fever or contact with someone who has similar symptoms. Vomiting without diarrhoea, or vomiting with an unusual colour or pattern, can point to causes inside or outside the abdomen. The colour of the vomit, the child's age, pain, alertness, breathing, urine output, medicines and possible ingestion therefore matter more than trying to name the cause at home [1, 4, 8, 10].

Record when vomiting began, roughly how often it occurs, whether any fluid stays down and when the child last passed urine. Note diarrhoea or fever, and whether abdominal pain is general or fixed in one area. If the vomit looks green or bloody, a photograph may help describe the colour, but do not delay seeking care to take one. Tell the clinician about a head injury, new medicine, chronic condition, previous abdominal surgery, or any chance that the child reached a cleaning product, medicine or foreign object [1, 4, 8].

Activity between episodes is useful context: a child who vomits once then returns to play and drinking is different from one who is lethargic or worsening. Duration also changes the assessment. Vomiting from uncomplicated gastroenteritis commonly improves within one or two days, while persistent or recurrent vomiting needs the cause reviewed. If this has become a recurring pattern with abdominal pain between episodes, read Recurrent tummy aches in children: functional pain or another cause? without assuming that every episode is IBS or H. pylori [1, 3, 8].

Dehydration signs: assess the whole child, not one clue

Dehydration develops when fluid losses exceed what a child can replace. Vomiting reduces intake, while diarrhoea or fever may add further losses. Thirst, a dry mouth and tongue, few tears, less urine or fewer wet nappies, dark urine and sunken eyes are useful clues. Behaviour matters too: unusual irritability, reduced play, increasing sleepiness or difficulty waking the child can be more important than dry lips alone [1, 2, 9].

Do not use one sign to decide that dehydration is mild or severe. Bedside signs are imperfect in isolation, so clinicians combine the history with pulse, breathing, circulation, weight and examination. When a reliable pre-illness weight is available, comparing it with the current weight helps estimate loss, but do not try to calculate a dehydration percentage at home or delay care while looking for a scale. A larger cluster of signs or deterioration over time raises concern [9, 13].

Infants, particularly those younger than six months, may deteriorate faster. Risk is also higher when a child cannot retain fluid, has stopped breastfeeding, has frequent diarrhoea, malnutrition, kidney or heart disease, a complex chronic condition, or takes medicine that affects fluid balance. A sunken fontanelle in an infant, cold limbs, pale or mottled skin, rapid breathing, a fast pulse, reduced responsiveness or fainting require urgent assessment. These can indicate severe dehydration or shock and are not a reason to extend a home fluid trial [1, 2, 9].

Rehydration at home: small frequent sips and the right solution

If the child is alert, has no red flag and can swallow, correctly prepared oral rehydration solution is the main way to replace lost water and salts. Offer small, frequent amounts with a spoon, cup or age-appropriate oral syringe rather than one large drink that may trigger more vomiting. If the child vomits after an attempt, do not assume treatment has failed immediately; restart more slowly with smaller amounts. Obtain medical advice if even tiny sips will not stay down or the child is worsening [1, 4, 5, 7, 12].

Mix each sachet with exactly the amount of water stated on the packet. Do not add extra powder, dilute the solution or formula independently, or improvise a salt-and-sugar mixture by guesswork. Juice, fizzy drinks and sports drinks are not equivalent to oral rehydration solution, and their sugar concentration can worsen diarrhoea. Ask a pharmacist or clinician which product is suitable for the child's age, particularly for an infant or a child with a chronic condition [1, 2, 3, 5, 6].

Continue breastfeeding frequently, and do not routinely stop usual milk or weaken formula without clinical direction. During correction of clear dehydration, the clinician may give specific fluid and food instructions for that child. Once rehydrated and able to drink, return gradually to the usual diet as tolerated; routine starvation or milk avoidance is unnecessary. Oral treatment succeeds for many children, but severe dehydration, shock, deterioration or persistent vomiting of oral solution may require tube or intravenous rehydration in a healthcare setting [1, 6, 7, 10, 12].

Signs that cannot wait: emergency or urgent assessment

Dark green, bile-coloured vomit is an emergency sign because it may indicate intestinal obstruction until proved otherwise. Bloody vomit, or material that resembles coffee grounds, also needs urgent assessment. Do not wait when there is severe, constant or localised abdominal pain; a swollen tender abdomen; rigidity or pain worsened by movement; black stool; or visible blood. Vomiting that stops after medicine does not exclude a serious cause when these features remain [1, 3, 8, 10].

Seek emergency help immediately if the child is difficult to wake, is not responding normally, faints, has a seizure, struggles to breathe, develops blue or grey colouring or marked pallor and mottling, or has cold limbs while deteriorating. Neck stiffness with fever or marked light sensitivity, a sudden severe headache, vomiting after a head injury, or vomiting with altered awareness should not be labelled a stomach bug. If poisoning, a cleaning product or a medicine overdose is possible, contact emergency services or the local poison centre immediately and do not induce vomiting [2, 3, 8].

A very young infant, a child with a complex chronic condition, or any child changing rapidly deserves a lower threshold for assessment. A clear fever in an infant younger than three months, or high fever with an unwell appearance in an older child, also needs urgent age-appropriate advice. If your overall impression is that the child is simply "not right" even though you cannot name one sign, take that concern seriously and seek assessment; guidance relies on deterioration in the whole clinical picture, not one number alone [1, 8, 10].

When to obtain same-day advice without an emergency sign

Seek same-day medical advice if vomiting is frequent and the child cannot retain enough fluid, urine output has clearly fallen, breast or bottle feeds have stopped, or dehydration signs appear even while the child is still awake. An infant younger than one year, especially younger than six months, needs earlier attention. Also make contact when vomiting lasts beyond two days, is worsening rather than following an improving course, or returns after improvement with new symptoms [1, 2, 3].

Blood or mucus in diarrhoea, localised pain, persistent fever, weight loss, excessive thirst and urination before vomiting, recurrent morning headache, or vomiting that wakes the child changes the pathway and needs assessment. Vomiting without diarrhoea can precede diarrhoea early in gastroenteritis, but it also broadens the possible causes; do not keep repeating a "virus" label for days when the pattern is not improving [1, 8, 10].

When a child has diabetes, kidney or heart disease, a metabolic disorder, immune suppression or previous gastrointestinal surgery, follow the treating team's sick-day plan and contact them early because dehydration and electrolyte disturbance may be more dangerous. The same applies when reliable observation or suitable fluids cannot be provided at home. Time thresholds do not replace the child's condition: one child may need assessment within hours because of clear deterioration, while another remains stable and improves with fluids and observation. Age, examination, losses and drinking ability guide the final decision [1, 9, 10].

At assessment: tests and treatment follow severity and cause

The clinician begins with airway, circulation and alertness, then reviews drinking, vomiting, diarrhoea, urine output and any reliable pre-illness weight. Examination covers hydration and the abdomen, with neurological, respiratory or other assessment guided by the history. Many children with uncomplicated gastroenteritis need no routine blood tests or imaging. Investigations become relevant when the diagnosis is unclear, a red flag or shock is present, intravenous fluid is required, or an electrolyte or glucose disturbance is possible [1, 4, 9, 10].

The priority is fluid replacement by the least invasive route that works safely. Oral rehydration solution is first-line in suitable cases, and it can sometimes be given through a nasogastric tube when drinking is difficult. Intravenous fluid is needed for shock, severe dehydration, deterioration or failed enteral rehydration. Comparative evidence shows that oral treatment is appropriate and effective for many children with gastroenteritis, but it is not a test of endurance and should not be prolonged at home when the child is failing or worsening [1, 7, 12].

Antiemetic medicine is not automatic for every child. Ondansetron, for example, may be used by an emergency team for selected children older than six months with gastroenteritis-related vomiting and mild-to-moderate dehydration, or after a suitable oral rehydration attempt fails. Selection, dose and contraindications remain clinical decisions. Do not use an old prescription or another child's medicine, and do not treat cessation of vomiting as proof that a serious cause has disappeared [7, 8, 11].

When vomiting is chronic or recurrent, growth is faltering, swallowing is difficult, bleeding occurs or directed findings raise concern, the clinician may discuss specialist tests rather than treating symptoms alone. Endoscopy in children: when is it considered? explains the role and limits of a scope, but acute vomiting alone does not mean that every child needs endoscopy [8].

A practical monitoring plan and next step

When the child is stable and has no red flags, keep a simple record: vomiting episodes, sips retained, urine or wet nappies, temperature, diarrhoea and activity. Use oral rehydration solution correctly, continue breastfeeding and reassess regularly rather than assuming that sleep means recovery. Do not force food, but once rehydrated allow the usual diet according to appetite. Wash hands with soap and water, clean contaminated surfaces, and avoid sharing towels or utensils when infection is possible [1, 2, 3, 10].

Before contacting a clinician, have the child's age and weight, symptom start time, last urine output, number of vomiting and diarrhoea episodes, vomit colour, fever and pain ready. Mention medical conditions, medicines, allergies and possible exposure to infection, contaminated food or a toxic substance. These details help the team decide whether home observation is safe, same-day assessment is needed or emergency care is required [1, 4].

If a red flag appears, do not use a routine clinic booking or wait for a message reply; seek the appropriate emergency care immediately. For recurrent or persistent vomiting in a stable child without an emergency sign, you can request a paediatric appointment at the clinic to review the history, hydration, growth and examination. The treating clinician can then choose the plan or referral. This clinic mention is organisational only: an article cannot diagnose an individual child, and decisions about medicine, therapeutic fluid volumes or tests must be tailored to age and condition [1, 8, 9].
