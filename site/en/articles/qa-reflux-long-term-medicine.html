Long-term acid-reflux medicine: stop or continue?

Published: 7 September 2026 10 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

First step: identify the medicine and why you take it

“Acid medicine” may mean a fast-acting antacid, a histamine-2 receptor blocker, or a proton-pump inhibitor, a class that suppresses acid more strongly. This article mainly addresses proton-pump inhibitors because they most often prompt questions about prolonged use. Box colour or the phrase “stomach protector” is not enough: write down the active ingredient, dose, frequency, timing, who prescribed it and the original reason. [1, 6, 9]

The useful question is not simply “How long have I taken it?” but “What diagnosis or risk is this medicine treating now?” A defined treatment trial for typical heartburn differs from therapy prescribed for severe oesophagitis, a previous ulcer, or protection from bleeding in someone taking certain medicines and carrying relevant risk factors. Long use without a documented reason deserves review, but does not automatically prove that the medicine has harmed you. Likewise, disappearance of heartburn does not automatically prove that the underlying indication has gone. [2, 4, 5]

If the distinction between a symptom and a diagnosis is unclear, read heartburn or gastro-oesophageal reflux, then return to the medicine question. Knowing that you experience burning does not by itself establish proven reflux disease, its dose or its duration. A clinician combines symptoms, history, any endoscopy or reflux-monitoring results, and other medicines before deciding whether to continue or reduce treatment. [1, 3, 7]

When does long-term treatment have a clear indication?

Some situations provide a clear benefit from continuing a proton-pump inhibitor. Examples include severe erosive oesophagitis or complications such as an oesophageal ulcer or stricture, Barrett’s oesophagus in defined contexts, and selected people who need protection from upper gastrointestinal bleeding because they have a high-risk medicine combination or clinical history. Guidelines generally advise against an unsupervised withdrawal trial in complicated reflux disease or high bleeding risk because preventing recurrent injury or bleeding is a real benefit, not merely symptom suppression. [1, 2, 4, 5]

Longer treatment can also be reasonable when established reflux symptoms repeatedly return during reduction and remain well controlled on the lowest effective dose. That does not mean everybody must remain on the same dose forever. Many people taking twice-daily treatment can be considered for step-down to once daily when the indication permits, and the dose itself should be reassessed periodically. The goal is the lowest dose that preserves the required benefit, not the lowest number on paper and not a dose written years ago that has never been questioned. [1, 2, 3, 4]

The indication needs to be documented. Phrases such as “a sensitive stomach” or “I take it with every medicine” do not reveal whether there was a previous ulcer, bleeding, severe oesophagitis, Barrett’s oesophagus, or simply a prescription begun during a hospital stay and continued unintentionally. If the reason is unknown, do not assume the medicine must be stopped. Gather old reports and ask the treating clinician to establish whether an ongoing indication remains. [2, 4, 10]

Who can discuss dose reduction or on-demand use?

If the medicine began as a trial for typical heartburn without an alarm feature, symptoms improved after the treatment period, and there is no severe oesophagitis, Barrett’s oesophagus, bleeding history or continuing need for protection, it is reasonable to discuss a supervised reduction or withdrawal trial. Guidance treats the absence of a clear chronic indication as a reason to review the prescription, and allows intermittent or on-demand treatment for some people with non-erosive reflux disease. This is a clinical option, not one rule for everyone. [1, 2, 5, 10]

The review asks whether the diagnosis was ever established, whether the dose was taken correctly before food, whether symptoms resolved or a persistent complaint was labelled reflux without evidence, whether another medicine raises ulcer or bleeding risk, and whether endoscopy or testing has occurred since treatment began. The answers may support continuation, fewer daily doses, a lower strength, on-demand use, or diagnostic assessment before committing to long-term therapy. [1, 2, 3, 7]

Do not use fear of possible adverse effects as the sole reason to stop when a strong indication exists, and do not use comfort on treatment as the sole reason to continue when the diagnosis remains unproven. The American Gastroenterological Association update emphasizes that deprescribing should be based primarily on the absence of an indication, not simply concern about research associations. Tell the clinician what matters to you: symptom control, fewer tablets, avoidance of relapse, or greater diagnostic certainty. [2, 3, 11]

Temporary rebound symptoms do not always mean disease relapse

After prolonged proton-pump inhibitor use, some people develop temporary heartburn or dyspepsia during dose reduction or withdrawal because of rebound acid hypersecretion. This can make the first few symptomatic days feel as though the body is “dependent” on the medicine or the disease has returned at full strength. Temporary symptoms alone, however, do not prove dependence or failure of the plan. The clinician should explain what may occur, when to seek review and what to do if symptoms appear. [2, 10]

No single withdrawal method is proven best for everybody. Expert updates allow either gradual dose reduction or abrupt discontinuation in an appropriate patient, while other guidance often favours reaching the lowest effective dose first because that may make symptom management easier. A plan might move from twice daily to once daily, use a lower dose, or change to on-demand treatment, sometimes with a temporary symptom-relief option selected by a clinician or pharmacist. Do not copy another person’s schedule because the indication and bleeding risk may differ. [2, 5, 10]

During the trial, record the timing and severity of burning, its relationship to meals and sleep, and any rescue treatment. Seek earlier review when symptoms are severe, persistent or different from usual rather than changing doses at random. If symptoms recur after every attempt, that still does not automatically establish a lifelong need: the next step may be confirming the diagnosis, reviewing timing and adherence, or looking for another cause. [1, 3, 7]

How should long-term safety claims be interpreted?

Proton-pump inhibitors are among the most effective medicines for reflux disease, and their benefit is well established when the indication is sound. Observational studies have also associated prolonged use with intestinal infection, fractures, kidney disease, deficiencies, dementia and cardiovascular disease. The word “associated” matters: an observational study may find an outcome more often among users but cannot by itself prove that the medicine caused it, because users may be older or have more illnesses and medicines. [1, 4]

American guidance explains that higher-quality evidence has not shown a meaningful increase in most risks commonly discussed online, while a possible small increase in enteric infection remains the more consistent signal. In a large randomized trial that followed pantoprazole use for several years, most measured safety outcomes did not differ significantly, apart from a signal for more enteric infections. This does not make every PPI free of every adverse effect, but it prevents turning every association into proven causation or frightening someone away from indicated therapy. [1, 11]

Recognized adverse effects and official safety information include rare or longer-term possibilities such as low magnesium, vitamin B12 deficiency and bone-related concerns in susceptible people. The answer is neither to dismiss these issues nor to order a universal panel for everyone. It is to weigh personal benefit and risk, reassess the lowest effective dose, and respond to genuine symptoms or risk factors. Do not start supplements because of a social-media post, and do not stop treatment because of a frightening headline. [4, 8, 12]

Does every long-term user need special tests?

Not everyone taking a proton-pump inhibitor needs routine magnesium, vitamin B12, kidney-function and bone-density testing solely because of the medicine’s name. Gastroenterology guidance does not recommend such universal monitoring in people without additional risk factors. “Not routine” does not mean “never appropriate”: age, nutrition, kidney disease, osteoporosis risk, treatment duration and dose, new symptoms and accompanying medicines may make a particular test reasonable. [1, 4]

Bring a complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines and supplements. Some medicines can affect the choice of a particular PPI or create the bleeding risk for which protection is being prescribed, while diuretics and other treatments may change the relevance of magnesium monitoring. Acid suppression can also affect preparation for selected investigations, including some H. pylori tests. Do not stop treatment before a test or endoscopy independently; ask for instructions specific to the clinical question. [4, 5, 8]

A useful review documents the indication, the dose actually taken rather than merely prescribed, timing in relation to food, symptom control, troublesome effects and bleeding risk. The clinician can then decide whether to keep or adjust the dose or order a targeted test. If you have fatigue, tingling, cramps, persistent diarrhoea or another new symptom, report it instead of assuming it comes from the medicine or reflux; the same symptom can have more than one explanation. [2, 4, 8, 12]

When does the plan need quicker assessment or testing?

Difficult or painful swallowing, food sticking, repeated vomiting, vomit containing blood or resembling coffee grounds, black stool, unexplained anaemia, unintentional weight loss, or clear deterioration despite treatment need medical assessment rather than a simple dose increase. New or severe chest pain, especially with breathlessness, sweating, dizziness or spread to the arm or jaw, needs urgent care to exclude causes more dangerous than heartburn. Our page acid reflux: when should you see a doctor? explains the broader warning-sign pathway without repeating it all here. [1, 7]

Endoscopy is not an automatic annual test for every long-term user and is not required merely because someone worries about the medicine. It may be appropriate for alarm features, possible complications, symptoms that have not responded as expected, or an uncertain diagnosis. Reflux monitoring answers selected questions, especially when endoscopy is normal and commitment to long-term therapy is being considered. The history and the question the result should answer determine the investigation. [1, 3, 7]

If a clinician proposes an upper examination, read gastroscopy: procedure, biopsy and sedation for preparation and practical questions, while following the instructions from your own unit. Do not stop the PPI before endoscopy or reflux testing unless the clinical team tells you to. Some questions require testing off treatment and others require testing while treatment continues; the wrong preparation can make the result less useful. [1, 3, 7]

Turn the decision into a personal review plan, not yes or no

Before the appointment, photograph the medicine box or write down the active ingredient and dose, the original reason if known, duration of use and what happened during earlier reduction attempts. Bring reports of endoscopy, ulcer or bleeding, and list painkillers, blood-thinning medicines and every supplement. For a short period, record heartburn and regurgitation, their timing, and whether they wake you or affect swallowing and eating. This information grounds the decision in a real pattern. [2, 3, 5]

Ask four clear questions: what is the current indication, are the dose and timing appropriate, do I have a reason not to stop or a higher bleeding risk, and what is the plan if symptoms return? The result may be documented continuation with periodic review, the lowest effective dose, an on-demand trial, withdrawal with follow-up, or a test to establish whether reflux is truly the cause. These decisions may change if a warning feature appears or another medicine changes. [1, 2, 3, 5]

If the treatment reason remains unclear, symptoms recur with every attempt, or use has continued without review, you can request a gastroenterology consultation to begin a structured assessment in the clinic. The clinic is mentioned here only as a practical next step, not as a promise that stopping is suitable or endoscopy is necessary. If there is bleeding, severe chest pain, fainting or progressive swallowing difficulty, seek appropriate urgent care rather than waiting for a routine booking. [1, 7]
