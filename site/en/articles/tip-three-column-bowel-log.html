A three-column bowel log: what to record and how to review it

Published: 29 August 2026 10 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

What a bowel symptom log can and cannot show

When you say “my stomach hurt” or “my bowels were unsettled,” the description is genuine but broad. A bowel symptom log turns a day into information a clinician can review: when and where a symptom began, what happened in the bathroom, and what food or medicine was taken and when. Recording an event as it happens reduces reliance on end-of-week memory. A study of a food and symptom diary in people with IBS found that contemporaneous entries could measure symptoms when they occurred and relate their timing to food, within the study's stated limitations [1].

The log is not a laboratory test, does not look inside the bowel and cannot make a diagnosis by itself. Our guide to IBS symptoms and warning signs explains that a clinician looks for a recurring pain pattern related to bowel movements together with a change in stool frequency or form, while also reviewing duration, medical and family history, and examination [3, 4, 5]. The same details may occur with constipation, medicine-related or infectious diarrhoea, an intolerance, or another condition, so a disease name does not belong in the result column.

Keep the purpose simple: describe an ordinary week with as little effort as possible, rather than monitoring your body all day. Choose paper or a phone note and enter an event when it happens if you can. Days without pain or a bowel movement matter too; write that explicitly instead of leaving a blank. This lets the clinician distinguish a quiet day from a missing entry and ask better questions without allowing the log to replace the whole clinical story [1, 11].

Set up three columns you can realistically maintain

Label the first column “symptom and time,” the second “bowel movement,” and the third “context.” Each row represents a timed event, not an entire day. If pain began at 9:00, a bowel movement occurred at 9:20 and breakfast was at 7:30, place them on separate rows or one clearly organized row. What matters is that the sequence is visible without guesswork. Include the date so events from the same day remain together [1, 10].

Begin with seven days that include work and non-work days if possible, extending to two weeks if symptoms are intermittent. This is a practical collection period, not a diagnostic rule or a reason to delay an appointment. Enter the event at the time or as soon as you remember it; if you backfill the log that evening, mark it as recalled. Do not try to account for every minute. A burdensome diary is unlikely to be completed, and the aim is a reviewable summary [1, 11].

Use the same words and scales throughout. For severity, choose zero to ten or mild, moderate and severe, but do not switch systems halfway through. For stool form, use Bristol Stool Form Scale types 1 to 7 after viewing a reliable image of the scale. The original research found stool form to be a simple guide associated with intestinal transit, and NICE notes that showing the scale can help describe stool quality and quantity [2, 5]. The number is a description; no single type diagnoses a disease.

Column one: symptom time, location and relation to a bowel movement

Start with the clock time, then name the symptom precisely: pain, cramping, bloating as a feeling of fullness, visible distension, nausea or burning. Locate it: above or below the navel, right or left, or spread across the abdomen. Add severity, duration and whether it stopped an activity or woke you. “Pain” alone does not show whether it remained fixed in one place or moved, and that detail forms part of the symptom profile [3, 5].

Record the relationship to a bowel movement without interpreting it: began before, eased afterwards, worsened afterwards or did not change. IBS assessment considers pain related to defecation together with a change in stool frequency or form, but that relationship is one part of a clinical diagnosis and is not sufficient alone [3, 4]. If there is marked urgency, incomplete emptying or leakage, write it without embarrassment; these symptoms matter when bowel function is assessed [5, 12].

Do not group every sensation under the word “bowel.” If the main symptom is heartburn, upper abdominal pain or vomiting, record exactly that. If pain accompanies urination or another non-digestive symptom, note it without forcing a digestive explanation. Also write “no pain” on a quiet day. Comparing the presence and absence of symptoms is useful, but a log cannot identify the organ or cause from pain location alone [3, 10].

Column two: frequency, form, effort and blood

For every bowel movement, record the time and Bristol stool type, then describe the amount approximately as small, medium or large. Add straining, pain, urgency, incomplete emptying or mucus. There is no need to weigh or measure stool precisely at home. Stool form, frequency, straining and urgency help describe constipation, diarrhoea and the bowel pattern, but interpretation depends on what is usual for you and the rest of the history [2, 5, 7, 9].

If no bowel movement occurs that day, state it clearly. Constipation is not only a number below normal; it may include hard, dry or lumpy stool, difficult or painful passage, or a feeling that stool has not completely passed. Conversely, diarrhoea involves loose or watery stools occurring repeatedly or more often than normal for you, and may include urgency and cramping. Writing the observation rather than only “constipation” or “diarrhoea” leaves room for the clinician to interpret it [7, 8, 9].

Record an unusual colour and visible red blood or black tarry stool, but do not assign a cause. Blood can have different explanations and should not be entered as confirmed haemorrhoids. If leakage occurs, note whether it involved gas, liquid or stool and whether you felt warning urgency; NIDDK explains that loss of bowel control may involve solid or liquid stool and has several possible causes [12]. Heavy bleeding or black stool with dizziness should not wait until the end of the week [3, 9].

Column three: food, medicine and context without a quick verdict

Record the time of each meal or drink and a practical description: the main ingredients and approximate amount, not every spice. Do not write “forbidden food”; write, for example, “beans, bread and water at 2:00.” The time-based food and symptom diary study recorded the timing, duration and severity of abdominal symptoms alongside intake, but relationships between dietary components and symptoms were limited and differed among participant subtypes. That is another reason not to blame one meal [1].

Our guide, food intolerance or food allergy?, separates digestive symptoms from an immune reaction, because bloating or diarrhoea after a meal does not prove allergy. In the log, record timing and symptoms only. Swelling of the lips or tongue, wheeze or breathing difficulty, or marked dizziness after food may signal an urgent allergic reaction and is not a situation for home re-exposure. Broad restriction after one episode of cramping can complicate the picture and unnecessarily narrow the diet [1, 13].

Record every new medicine or supplement, its dose and time if known, including antibiotics, laxatives, antacids and pain medicines. Some medicines can cause diarrhoea, while others may be associated with constipation, but do not stop prescribed treatment by yourself. Add recent travel, infection, a major routine change, poor sleep or stress as “context,” not a proven cause. Clinicians review food, medicines and recent infection as part of the history [3, 9, 10].

Review repetition, not the worst day

After several days, do not search for a culprit; look for repetition. Count days with pain, bowel movements per day and the Bristol types that appeared most often. Mark whether pain came before or after a bowel movement and whether it changed, and whether some days involved diarrhoea while others involved constipation. Comparing days gives a better picture than remembering the worst episode, and IBS assessment uses a pattern of pain and changed frequency or form rather than one day [3, 4, 5].

Review timing cautiously. If bloating followed dinner three times, the useful question is “what was shared?” rather than “dinner caused it.” Meal size and timing, medicines, constipation developing over days and sleep may overlap. Our guide to persistent bloating: when should you see a doctor? separates the sensation of bloating from visible distension and explains causes that need assessment; here, simply circle the pattern and take it to the clinician [1, 5]. Finding no clear pattern is an acceptable result, and you do not need to invent an association.

If your condition is stable and there is no medical instruction to do otherwise, avoid changing many things during the recording week. Removing several foods while starting a supplement and laxative makes the result difficult to interpret. Conversely, do not stop necessary treatment to create a “clean” week. If a clinician or dietitian suggests a dietary trial, keep it defined and supervised; NICE recommends that exclusion approaches such as a low-FODMAP diet be delivered by a professional with dietary expertise when needed [5, 6].

Warning signs that should not wait for the log

Seek appropriate urgent care for sudden or severe worsening abdominal pain, heavy bleeding, black tarry stool with weakness or dizziness, fainting, repeated vomiting that prevents fluid intake, clear signs of dehydration, or painful distension with inability to pass stool or gas. Diarrhoea with dehydration, blood or severe pain needs prompt medical contact, and urgency depends on severity, age and other health conditions [7, 9]. A log is not an emergency triage tool.

Arrange prompt assessment for repeated blood even in small amounts, unintentional weight loss, anaemia, continuing fever, pain or diarrhoea that wakes you, a new persistent bowel-habit change, or a significant family history of colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease or coeliac disease. These features do not prove a particular disease, but they make it unsafe to assume “this is IBS” without assessment [3, 4, 5]. Record them, but do not wait to complete two weeks before contacting a clinician.

Our guide to whether IBS symptoms need colonoscopy explains that the procedure is not automatic for every pain or episode of bloating; the decision depends on warning features, age, risk, examination and test results. Do not use the log to order or cancel an investigation yourself. The treating clinician decides whether the story supports a positive IBS diagnosis or whether blood or stool tests, colonoscopy or another investigation should answer a defined question [3, 5, 6].

Turn the page into a useful appointment summary

Before the visit, write five lines: the recording period, most frequent symptom, usual bowel frequency and observed range, predominant Bristol types, and the clearest relationship you noticed without calling it a cause. Put any blood, night waking or weight loss at the top. Bring a medicine and supplement list with package photographs, and say when each began in relation to symptoms. An organized history helps the clinician review the pattern and choose an appropriate investigation if one is needed [3, 10, 11].

Prepare clear questions: does the description fit constipation, diarrhoea or an IBS pattern; is there a feature that needs faster investigation; could a medicine be contributing; and is there one safe change to try and monitor? Do not assume you need every test, and do not treat a normal result as cancellation of the symptoms. The clinician selects tests from the history, examination and risk factors; the question may be anaemia, inflammation, infection or coeliac disease depending on the case [3, 5, 6, 10].

If symptoms recur or affect eating, sleep or daily life, you can request a gastroenterology consultation and bring the log and summary to the clinic. The clinic is mentioned only as a practical next step, not a promise of a diagnosis, test or outcome. If an urgent feature appears, such as heavy bleeding, fainting, severe worsening pain or dehydration, seek appropriate care immediately rather than waiting for a routine appointment [3, 7, 9].
