Mounjaro and Wegovy serious side effects: red flags

Published: 1 October 2026 11 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

Not every symptom is dangerous, but some signs cannot wait

Mounjaro is a brand of tirzepatide, while Wegovy is a brand of semaglutide. Both can cause common digestive symptoms such as nausea or constipation, particularly when treatment begins or a dose changes. This article is not a guide to managing mild symptoms; the linked page covers those in detail. The focus here is on less common but more important problems: when symptoms may indicate pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, dehydration affecting the kidneys, low blood glucose, eye changes in a person with diabetes, or a serious allergic reaction [1, 2, 3].

The distinction does not depend on the symptom name alone. Mild nausea while you can drink is different from repeated vomiting accompanied by reduced urine and dizziness. Brief mild cramps are different from severe, continuing pain, especially when it reaches the back. Official prescribing information warns that some serious problems can begin with symptoms resembling an ordinary digestive complaint. Severity, persistence, and accompanying features therefore matter more than trying to diagnose yourself at home [1, 2, 4, 5].

Call the emergency service appropriate to your location immediately for loss of consciousness, difficulty breathing, swelling of the tongue or throat, severe confusion, unbearable abdominal pain, or sudden loss of vision. Do not drive yourself if you are faint, confused, or struggling to breathe. Take the product name, strength, time of the last dose, and medicine list, especially insulin or other glucose-lowering medicines. The purpose is to recognise urgency, not to stop or change treatment independently [1, 2, 8, 11].

Severe persistent pain: when pancreatitis is a concern

Official semaglutide and tirzepatide prescribing information includes a warning about acute pancreatitis. The main sign that should not be dismissed as injection-related cramps is severe, persistent pain in the upper or middle abdomen. It may travel to the back and may occur with or without vomiting. Pain can worsen after food and the abdomen may be tender, but absence of vomiting does not rule the problem out. This pattern needs urgent assessment now, rather than waiting for a routine appointment or trying painkillers and light meals at home [1, 2, 3, 6].

A clinician determines the cause through assessment and may order blood tests or imaging when indicated. Abdominal pain has many possible causes, and the label does not say that every episode is pancreatitis. Equally, you should not reassure yourself simply because treatment began recently or the pain followed a meal; timing alone cannot make the diagnosis. If pain is severe, persistent, reaches the back, or comes with repeated vomiting, seek urgent care and tell the team that you use tirzepatide or semaglutide [1, 2, 6, 13].

Do not start, stop, or repeat a dose on the basis of a self-diagnosis. The labels direct the prescriber to discontinue treatment when pancreatitis is suspected, but applying that instruction and deciding what follows are clinical decisions after assessment. A previous episode of pancreatitis should be known to the prescriber. The immediate priority is not to overlook characteristic pain or delay care because you fear that you are overreacting [1, 2, 4, 5].

Right upper abdominal pain: gallbladder warning signs

Official prescribing information lists acute gallbladder disease, including gallstones and gallbladder inflammation, among warnings associated with semaglutide and tirzepatide. Typical gallbladder pain is often in the right upper or central abdomen and may reach the back or right shoulder. It can begin after food and persist rather than settling quickly. Strong or continuing pain, especially with fever, chills, or vomiting, needs prompt medical assessment because gallbladder inflammation or a blocked duct requires diagnosis and treatment, not only dietary adjustment [1, 2, 3, 7].

Yellow eyes or skin, dark urine, pale stool, or pain accompanied by fever increases the urgency. These features may reflect impaired bile flow or infection. Seek urgent same-day care, and use emergency care if pain is extreme or there is confusion or fainting. Do not try to distinguish a stone, gallbladder sludge, and indigestion at home. Ultrasound and blood tests are chosen after assessment; the linked page explains sludge, stones, and the role of ultrasound [3, 7].

Rapid weight loss itself can increase gallstone risk in some people, but this does not mean everyone who loses weight will develop a problem or that finding a stone proves the medicine caused it. Useful details include pain location, duration, relation to food, fever or jaundice, and dose timing. Do not use a gallbladder “cleanse” or alter treatment yourself. Respond to the severity of the signs and leave treatment decisions until the cause is assessed [1, 3, 7].

Vomiting and dehydration: when the kidneys may be at risk

The kidney risk is usually not direct toxicity from the injection. It can arise when vomiting, diarrhoea, or reduced drinking produces a substantial fluid deficit. Official labels warn about acute kidney injury due to volume depletion and note that some reported cases required hospital treatment or dialysis. Risk deserves extra attention if you already have kidney disease, take diuretics, or have a condition that limits fluid intake, although severe dehydration can also injure kidneys in someone without known kidney disease [1, 2, 3, 9].

Concerning signs include being unable to retain even small sips, clearly reduced or absent urine, very dark urine, dizziness on standing, unusual drowsiness, confusion, a very dry mouth, or a rapid heartbeat. Repeated vomiting with any of these signs needs urgent clinical contact. Use emergency care for fainting, confusion, absent urine, or profound weakness. Do not force a large volume of fluid at once when everything comes back up, and do not follow a generic drinking target if a heart or kidney clinician has given you a fluid limit [1, 2, 4, 5, 9].

Tell the clinician about every medicine, particularly diuretics, blood-pressure medicines, and painkillers, because dehydration and medicine combinations can alter kidney risk. Do not stop prescribed treatment or take random painkillers or rehydration salts without advice. Flank pain or blood in urine has other possible causes, including kidney stones, which the linked page covers. Reduced urine after repeated vomiting should be treated as possible dehydration or kidney injury until a clinician assesses you [2, 5, 9].

Low blood glucose: risk rises with certain diabetes medicines

Mounjaro and Wegovy used alone do not usually cause severe hypoglycaemia in most people, but risk increases when either is combined with insulin or a medicine that stimulates insulin release, such as a sulfonylurea. The labels therefore warn that the clinician may need to review accompanying diabetes medicines. That review must be clinical; do not reduce insulin or stop a diabetes medicine independently before or after an injection [1, 2, 3, 8].

Low-glucose symptoms can include shaking, sweating, sudden hunger, palpitations, dizziness, headache, irritability, blurred vision, or difficulty concentrating. Severe hypoglycaemia can cause confusion, unusual behaviour, seizures, or unconsciousness. If you have diabetes, know the glucose threshold and written steps your team set for you, and keep a rapid source of glucose available as directed. If the person is conscious and can swallow, follow that individual plan. If the person is unconscious, having a seizure, or cannot swallow safely, do not put food or drink in the mouth. Call emergency services and use glucagon only if prescribed and someone present knows how to use it [2, 3, 8].

Nausea, sweating, and dizziness can overlap with other problems, so check glucose when that is available and part of your plan. Tell the clinician about reduced eating, vomiting, fasting, or any diabetes-medicine change because these can alter risk. Someone without diabetes who takes no glucose-lowering medicine should not assume that every dizzy spell is hypoglycaemia. Severe dizziness or fainting still must not be ignored; it needs assessment rather than repeated sugar intake without a measurement or diagnosis [1, 2, 8].

Vision changes with diabetes: do not wait for a routine review

Official labels call for monitoring people with diabetes who have a history of diabetic retinopathy. Retinopathy complications occurred in semaglutide trials, and rapid improvement in glucose control can be accompanied by temporary worsening in some people with pre-existing disease. The Mounjaro label also advises monitoring patients with a history of diabetic retinopathy. This does not mean that everyone using these medicines will develop eye damage, or that every episode of blurred vision comes from the retina [1, 2, 10, 13].

If you have diabetes and develop a new vision change, more floaters than usual, dark areas, sudden difficulty seeing, or rapid deterioration, contact the eye clinician and diabetes team promptly. Sudden loss of vision, a curtain or shadow across the visual field, or severe eye pain is an emergency. Do not wait for an annual examination, and do not alter the medicine or glucose-lowering dose yourself. Examination distinguishes a temporary focusing change associated with changing glucose from a retinal, optic-nerve, or other eye problem [1, 10].

The linked article examines semaglutide, tirzepatide, and vision loss in detail, including the distinction between diabetic retinopathy and research signals involving the optic nerve. The practical message here is simpler: note when the change began and whether it affects one or both eyes, and take glucose readings and a medicine list, but do not let note-taking delay care. If you already have diabetic retinopathy, ask at the start about an eye-monitoring plan while glucose control improves [1, 2, 10, 13].

Severe allergy and stomach slowing: other important signs

Swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, difficulty breathing or swallowing, wheeze, fainting, or a rapidly spreading rash after an injection may indicate a severe allergic reaction. This is an emergency: call emergency services immediately rather than waiting to see whether it settles. A small rash or local itch is not the same picture, but it still needs to be reported, particularly if it grows or recurs. Labels contraindicate the product after a previous serious hypersensitivity reaction to its active ingredient or components. A clinician determines what the reaction means for future treatment after urgent care [1, 2, 3, 11].

Semaglutide and tirzepatide slow gastric emptying. Their labels warn about severe gastrointestinal reactions and state that use is not recommended in severe gastroparesis. Repeated vomiting, profound fullness after a small amount, continuing pain and swelling, or inability to eat or drink needs prompt review. Increasing abdominal swelling with vomiting and inability to pass stool or wind needs urgent assessment for something more serious than ordinary constipation; do not respond with a random large dose of laxative [1, 2, 3, 12].

Tell an anaesthesia or endoscopy team that you use the medicine before any planned procedure because delayed stomach emptying matters during anaesthesia. Follow instructions specific to your procedure; dose decisions vary by circumstance and cannot be taken from a general post. Keep two questions separate: does the symptom need urgent care now, and after the person is stable, should treatment continue or change? Red flags determine the first answer, while the prescriber decides the second after the cause is assessed [1, 2, 3].

Your next step: prepare a plan before symptoms occur

Before treatment begins or at a review, list every diabetes and blood-pressure medicine, diuretic, painkiller, and any history of pancreatitis, gallstones, kidney disease, diabetic retinopathy, or medicine allergy. Ask the prescriber directly which effects are expected, which signs need same-day contact, and which require emergency care. If you have diabetes, request a written hypoglycaemia plan and eye-monitoring plan suited to your history. Preparing this information is more useful than trying to remember a long list while unwell [1, 2, 3, 8, 10].

When a symptom occurs, note pain location, severity and duration, episodes of vomiting or diarrhoea, ability to retain fluid, urine output, temperature, requested glucose readings, and timing of the last dose. Do not delay emergency care to complete the record. Take the original package or a clear photograph of its label without personal details. The name “Mounjaro” or “Wegovy” alone is not enough; the team needs the ingredient, strength, and other medicines to assess risk and interactions [1, 2, 4, 5].

If there is no emergency sign and you want a structured review of risks and symptoms, the medical team at La Rose can assess the clinical picture during a consultation, with branches in Maadi and Fifth Settlement, New Cairo. A consultation does not replace emergency care, promise that a particular medicine is suitable, or mean treatment must continue. Bring the medicine list, medical history, and symptom record. The clinician decides whether treatment continues or changes after weighing benefit and risk; do not change the dose yourself [1, 2, 3]. For non-urgent concerns, you can book a consultation with the medical team.

Frequently asked questions

Important warnings include pancreatitis, acute gallbladder disease, kidney injury from dehydration, hypoglycaemia especially with insulin or a sulfonylurea, worsening retinopathy in some people with diabetes, severe allergy, and severe gastrointestinal reactions. These are possible risks, not expected outcomes for everyone. Urgency depends on the presentation: severe persistent pain, breathing difficulty, fainting, confusion, sudden vision loss, or inability to retain fluids cannot wait for routine follow-up [1, 2, 3].

Seek urgent assessment when pain is severe or persistent, particularly upper abdominal pain reaching the back or accompanied by repeated vomiting. Right upper abdominal pain with fever, jaundice, or dark urine may indicate a gallbladder problem. Increasing swelling with vomiting and inability to pass stool or wind also needs urgent assessment. Do not diagnose the cause or change the dose at home; tell the team the active ingredient and last-dose time [1, 2, 6, 7].

Dark urine with reduced volume after vomiting or diarrhoea can indicate dehydration, and severe dehydration can cause acute kidney injury. Seek urgent help if you cannot retain sips, urine output clearly falls, or there is severe dizziness, drowsiness, or confusion. Dark urine accompanied by yellow eyes and right upper abdominal pain may reflect impaired bile flow rather than dehydration. Assessment distinguishes these causes, so do not rely on urine colour alone [1, 2, 7, 9].

Risk rises substantially when these medicines are combined with insulin or an insulin-releasing medicine such as a sulfonylurea. If you use these treatments, have a glucose threshold and treatment plan from the diabetes team; the clinician reviews doses. Shaking, sweating, palpitations, confusion, or fainting are important signs. Do not give food or drink by mouth to someone who is unconscious or cannot swallow safely; call emergency services. Do not adjust diabetes medicines yourself [1, 2, 3, 8].

Blurred vision has many causes, including changing glucose, but a new change deserves prompt contact, especially with diabetes or known retinopathy. Sudden vision loss, a curtain or shadow, or severe eye pain is an emergency. Labels advise monitoring a patient with diabetes who has a history of diabetic retinopathy, but they do not say every blurred episode is caused by the medicine. Do not stop treatment independently; seek examination to identify the cause [1, 2, 10, 13].

When there is an emergency sign, the priority is to obtain care immediately and tell the team the medicine and dose time, not to wait for online advice. Some warnings, including suspected pancreatitis or serious hypersensitivity, have specific label instructions, but a clinician applies them after assessment and decides whether treatment stops, changes, or is replaced. Do not take an extra dose or independently rebuild the treatment schedule while awaiting assessment [1, 2, 3, 11].
