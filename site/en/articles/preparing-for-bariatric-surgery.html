Preparing for bariatric surgery: tests, planning and questions

Published: 7 September 2026 10 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Preparation starts with understanding the decision

Preparing for bariatric surgery is neither a universal checklist nor a punishment before surgery. It helps you understand benefits, risks, alternatives and required postoperative changes while the team identifies problems that can be treated before the date. Informed consent requires real room for questions, and surgery may be delayed when more preparation improves safety. [1, 2, 3]

NIDDK explains that weight-loss surgery may be an option for adults with a body mass index of 40 or more, 35 or more with a serious health problem such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease or sleep apnoea, or 30 or more with type 2 diabetes that is hard to control. These numbers start the conversation rather than decide it; the team looks at the whole picture. [13]

For teenagers, NIDDK says assessment should involve a multidisciplinary team with expertise in treating young people. At any age, multidisciplinary bariatric assessment is what determines whether the operation is suitable and what needs attention first. [1, 13]

Start by writing down why you want the operation, what you have tried before and what worries you. That page makes the first visit clearer and helps the surgeon address what matters to you instead of general points. [2, 4]

Tests are tailored to your history

Tests should not be ordered merely to complete paperwork; each investigation should answer a question linked to your history, examination and proposed procedure. The team may review diabetes, blood pressure, heart, breathing, sleep and nutrition according to the case. Ask how each test could change the plan and bring previous reports. [2, 3]

Perioperative guidelines from endocrine and bariatric societies link pre-op testing to history and procedure, including basic blood tests, glucose and lipid assessment and checks of some vitamins and minerals when indicated. A deficiency found before surgery is treated early rather than growing afterwards as intake falls. [2]

If you have heart or lung disease or take many medicines, you may be asked to see a cardiologist, chest physician or anaesthetist. That does not mean a major problem; it means the team wants to understand how your body will handle anaesthesia and recovery before setting a date. [2, 3]

Keep one file containing test results, scans and reports of earlier operations, arranged by date. It saves time and reduces repeating tests done recently. [2]

If a result is abnormal, ask whether it needs treatment before surgery or only follow-up afterwards. Sometimes controlling glucose or blood pressure or treating anaemia takes a few weeks, and that time makes anaesthesia and recovery much safer even if the date moves slightly. [2, 3]

Medicines, smoking and sleep

Review every medicine and supplement with the team, including diabetes treatment and blood thinners. Do not stop treatment yourself because safe timing and alternatives require clinical direction. Smoking, alcohol and sleep apnoea matter for anaesthesia and recovery; disclose them without embarrassment so the plan can be safer. [2, 3]

ERAS Society recommendations for bariatric surgery say every patient should be screened for tobacco and alcohol use, and that smoking should stop at least 4 weeks before surgery. Stopping early reduces wound, heart and lung complications, and regular counselling with nicotine replacement improves success. [3]

The same recommendations note that people with alcohol misuse need sustained abstinence for a long period before surgery, because the risk of relapse may rise after bariatric surgery. That is why the question is asked directly, and an honest answer protects you. [3]

If you snore loudly, wake up exhausted or someone has noticed pauses in your breathing during sleep, tell the team. Obstructive sleep apnoea is assessed before surgery, and if you use CPAP at home, ERAS recommends using it immediately after the operation, so bring it to hospital. [3]

The preoperative nutrition plan

If the team prescribes a preoperative eating plan, follow it as written rather than copying another patient. Its purpose and timing depend on the operation and health status, and diabetes medicines may need closer review when intake changes. Report dizziness, low-glucose symptoms or major difficulty instead of continuing silently. [2, 3]

ERAS recommendations note that a low-calorie or very-low-calorie diet for 2 to 4 weeks before surgery reduces liver volume, making it easier for the surgeon to reach the stomach. That is why this plan is not an ordinary diet but part of preparing for the operation itself. [3]

Nutritional guidance for bariatric surgery advises a preoperative nutritional assessment, including iron, vitamin B12, folate and vitamin D as appropriate, with any deficiency treated before surgery. This builds a better base for the postoperative period, when intake falls and absorption changes. [2, 8, 9]

Start practising now the habits you will need later: eat slowly, chew well, separate drinks from meals and start meals with protein. Practising before surgery makes the transition afterwards much easier. [10, 11]

If you take diabetes medicines, a low-calorie plan can lower glucose more than usual. Check as the team advised, record readings and contact them if shakiness, sweating or dizziness appear, because the dose may need adjusting before surgery. [2, 3]

Psychological readiness and practical support

Psychological assessment is not a test of willpower. It is a chance to discuss expectations, eating patterns, depression, anxiety, substance use and your ability to follow the plan and seek support. Also identify a trusted person for help after discharge and arrange transport and responsibilities according to the centre’s instructions. [4]

Recommendations for presurgical psychosocial evaluation emphasise that the interview looks for issues that can be treated or supported, such as binge eating, depression or life stress, rather than aiming to exclude people. In many cases the result is added support or follow-up, not refusal. [4]

Talk with family or those you live with about the coming changes: small portions, meal timing, supplements and visits. When the household understands, pressure at gatherings and occasions falls, and someone can remind you if you forget. [4, 6]

Arrange enough time off work for recovery according to the team's advice, and prepare the home beforehand with suitable fluids, measuring tools and a comfortable space. This reduces stress in the first days after discharge. [6]

Final instructions before surgery day

Before surgery day, you should have written instructions for fasting, medicines, arrival and what to bring. Report new illness, fever, severe cough or medicine changes; do not assume the date remains fixed regardless. You can also review sleeve surgery day and recovery to see the steps that follow. [3]

ERAS recommendations say solid food may continue until 6 hours before anaesthesia and clear fluids until 2 hours before it for planned bariatric surgery, unless there is a reason not to, such as delayed stomach emptying. Your anaesthesia team sets the final times, so follow the sheet you were given. [3]

People with diabetes may need specific instructions for insulin or tablet doses on the day of surgery and the day before. Do not guess the dose; ask about it by name, write it down and check your glucose as instructed. [2, 3]

ERAS also notes that clot risk rises with factors such as a previous clot, smoking, varicose veins, sleep apnoea or oestrogen-containing contraceptive pills. Tell the team about any of these, because they can change the prevention plan. [3]

Questions that need clear answers

Read multidisciplinary bariatric assessment if you have not done so, and understand sleeve gastrectomy before deciding. Ask who to contact, what warning signs matter and how food, supplements and follow-up begin. [1, 2, 3, 4]

Useful questions to bring: which operation is proposed and why? What are the most likely risks in my case? How long will I stay in hospital? When can I return to work? Which supplements will I take afterwards, and for how long? [5, 10, 11]

Follow-up does not end after the first months. Read lifelong bariatric follow-up to see that tests, supplements and visits are a permanent part of the plan, and that keeping to them shapes long-term results. [6, 9]

You can use book an appointment for non-urgent discussion, bringing your questions, reports and medicine list. [1, 2]
