Multidisciplinary bariatric assessment: what each team member reviews

Published: 15 August 2026 10 min read Dr. Mohab Ashraf Fouad

Assessment is not a pass-or-fail test

Multidisciplinary bariatric assessment gathers the whole picture rather than judging you. The team reviews conditions, medicines, eating, sleep, psychological health, expectations and practical support, then balances benefits, risks and alternatives. The outcome may be readiness, a preparation step, or a different option being more suitable for now. [1, 2, 4]

Body mass index is a starting point, nothing more. NIDDK explains that weight-loss surgery may be an option for adults with a BMI of 40 or more, a BMI of 35 or more with a serious obesity-related health problem such as type 2 diabetes, heart disease or sleep apnoea, or a BMI of 30 or more with type 2 diabetes that is hard to control with treatment and lifestyle change. That number opens the discussion; the assessment makes the decision. [1, 13]

For teenagers, guidelines recommend evaluation by a multidisciplinary team with paediatric expertise, because growth, family support and long-term follow-up all shape the decision. [13]

So do not approach the assessment as an exam to pass. Honesty about eating, mood, smoking and medicines protects you, because whatever is hidden now tends to surface after surgery at a harder time. [2, 4]

The surgeon and physician

The surgeon explains the options, the change to the stomach and intestine, and the risks, and reviews previous abdominal surgery, reflux and anything that could affect procedure choice. A physician or obesity clinician reviews obesity-related conditions, medicines, investigations and treatment goals. The decision should not rest on a BMI number alone. [1, 2, 5]

Reflux is a clear example: with chronic heartburn, the surgeon may explain that a sleeve can sometimes worsen it, that a bypass may suit some people better, and may request an endoscopy before deciding. Diabetes is another, since the type of operation and its effect on blood sugar are part of the discussion. [1, 5, 12]

The physician also optimises conditions that could raise surgical risk before the date, such as high blood sugar, uncontrolled blood pressure or anaemia, and reviews blood thinners and other medicines that need pausing or adjusting before surgery. [2, 3]

Ask both the same question: why is this particular operation right for me, and what is the alternative if I decide against it for now? A clear answer shows the decision is genuinely built on your situation. [1, 2, 5]

Before the visit, prepare a file with previous tests and scans, reports of any earlier operations, a medicine list with doses, and previous weight-loss attempts, including what worked and what did not. It saves time and lets the surgeon and physician form their view on complete information rather than memory. [1, 2]

The dietitian

The dietitian explores eating and drinking patterns, symptoms, allergies, supplements and your ability to follow the stepwise diet after surgery. The assessment is not searching for perfect eating; it identifies existing deficiency or practical barriers and teaches skills you will need after surgery. The plan is tailored to the operation and to you. [2, 7, 8, 9]

The BOMSS guidance recommends a comprehensive nutritional assessment before bariatric surgery, including tests such as a blood count, ferritin, folate, B12 and vitamin D, because deficiencies are common in people with severe obesity even before surgery. Treating them beforehand makes the next stage easier. [8, 9]

The dietitian also coaches practical skills: eating slowly, chewing well, stopping at fullness, separating drinks from meals, prioritising protein, and drinking enough fluid with a small stomach. Practising these before surgery is much easier than learning them while recovering. [2, 7, 10]

If there is stress eating, night eating or a lot of sugary drinks, say so honestly. It is not a reason for refusal; it is information that makes the plan realistic. [2, 4]

If you take supplements, herbs or slimming products, write down their exact names and amounts. Some can affect test results or interact with medicines, and the dietitian needs to know them to decide whether to stop or continue them before surgery. [2, 7]

Psychological and behavioural care

The psychological or behavioural clinician reviews expectations, mood, anxiety, eating patterns, substance use and social support. This does not frame obesity as a willpower problem and is not a session designed to exclude people. It identifies support or treatment that can make the decision and follow-up safer, and that support can continue after surgery. [4]

The recommendations by Sogg and colleagues for presurgical psychosocial evaluation treat it as an opportunity to identify treatable issues, such as depression, anxiety or binge eating, and to arrange support before and after surgery, rather than to issue a final verdict on the person. [4]

Expectations are an important part of the conversation: surgery helps with weight loss and improves many conditions, but it does not solve everything in life, and adjusting to a body after major weight loss can take time. The assessment helps keep expectations realistic. [4, 6]

Social support matters a great deal: who will help in the first weeks, who at home understands the eating changes, and how you will handle invitations and occasions. All of this is discussed so the plan works in your real life. [4, 6]

If you see a psychiatrist or take treatment for mood or anxiety, say so from the start and bring a report if one exists. That treatment usually continues, and the team needs to know so it can make sure the medicine will be absorbed well after surgery and that support continues in the early period. [4, 6]

Anaesthesia, sleep and medical fitness

The anaesthesia team assesses the airway, heart and lungs, anaesthetic history, medicines and allergies. Sleep apnoea matters because care after surgery may need monitoring or the device you normally use. Sometimes the team needs a cardiology, respiratory or other opinion according to your history, not because everyone requires identical testing. [2, 3]

Sleep apnoea is common with obesity, and many people do not know they have it. Loud snoring, pauses in breathing noticed by someone else, or marked daytime sleepiness may lead the team to request a sleep study; if the diagnosis is confirmed, the device starts before surgery and comes to hospital. [2, 3]

ERAS recommendations for bariatric surgery also encourage stopping smoking before surgery, optimising blood sugar, assessing clot risk and planning to prevent nausea and vomiting. These steps are set during assessment so that surgery day is safer. [3]

Tell the anaesthesia team about any previous problem with anaesthesia, yours or a relative's, and about all medicines, herbs and supplements you take, even those you think are unrelated. [3]

You may be asked to do breathing exercises or a short daily walk before surgery, depending on your condition. This activity helps the lungs and circulation and makes it easier to get up and walk early after surgery, which is an important part of safe recovery. [3]

Procedure choice and plan building

When each discipline shares its findings, the team can choose a procedure or an alternative and identify what needs addressing before a date. Reflux, nutritional deficiency, a particular medicine or limited support can change the plan. Shared choice means knowing why an option is recommended, its limits and what care after surgery will require from you. [1, 2, 4, 5]

ASMBS describes different procedures, such as the sleeve and gastric bypass, each with its own benefits, limits and follow-up needs. Do not choose an operation by name or because someone you know had it; ask how each type would affect your reflux, blood sugar, vitamins and medicines. [5, 11]

At this stage the team may ask for steps before setting a date: treating a vitamin deficiency, improving blood sugar, a sleep study, stopping smoking or support sessions. These steps are part of preparation, not a delay without reason. [2, 3, 4]

Follow-up after surgery is part of the decision itself: if long-term follow-up is not possible for any reason, that should be said clearly, because it may change the choice of operation or its timing. [6, 8, 9]

You are entitled to ask for time to think before agreeing. Sit with your family, review the written summary and come back with your questions. A decision taken with understanding and conviction makes it easier to keep to the diet, vitamins and follow-up after surgery, which is what shapes long-term results. [2, 4, 6]

After assessment: a clear shared next step

After assessment you should leave knowing the decision, who will coordinate the team's views, what is still missing and when the decision will be reviewed. Write this down and keep a copy of the summary. [1, 2, 4]

If you are ready, the next step is preparing for bariatric surgery, with the practical instructions before the date. If a sleeve is the option on the table, read sleeve gastrectomy before deciding to understand its benefits, risks and alternatives. [2, 5, 10]

If a preparation step is needed, ask exactly what it is, who is responsible and when the review is expected. It usually means some information, treatment or support needs completing, not a refusal. Include lifelong bariatric follow-up in the discussion from the start. [2, 4, 6]

You can use book an appointment to discuss the assessment at a non-urgent time, bringing your reports, medicine list and questions. [1, 6]
