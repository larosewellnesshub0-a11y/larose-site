Preparing for an InBody test: a more reliable follow-up

Published: 11 September 2026 9 min read Dr. Shimaa Fouad

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

A fair comparison matters more than a perfect reading

InBody is a form of bioelectrical impedance analysis, or BIA. The device passes a very small current through the body, measures tissue impedance, then uses equations to estimate body water, fat-free mass and fat mass. It does not directly see fat or muscle, and a value displayed to a decimal place remains a model-based estimate. Hydration, body shape, device type, electrode placement and the selected equation can all influence the result. [1, 2]

Preparation is therefore not about producing the “best number” or creating a perfect day that cannot be repeated. It is about keeping conditions reasonably similar before each follow-up so that changes in food, fluid or exercise do not obscure the underlying direction. Current methodological standards emphasise documenting measurement time, fasting or recent intake, hydration, activity, medicines, body position and device details when repeated measurements are interpreted. [1, 3]

One reading cannot diagnose fat gain, muscle loss, dehydration or fluid retention. It becomes more useful when compared with an earlier reading on the same device and considered alongside the health goal, symptoms, strength and measurements. If today's conditions were clearly different, the result is not a failed test; the difference simply needs to be recorded and considered before the plan changes. [1, 3]

Start with a baseline you can repeat

Before the first measurement, ask the provider for that device's instructions and follow them; devices and protocols are not all identical. Choose a time you can usually revisit, such as the same part of the day, and try to keep the interval between the last meal and measurement similar from visit to visit. Wear similarly light clothing each time, and provide accurate height, age and other requested details. Input errors or a different device can alter an estimate even when the body has not changed. [1, 7, 8]

Keep a photograph or copy of the report and record the date, time, device name or model if shown, and whether its design required standing or lying down. Also note unusual circumstances: long travel, marked heat, hard exercise, a late night, a large meal, illness, vomiting or diarrhoea, menstrual timing, swelling or a new medicine. That short record helps distinguish a possible body change from a possible change in testing conditions. [1, 2]

If the follow-up site or device changes, treat the new reading as a new baseline rather than joining both series as though they were one. Guidance and studies show that frequencies, electrodes, equations and calibration can differ between instruments, and even longitudinal use of one device needs cautious interpretation. Consistency reduces noise; it does not remove it completely. [1, 3]

Food, fluid and the bathroom: standardise without restriction

Eating or drinking close to measurement can alter weight and fluid distribution, but the size of the effect is not identical in every study or every person. A recent fluid-ingestion experiment found time-related changes in estimates of water, muscle and fat, while a small study in older men found no important difference after a defined breakfast. The practical message is not severe fasting: follow the provider's instructions and repeat a similar interval from the last food and drink each time. [5, 6]

Drink in your usual safe pattern during the preceding day. Do not restrict water to make body-fat percentage look better, and do not suddenly drink a large amount to make muscle mass appear higher. A study in athletes showed that dehydration and rehydration were interpreted by BIA as large changes in fat even though the main change was fluid. Manipulating water obscures decisions and may be harmful, particularly during heat, illness or use of medicines that affect fluid balance. [4, 5]

Using the bathroom before measurement is a common part of standardisation protocols and reduces differences in bladder contents between visits. Do so when needed without forcing it or delaying prescribed fluids or medicines. If you also have fasting instructions for a blood test or another procedure that day, ask the care team how to coordinate both; do not assume that InBody preparation overrides a more important medical instruction. [1, 7, 8]

Exercise, caffeine and alcohol can change fluid balance

Hard exercise before measurement can change sweating, skin temperature, blood flow and fluid distribution, all of which feed into the impedance the device measures. An Obesity Medicine Association clinical statement advises avoiding sweat-producing exercise for eight hours before BIA and avoiding large amounts of caffeine or alcohol for 12 hours. These are published general instructions; directions for the actual device and the person's medical situation come first. [1, 8]

If the appointment follows physical work or exercise and cannot be moved, do not try to correct the situation by dehydrating or overdrinking. Tell the team what activity occurred, when it ended and whether sweating was unusual. They may record the circumstances and interpret cautiously, or repeat the measurement on a more suitable day if the number would affect an important decision. Every departure from routine does not automatically require cancellation. [1, 4]

Caffeine and alcohol are not merely checklist details; they can be associated with drinking, sleep, urination and hydration. Aim for a repeatable routine and avoid unusual amounts rather than inventing severe rules. If abstaining or changing timing could conflict with treatment, or if alcohol dependence is a concern, do not stop abruptly for the test; discuss it safely and openly with a doctor. [1, 8]

At measurement: light clothing, good contact and consistent position

Wear light clothing similar to the previous visit, and remove shoes, socks, jewellery or metal items when the team requests it. The obesity-medicine statement includes removing metal in preparation, while methodological standards explain that electrode type and placement, body position and skin temperature can influence precision. Avoid cream or oil on hands or feet before a contact-electrode device if the provider's instructions say so. [1, 8]

Stand or lie as the operator directs and remain still during the short measurement. For devices requiring rest before a reading, repeat the rest period and limb position. Do not alter hand or foot position in an attempt to improve a segmental value; arm and leg distributions are estimates sensitive to the electrical path and contact. [1, 2]

Tell the team beforehand about a wound, cast, prosthesis, difficulty standing or difficulty holding the handles. If you have an implanted medical device, ask the team and your doctor whether measurement is suitable and follow manufacturer instructions and local rules; current standards note that pacemakers or other implanted cardiac devices may preclude use depending on local regulations. A general webpage should not decide the safety of a specific implant. [1]

Illness, medicines, menstrual timing and swelling need context

Fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, marked heat, limb swelling, heart, kidney or liver disease and some medicines can alter the amount or distribution of body fluid. In these circumstances, BIA assumptions are weaker and the output may reflect fluid more than a change in fat or muscle. ESPEN guidance advises caution with abnormal hydration and at the extremes of weight, while a study in adolescents with obesity found poorer tracking accuracy as obesity and the magnitude of change increased. [3, 9]

Do not stop a diuretic, blood-pressure medicine, diabetes medicine or any treatment to make the report look cleaner. Take medicines as prescribed and record the name, timing and recent changes. Whether to postpone or reinterpret a measurement depends on the individual situation, not one universal rule. If illness is temporary and testing is routine, repeating after recovery may sometimes be clearer; if bioimpedance is being used specifically to monitor fluid in a medical condition, the doctor defines the protocol. [1, 3]

Studies have not consistently found a meaningful menstrual-cycle effect on composition estimates; one contact-electrode study found no significant differences between cycle phases. Weight, symptoms and fluid retention can still vary for some people. Record cycle timing or bloating if it is a clear personal pattern, and compare under similar conditions where practical without treating menstrual timing as a proven explanation for every difference. [10]

After measurement: review the trend before changing the plan

If a new result is different, start with two questions: was the same device used, and were conditions similar? Then review weight, waist, strength, function, intake and symptoms according to the goal. InBody results explained: how to read your report covers the report fields and their limits so this preparation page does not become a complete interpretation guide. Do not change food, exercise or medicine because of one field without review by the treating clinician. [1, 2]

A small difference between visits may sit within method variation, and there is no single threshold that proves a real change for every device and every body. A reliability study of a multifrequency device found good reliability under organised conditions, but that does not remove day-to-day variation or turn an estimate into a direct measurement. Ask whether the change exceeds what is expected from the method and whether other indicators point in the same direction. [7]

The repeat interval should be based on the question and whether the answer would change a decision, not on seeking reassurance from a new number every few days. Stable scale weight also does not automatically mean the body is unchanged; body composition is not the scale explains how fat, muscle and fluid can move differently. A useful measurement supports a conversation and follow-up; it is not a certificate of success or failure. [1, 3]

When can the number wait, and when should symptoms not wait?

An unusual reading without symptoms usually begins with reviewing measurement conditions and discussing it with a clinician, not panic or an extreme change in eating. Continuing unintentional weight loss, new weakness, poor appetite or unusual fatigue need medical assessment for a cause; a body-composition report cannot diagnose that cause and should not delay care. The NHS notes many possible causes of unintentional loss, including digestive, hormonal and mental-health conditions, medicine effects and other illnesses. [12]

Sudden, severe or painful swelling, or swelling that is worsening or appearing in more than one area, needs faster review. With severe breathlessness, difficulty breathing, chest pain or heaviness, fainting or coughing blood, seek emergency care immediately through local emergency services. Do not wait for another InBody result to decide whether this is “water”; the report is not a substitute for urgent assessment. [11]

If your goal is to follow nutrition, weight or strength and you want a repeatable protocol that fits your treatment, you can start at the book an appointment page and bring earlier reports plus your conditions log. The clinic is mentioned only as a general next step; the treating clinician decides whether measurement is suitable, when it should be repeated and how it fits the wider picture. [1, 3]

Frequently asked questions

[1, 5, 6]

Keep a normal, safe drinking routine and follow the provider's instructions. The aim is similar conditions, not avoiding water all day or drinking a large amount at once. Dehydration and rehydration can appear as changes in fat or muscle, so tell the team if intake was clearly unusual. [4, 5]

One clinical statement advises avoiding sweat-producing exercise for eight hours before measurement. If exercise occurred, do not automatically discard the result; record its type and timing and let the clinician decide whether to repeat it. [1, 8]
