My fasting glucose is normal. Could I still have insulin resistance?

Published: 20 July 2026 10 min read Dr. Alyaa Said Abu Taleb

Medically reviewed by: Dr. Shimaa Fouad

The Arabic version of this article is the original; this English page is a translation of it.

The short answer: yes, but the result alone cannot diagnose it

Yes. Fasting glucose can be within the laboratory range while insulin resistance is present because the body may compensate for a weaker response in muscle, fat and the liver by releasing more insulin to keep glucose in a suitable range. While the pancreas can maintain that compensation, fasting glucose may look reassuring. This is a physiological possibility, however, not a diagnosis that follows automatically from a normal result, tiredness or a weight plateau [1, 6].

It helps to separate three ideas. Insulin resistance means that tissues respond less effectively to insulin; prediabetes and diabetes are clinical categories defined by glucose or HbA1c measurements under diagnostic criteria. They can overlap, but they are not different names for the same condition. A normal fasting result says that glucose at the moment of sampling was below the prediabetes and diabetes thresholds for that test. It does not say that insulin sensitivity is ideal, and it cannot guarantee that every other measure of glucose regulation is normal [1, 2, 3].

The reverse is also true: normal glucose does not itself prove insulin resistance. The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases explains that direct testing for insulin resistance is used mainly in research, while routine care focuses on risk factors, abnormal glucose regulation and associated health concerns [1]. The useful question is not “normal or insulin resistant?” as though they are opposites. It is why the test was requested, which risk factors are present, and whether another test would change a clinical decision.

Fasting glucose measures one moment, not the whole day

A fasting plasma glucose test measures glucose in a sample after an appropriate fast and is usually performed in the morning according to the clinician's and laboratory's instructions. It is an established, useful test for detecting prediabetes and diabetes, but it is a snapshot at a particular time. Food intake on preceding days, acute illness, physical stress, some medicines, fasting duration, sampling time and specimen handling can affect the reading. A clinician therefore interprets the number with its circumstances rather than in isolation [2, 3].

The body does not regulate glucose identically throughout the day. A person can have fasting glucose within range while HbA1c or the two-hour value after an oral glucose load shows an abnormality that fasting did not capture. Clinical sources explain that fasting glucose, HbA1c and the oral glucose tolerance test do not always identify the same people because they examine different aspects of glucose regulation. That is a reason not to close an assessment after one normal result when clinical suspicion is strong; it is not a reason for everyone to order every test [2, 3, 5].

The word “normal” must also be read beside the unit and reference interval, and with whether the result came from a laboratory plasma sample or a home meter. Home glucose meters can be useful for monitoring when a clinician recommends them, but they are not independent diagnostic tools for diabetes or prediabetes. A result close to a threshold may not carry the same context as one well below it in someone with the same risk factors. Do not compare your value with an online image or another person's result; bring the complete report, including date, unit and laboratory, for review [2, 3].

What makes a clinician look beyond the fasting number?

A clinician does not decide from weight or body shape alone. The picture includes age, family history of diabetes, changes in weight and waist pattern, physical activity, blood pressure and lipids, smoking, sleep, and medicines that may affect glucose regulation. A history of gestational diabetes, PCOS, sleep apnoea or fatty liver can increase the importance of assessment, but a risk factor is not a diagnosis, and the absence of excess weight does not exclude a problem [1, 7].

Common online symptoms such as early hunger, sweet cravings, sleepiness after meals, headache or difficulty losing weight are not specific to insulin resistance. They can occur with insufficient sleep, a large meal, anaemia, a thyroid disorder, medicine, stress or other explanations. Conversely, insulin resistance and prediabetes often cause no clear symptoms at all. Recording the timing of a concern, meals, sleep and medicines is therefore more useful than assigning a diagnosis from one sensation [1, 10].

When periods are irregular, androgen-related features are present or PCOS has been diagnosed, metabolic assessment has a particular context, and pregnancy plans affect testing and treatment choices. The PCOS and weight guide explains that territory without assuming everyone with PCOS has the same degree of insulin resistance. Darkened neck skin or skin tags may also deserve assessment, but a skin sign is not a laboratory test and cannot establish the cause by itself [1, 9].

Most importantly, testing needs a purpose. Are we looking for prediabetes, explaining disagreement between results, reviewing a medicine, or searching for another cause of fatigue? Defining the question prevents random test panels and connects every result to a clear next step.

When might HbA1c or a tolerance test add information?

When the aim is to detect prediabetes or diabetes, a clinician may choose fasting glucose, HbA1c or an oral glucose tolerance test according to risk factors, previous results and health circumstances. HbA1c estimates average glucose exposure over recent months and ordinarily does not require fasting. The tolerance test measures the body's response after a standard glucose drink and requires preparation and time. These are established tests of glucose regulation and diagnosis, not direct measurements of insulin sensitivity [2, 3, 4].

HbA1c has limitations of its own. Anaemia or altered red-cell lifespan, recent bleeding or transfusion, some haemoglobin variants, pregnancy, kidney disease and particular treatments can make the result less representative of usual average glucose. If HbA1c does not agree with glucose or the clinical picture, the clinician reviews the reason and may use another test rather than selecting the more reassuring result [2, 4]. Pregnancy also has different pathways, tests and timing, so non-pregnancy thresholds should not be used to interpret a pregnancy result [2, 3].

The tolerance test is not necessary for everyone, but it can identify abnormal glucose regulation that fasting glucose missed in some people. The decision depends on the clinical question, gestational-diabetes history, PCOS, symptoms and earlier results. If the initial test is normal but suspicion remains high, a clinician may select another test or repeat measurement at a suitable time instead of ordering everything on the same day [2, 5, 9].

For a fuller comparison, Is there one test that proves insulin resistance? explains the roles and limits of fasting glucose, HbA1c and the tolerance test. The central point is that an additional test is useful when it answers a defined question or changes follow-up, not simply because it creates more numbers.

What if fasting insulin is high while glucose is normal?

This pattern can be consistent with the pancreas releasing more insulin to keep glucose in range, but it does not prove insulin resistance by itself. The unit, laboratory reference interval, fasting duration, sample timing, glucose measured at the same time, medicines and health context all matter. “High” means the result falls outside the interval used by that laboratory; it does not automatically establish the cause or a fixed degree of resistance in every person [1, 6].

Insulin measurements are not harmonised across all laboratories to the same extent as some glucose tests, and research methods for measuring insulin sensitivity differ in complexity and precision. The research reference method requires controlled insulin and glucose infusion with repeated monitoring, so it is not a routine clinic test. Indices such as HOMA-IR use fasting glucose and insulin to estimate their relationship, but they inherit the insulin assay's limitations and vary with population and sampling conditions. No single worldwide number proves the diagnosis for everyone [1, 6].

Do not use an online calculator as though it were a medical report or repeat the test every week to chase a lower number. If a clinician requests insulin, ask which question the result will answer and whether a different value would change the decision. Insulin resistance: what it means and why weight can feel stuck provides the broader account of the mechanism and risk context, while the single-test guide examines laboratory indices in more detail [1, 6].

Do not start metformin, supplements or injections by yourself because insulin is raised while glucose is normal. Treatment depends on the actual diagnosis, risk of future abnormal glucose, associated conditions, pregnancy or its possibility, kidney function, potential benefits and harms, and the treating clinician's judgement [2, 8].

What can you do now without extra tests or restriction?

Begin by collecting simple information instead of self-diagnosing. Keep the complete fasting-glucose report and note why it was requested, fasting duration, medicines and supplements, and any acute illness at the time. For a few days, record meal timing, hunger or sleepiness, sleep and activity without trying to manufacture a perfect day. Bring previous HbA1c, lipid, blood-pressure or pregnancy-related results instead of repeating them at random. Organised information helps the clinician decide whether the result is sufficient or another step is useful [2, 3].

You do not need to wait for an “insulin resistance” label before beginning broadly health-supporting habits: regular meals containing suitable sources of fibre, vegetables, protein and carbohydrate in an amount that fits your needs; gradual activity you can sustain; enough sleep; and stopping smoking if relevant. There is no single food list for everybody, however. Pregnancy, kidney or liver disease, glucose-lowering medicines, eating disorders and injuries can alter the details, so individual planning belongs with the appropriate professional [1, 8, 12].

Do not remove all carbohydrate, undertake prolonged fasting or buy supplements that promise to lower insulin without review. The goal is not to “rest the pancreas” through a slogan; it is a sustainable pattern that improves food quality, movement, sleep and risk factors. If prediabetes is actually present, structured lifestyle intervention has stronger evidence than chasing nonspecific symptoms or one insulin value [7, 8, 12].

Agree with the clinician on a clear follow-up marker and suitable timing. It might be repeat glucose or HbA1c, blood pressure or lipids, a movement pattern, or a realistic change in eating and sleep. Successful follow-up is not the largest number of tests. It is an understood decision, an achievable action and a marker that shows whether the plan is helping.

When not to wait, and how to arrange a useful review

Insulin resistance itself is not usually an emergency, but clear symptoms of high glucose need faster action even when an older result was normal. New intense thirst, frequent urination, unintended weight loss, blurred vision or worsening fatigue warrants prompt medical advice and an appropriate glucose check. A normal result in the past should not be used to dismiss new symptoms because health can change and those symptoms can also have other causes requiring assessment [3, 10].

Seek emergency care immediately for repeated vomiting, abdominal pain, fast or deep breathing, fruity-smelling breath, severe dehydration, unusual drowsiness or confusion. These can be signs of diabetic ketoacidosis, a serious condition that may be the first presentation of diabetes. Do not wait for a nutrition appointment or a fasting insulin result. If symptoms are severe or the person cannot keep fluids down, use local emergency services [10, 11].

Outside an emergency, prepare for the visit with the complete report, medicine list, family history, any gestational-diabetes or PCOS history, weight changes, sleep, and the timing of symptoms. Ask whether the result is normal for the question being addressed, whether HbA1c or another test is useful, when testing should be repeated, and what finding would change the plan. The treating clinician chooses tests and treatment after reviewing the context and may direct you to another specialty when the cause is not nutritional [1, 2, 3].

If you need to start with an organised review at the medical centre, you can use the appointment request, mention that your question concerns normal fasting glucose and possible insulin resistance, and bring earlier results. Booking is not a promise of a particular diagnosis or medicine; it is a step towards reading each number in context and agreeing on a safe plan with the clinician [1, 2].
